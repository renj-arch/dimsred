Geometric Group Theory — the Cayley Graph, Hyperbolic Groups & the Growth of Groups

Geometric Group Theory studies finitely generated groups as geometric objects. The central idea: a group G with a generating set S gives a metric space — the Cayley graph Cay(G,S) — where vertices are group elements and edges connect g to g·s. The word metric d(g,h) equals the minimal length of a word representing g−1h. Different generating sets yield quasi-isometric Cayley graphs, so geometric properties (growth type, hyperbolicity) are group invariants. Gromov's theorem: groups of polynomial growth are virtually nilpotent. Hyperbolic groups (Gromov, 1987) are those whose Cayley graph is δ-hyperbolic — geodesic triangles are δ-thin. The Dehn function measures the isoperimetric complexity; hyperbolic groups have linear Dehn functions, giving linear-time solvable word problems. CAT(0) cube complexes (Haglund-Wise, Agol) led to the resolution of the virtual Haken conjecture. The boundary at infinity of a hyperbolic group encodes its dynamics at large scale.

Part 1

📏 Cayley Graphs and Growth of Groups

Growth
Cayley Graphs, the Word Metric, and the Growth Function
For a finitely generated group G with a finite symmetric generating set S (S = S−1, 1 ∉ S), the Cayley graph Cay(G,S) has vertex set G and an oriented edge from g to g·s labeled by s ∈ S. This is a regular graph of degree |S|. The word metric d(g,h) = min{ℓ : g−1h = s₁⋯s_ℓ, s_i ∈ S} makes G into a proper geodesic metric space. The growth function γ_{G,S}(n) = |{g ∈ G : d(1,g) ≤ n}| counts elements in the ball of radius n. Growth type is independent of S up to quasi-isometry: polynomial (∃ C,d such that γ(n) ≤ C n^d), exponential (γ(n) ≥ a^n for some a > 1), or intermediate (neither polynomial nor exponential). Gromov's theorem (1981): a finitely generated group has polynomial growth iff it is virtually nilpotent — contains a nilpotent subgroup of finite index. The Grigorchuk group (1984), the first example of intermediate growth, is an infinite 2-group generated by 4 automorphisms of the rooted binary tree. Bass-Guivarc'h formula: for a nilpotent group, γ(n) ∼ n^d where d = Σ k·rank(G_k/G_{k+1}) is the Hirsch length (the sum of ranks of the quotients in the lower central series). The growth of free groups is exponential: for F_r, γ(n) = 1 + 2r·((2r−1)^n − 1)/(2r−2).
The growth function measures how fast a group expands; its asymptotic type (polynomial, exponential, intermediate) is a quasi-isometry invariant with deep algebraic consequences.
Part 2

🌍 Hyperbolic Groups

Hyperbolic
δ-Hyperbolic Spaces, Gromov's Definition, and the Boundary at Infinity
A geodesic metric space X is δ-hyperbolic if every geodesic triangle Δ = [x,y] ∪ [y,z] ∪ [z,x] satisfies the δ-thin condition: each side is contained in the δ-neighborhood of the union of the other two sides. A finitely generated group G is hyperbolic if its Cayley graph (with any finite generating set) is δ-hyperbolic for some δ ≥ 0 — this is independent of the generating set up to quasi-isometry. Examples: free groups F_n (their Cayley graphs are trees, δ = 0), surface groups π₁(Σ_g) for g ≥ 2, fundamental groups of closed hyperbolic n-manifolds, co-compact lattices in Isom(ℍⁿ). Properties: hyperbolic groups are finitely presented, have a solvable word problem in linear time (the Dehn algorithm), and every element of infinite order has exponential growth. The Gromov boundary ∂G: the set of equivalence classes of geodesic rays (two rays are equivalent if they stay within bounded Hausdorff distance). For a non-elementary hyperbolic group, ∂G is a compact metrizable space. For F₂, ∂F₂ is the Cantor set. For surface groups, ∂π₁(Σ_g) is a circle S¹. For a hyperbolic 3-manifold group, ∂G ≅ S². The group acts on its boundary as a convergence group — a minimal, uniform convergence action. Quasi-isometries between hyperbolic groups induce homeomorphisms of boundaries. The Rips complex construction: for sufficiently large R, the Rips complex P_R(G) is contractible and G acts properly and cocompactly — giving a finite model for EG.
Hyperbolicity is a coarse negative-curvature condition: the Cayley graph looks like a negatively curved space at large scales, forcing strong algorithmic and structural properties.
Part 3

🧮 The Word Problem and the Dehn Function

Isoperimetry
The Word Problem, Van Kampen Diagrams, and the Dehn Function
The word problem for a finitely presented group G = ⟨S | R⟩: given a word w in the free group F(S), decide whether w = 1 in G. A van Kampen diagram is a planar, simply connected 2-complex whose boundary label is w and whose 2-cells are labeled by relators in R — it provides a geometric proof that w = 1. The Dehn function (or isoperimetric function) φ(n) = max_{w = 1, |w| ≤ n} min{Area(D) : D is a van Kampen diagram for w}. A group is hyperbolic iff its Dehn function is linear (φ(n) ≤ C·n). The Dehn algorithm solves the word problem for hyperbolic groups in linear time: scan the word for a subword that is more than half of a relator; if found, replace it by the shorter side; repeat; if no reduction exists, the word is geodesic and non-trivial. The isoperimetric spectrum: Dehn functions can be polynomial (e.g., n² for Baumslag-Solitar BS(1,2)), exponential, or even super-exponential. Automatic groups (Epstein, Cannon, Thurston) are groups with a regular language of normal forms such that the multiplication by a generator is a synchronous regular relation. Hyperbolic groups are automatic: the set of geodesic words forms an automatic structure. The asynchronous automatic groups and biautomatic groups generalize this. A group is combable if there is a language of quasi-geodesic paths satisfying the fellow-traveler property. The word problem is solvable for any automatic group in quadratic time. The conjugacy problem for hyperbolic groups is also solvable (in polynomial time via the geodesic core). The small cancellation theory (C′(1/6) condition) gives explicit examples of hyperbolic groups via presentations where overlaps between relators are limited.
The Dehn function captures the complexity of the word problem geometrically: hyperbolic groups have the simplest possible isoperimetric behavior — a linear Dehn function.
Part 4

🔥 CAT(0) Cube Complexes and the Haglund-Wise Theory

Cubulation
CAT(0) Spaces, Cube Complexes, and the Virtual Haken Conjecture
A CAT(0) metric space is a geodesic metric space where every geodesic triangle is at least as thin as the corresponding Euclidean comparison triangle. A CAT(0) cube complex is a cube complex (built from unit cubes [0,1]ⁿ glued along faces) with the CAT(0) metric — it is simply connected and non-positively curved. The Sageev's construction (1995): a group acting on a CAT(0) cube complex yields a wall space structure (a set of half-spaces with finite width). A group is cubulated if it acts properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex. The Haglund-Wise theorem (2007): hyperbolic groups that are cubulated are virtually special — they contain a finite-index subgroup that embeds into a right-angled Artin group (RAAG). Virtually special groups are residually finite, have the Haagerup property (a-T-menability), and satisfy the Novikov conjecture. The Agol's theorem (2012, the virtual Haken conjecture): every closed hyperbolic 3-manifold has a finite cover that is Haken — equivalently, its fundamental group is cubulated. This used the Wise's theorem (2009): a hyperbolic group with a quasiconvex hierarchy is cubulated. A quasiconvex hierarchy is a way to build a group by repeatedly splitting over quasiconvex subgroups (analogous to Haken hierarchies in 3-manifold theory). Applications: the virtual fibering conjecture (proved by Agol): every closed hyperbolic 3-manifold has a finite cover that fibers over S¹. The residual finiteness of hyperbolic groups: cubulated hyperbolic groups are residually finite. The Novikov conjecture (the homotopy invariance of higher signatures) holds for groups acting properly and cocompactly on CAT(0) cube complexes. The Bartels-Lück theorem: the Farrell-Jones conjecture holds for CAT(0) groups and hyperbolic groups. The Stallings' theorem on ends of groups: a finitely generated group has more than one end iff it splits over a finite subgroup (an amalgamated product or HNN extension over a finite subgroup). The Dunwoody's accessibility theorem: finitely presented groups can be split only finitely many times over finite subgroups.
The cubulation program — showing hyperbolic groups act on CAT(0) cube complexes — revolutionized 3-manifold topology and proved the virtual Haken conjecture, establishing residual finiteness and the Novikov conjecture for large classes of groups.

Quasi-isometric Rigidity and Classification of Lattices

Quasi-isometric rigidity asks whether the large‑scale geometry of a group determines its algebraic structure up to finite extensions. For lattices in semisimple Lie groups, the answer is largely affirmative. Mostow’s strong rigidity theorem (1968) shows that any isomorphism between lattices in higher‑rank simple Lie groups extends to an isomorphism of the ambient groups. In the quasi‑isometric setting, Kleiner and Leeb (1997) proved that any group quasi‑isometric to a higher‑rank symmetric space is virtually a lattice in the same Lie group. Eskin and Farb (1997) extended this to rank‑one spaces of non‑compact type, showing that quasi‑isometries of complex hyperbolic and quaternionic hyperbolic spaces are at bounded distance from isometries. The Euclidean case is different: groups quasi‑isometric to ℝⁿ are virtually ℤⁿ by Gromov’s polynomial growth theorem. For products of trees and hyperbolic planes, the quasi‑isometry classification is more subtle; work of Whyte and others shows that certain right‑angled Artin groups are quasi‑isometrically rigid while others admit non‑trivial quasi‑isometries. The general program seeks a dictionary linking quasi‑isometry invariants — such as asymptotic dimension, Dehn functions, and boundaries — to algebraic properties like virtual nilpotence, virtual specialness, or the existence of a non‑trivial splitting.

A key tool is the notion of a quasi‑action: a group acts quasi‑isometrically on a metric space if each element moves points a uniformly bounded distance from an isometric action. The Schwartz–Moser lemma guarantees that a quasi‑action on a proper geodesic space is quasi‑conjugate to an isometric action on a possibly different space. This reduces many rigidity problems to the study of isometric actions on CAT(0) spaces or buildings. For example, a group quasi‑isometric to a product of trees must act properly and cocompactly on a product of trees, which forces a virtual splitting as a direct product of groups acting on trees. The classification of lattices in products of rank‑one groups (Burger–Mozes, 2000) shows that irreducible lattices are quasi‑isometrically rigid, while reducible lattices decompose as products. These results illustrate how coarse geometry can recover fine algebraic information, a theme that continues to drive research in geometric group theory.

Applications extend beyond pure group theory. Quasi‑isometric rigidity of mapping class groups (Behrstock–Kleiner–Minsky–Mosher, 2012) implies that any group quasi‑isometric to a mapping class group is virtually a mapping class group. Similar statements hold for Out(Fₙ) (Bestvina–Feighn–Handel) and for certain graph‑of‑groups constructions. The techniques involve the analysis of hierarchical hyperbolic spaces, projection complexes, and the geometry of curve graphs. Understanding these invariants equips the aspirant to recognise when a problem about an unknown group can be reduced to a known rigid class, a frequent pattern in advanced examinations.

Boundaries of Hyperbolic Groups and Conformal Dimension

The Gromov boundary ∂G of a hyperbolic group G captures its asymptotic geometry. It is a compact metrizable space defined up to homeomorphism by the quasi‑isometry class of G. For a hyperbolic group, the boundary carries a family of visual metrics: for a basepoint o and a parameter ε > 0 small enough, the metric d_ε(ξ,η) ≈ exp(−ε·(ξ|η)_o) where (ξ|η)_o is the Gromov product. Different ε give bi‑Lipschitz equivalent metrics, so the boundary has a well‑defined quasi‑symmetric structure. The conformal dimension of ∂G is the infimum of Hausdorff dimensions over all metrics quasi‑symmetric to a visual metric. This invariant, introduced by Pansu and studied by Bourdon and Pajot, distinguishes many hyperbolic groups. For example, the boundary of a uniform lattice in real hyperbolic space Hⁿ is a sphere Sⁿ⁻¹ with conformal dimension n−1. In contrast, the boundary of a free group is a Cantor set of conformal dimension 0. Bourdon and Pajot (1999) showed that for a hyperbolic group whose boundary is a Sierpiński carpet, the conformal dimension is strictly greater than 1 but less than 2, and it is attained by a unique (up to quasi‑symmetry) metric. This rigidity of the conformal gauge has strong algebraic consequences: if a hyperbolic group has boundary of conformal dimension 1, then the group is virtually a free group or a surface group (by a theorem of Kapovich and Kleiner).

Computing conformal dimension often involves constructing explicit quasi‑symmetric maps or using the combinatorial Loewner property. A metric space satisfies the combinatorial Loewner property if the modulus of curve families is controlled by discrete approximations. Bourdon and Pajot proved that the boundary of a hyperbolic group with a planar Cayley graph (e.g., a hyperbolic 3‑manifold group) satisfies this property, which forces the conformal dimension to be realized by a visual metric. For groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes, the boundary is a median space; recent work of Hagen and others relates the conformal dimension to the rank of the cube complex. In examinations, a typical question asks to explain why the conformal dimension is a quasi‑isometry invariant and how it can be used to rule out certain groups being quasi‑isometric to each other. The answer hinges on the fact that a quasi‑isometry induces a quasi‑symmetric homeomorphism of boundaries, and quasi‑symmetric maps preserve the conformal gauge.

Technique
Explain why the Gromov boundary of a hyperbolic group is a quasi‑isometry invariant and how the conformal dimension of the boundary can be used to distinguish two hyperbolic groups that are not quasi‑isometric.
A quasi‑isometry f: G → H between hyperbolic groups extends continuously to a homeomorphism ∂f: ∂G → ∂H because quasi‑geodesic rays in G map to quasi‑geodesic rays in H, and the fellow‑traveller property guarantees a well‑defined limit point. Moreover, ∂f is quasi‑symmetric: there exists a homeomorphism η: [0,∞) → [0,∞) such that for any distinct ξ,η,ζ ∈ ∂G, the ratio d(∂f(ξ),∂f(η)) / d(∂f(ξ),∂f(ζ)) is bounded by η of the corresponding ratio in ∂G. Since conformal dimension is defined as the infimum of Hausdorff dimensions over all metrics in the quasi‑symmetric gauge, it is invariant under quasi‑symmetric homeomorphisms. Therefore, if two hyperbolic groups have boundaries with different conformal dimensions, they cannot be quasi‑isometric. For instance, a free group has boundary a Cantor set of conformal dimension 0, while a uniform lattice in H³ has boundary S² of conformal dimension 2; these groups are not quasi‑isometric.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question asks for a quasi‑isometry invariant, first list the standard ones: growth type, Dehn function, asymptotic dimension, boundary homeomorphism type, and conformal dimension. Then match the invariant to the specific groups mentioned. If the groups are given by presentations, check whether they are hyperbolic (linear Dehn function) and compute or recall their boundaries. A quick mental table — free groups → Cantor set (dim 0), surface groups → circle (dim 1), hyperbolic 3‑manifold groups → Sierpiński carpet or S² (dim >1) — often yields the answer without heavy computation.

Random Groups and Generic Properties

Gromov’s density model provides a probabilistic framework for studying “typical” finitely presented groups. Fix a free group Fₘ of rank m ≥ 2 and a length ℓ. Choose each reduced word of length ℓ independently with probability p = (2m−1)−dℓ where d ∈ (0,1) is the density parameter. The resulting random presentation ⟨S | R⟩ defines a random group G(d). Gromov showed that for d < 1/2 the group is infinite, hyperbolic, and torsion‑free with overwhelming probability as ℓ → ∞, while for d > 1/2 the group is trivial (or finite) with overwhelming probability. The threshold d = 1/2 is sharp: at density exactly 1/2 the behaviour is more delicate and depends on the number of generators. Ollivier (2005) refined the model and proved that at densities d < 1/3 the random group satisfies a linear isoperimetric inequality with an explicit constant, and that the first ℓ²‑Betti numbers vanish. Žuk (2003) established property (T) for random groups at densities d > 1/3, showing that property (T) appears generically in this range. These results demonstrate a phase transition: low density yields free‑like hyperbolic groups, intermediate density yields hyperbolic groups with property (T), and high density yields trivial groups.

The generic properties of random groups have become a benchmark for conjectures. For instance, the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients is known for all hyperbolic groups (Lafforgue, 2002), and random groups provide a plentiful supply of test cases. The fact that random groups at density d < 1/2 are hyperbolic and have trivial first ℓ²‑Betti numbers supports the Singer conjecture for hyperbolic groups. Moreover, the existence of random groups with property (T) but without finite subgroups (for d ∈ (1/3, 1/2)) gives counterexamples to the conjecture that every group with property (T) has a finite subgroup. In examinations, a typical problem asks to state the density thresholds for hyperbolicity, property (T), and triviality, and to explain why the model is useful for producing groups with prescribed combinations of properties. The answer should reference the independence of relators at large length, the use of small cancellation theory at low density, and the spectral gap arguments of Žuk at higher density.

Beyond the density model, other random group models include the Gromov–Misha model (fixed number of relators) and the triangular model (relators chosen from a triangular region). These variants exhibit similar phase transitions but with different critical densities. The study of random groups also connects to the theory of expanders: the Cayley graphs of random groups at density d > 1/3 form a family of expanders with high probability, as shown by Żuk and later by Alon and Roichman. This link provides a geometric source of expander graphs, which are central in theoretical computer science and the study of property (τ). Understanding these connections equips the aspirant to discuss how probabilistic methods produce explicit families of groups with desired geometric and analytic features.

Higher Dehn Functions and Filling Invariants

The classical Dehn function measures the difficulty of filling loops in the Cayley complex. Higher Dehn functions, introduced by Gromov and developed by Alonso, Wang, Pride, and others, measure the difficulty of filling spheres of higher dimension. For a finitely presented group G = ⟨S | R⟩, the k‑th Dehn function δ_k(n) is the maximal minimal volume of a (k+1)‑chain filling a k‑cycle of volume ≤ n in the universal cover of the presentation complex. When k = 1 this recovers the usual Dehn function. For k = 2, δ2(n) captures the complexity of filling 2‑spheres, which is related to the second homotopy group of the presentation complex. A group is of type F_k if it admits a classifying space with finite k‑skeleton; the finiteness of δ_k(n) for all n is equivalent to the group being of type Fk+1 and having a polynomial (or linear) filling function. For example, hyperbolic groups have linear Dehn functions in all dimensions (Gromov, 1987). Automatic groups have quadratic Dehn functions in dimension 1, but their higher Dehn functions can be exponential. The Baumslag–Solitar group BS(1,2) has exponential Dehn function in dimension 1 and its higher Dehn functions grow even faster.

Filling invariants are closely tied to the isoperimetric profile of the group. The isoperimetric function of degree k, IP_k(n), is the supremum of minimal (k+1)‑volumes needed to fill k‑cycles of volume ≤ n in a contractible complex on which the group acts properly and cocompactly. For CAT(0) groups, the Euclidean isoperimetric inequality holds: IP_k(n) ≤ C n(k+1)/k. This is a consequence of the fact that CAT(0) spaces admit cone constructions with controlled volume. For groups acting on buildings, the isoperimetric profile can be computed using the combinatorics of apartments. The work of Young (2013) on the Dehn functions of SL_n(ℤ) shows that the k‑th Dehn function grows like n(k+1)/k for k < n−1, reflecting the rank of the symmetric space. These results illustrate how higher filling functions encode the large‑scale geometry of the group and its associated symmetric spaces or buildings.

In examinations, a common question asks to compare the Dehn function and the second Dehn function of a given group, or to explain why a linear isoperimetric inequality in all dimensions characterizes hyperbolic groups. The key points are: (1) hyperbolicity implies a linear isoperimetric inequality for all k because the Cayley graph is δ‑thin and the cone construction yields linear filling; (2) conversely, if a group has linear Dehn functions in all dimensions, its asymptotic cones are simply connected and have trivial higher homotopy, forcing the group to be hyperbolic (by a theorem of Gromov). The aspirant should be able to sketch the cone argument: a linear filling function gives a uniform bound on the volume of fillings, which passes to the ultralimit and yields contractibility of asymptotic cones, a hallmark of hyperbolicity.

Coarse Embeddability, Property A, and the Novikov Conjecture

A metric space X coarsely embeds into a Hilbert space if there exist non‑decreasing functions ρ₁, ρ₂: [0,∞) → [0,∞) with ρ₁(r) → ∞ such that ρ₁(d(x,y)) ≤ ‖f(x)−f(y)‖ ≤ ρ₂(d(x,y)) for some map f: X → ℋ. For finitely generated groups equipped with a word metric, coarse embeddability into Hilbert space is a quasi‑isometry invariant. Yu (2000) proved that if a discrete group coarsely embeds into Hilbert space, then the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture holds for that group, which in turn implies the Novikov conjecture on the homotopy invariance of higher signatures. This result provides a geometric sufficient condition for the Novikov conjecture that applies to a wide class of groups, including all hyperbolic groups (which embed via the Gromov boundary), all amenable groups (by a construction of Higson and Kasparov), and all groups acting properly on CAT(0) cube complexes (by a theorem of Guentner, Higson, and Weinberger).

Property A, introduced by Yu (2000), is a weaker, combinatorial condition that implies coarse embeddability into Hilbert space. A discrete metric space X has Property A if for every ε > 0 and R > 0 there exists a map ξ: X → ℓ¹(X) with ‖ξ(x)‖₁ = 1, support of ξ(x) contained in B(x, S) for some S, and ‖ξ(x)−ξ(y)‖₁ < ε whenever d(x,y) ≤ R. For groups, Property A is equivalent to the existence of a sequence of almost invariant means (Reiter’s condition) and to the exactness of the reduced C*‑algebra. All amenable groups have Property A, and so do all hyperbolic groups (by a construction using the Gromov boundary). However, there exist groups that coarsely embed into Hilbert space but do not have Property A (e.g., certain expanders with property (T) constructed by Gromov). The distinction is important: coarse embeddability is sufficient for the Novikov conjecture, while Property A is sufficient for the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients.

Recent work has extended these ideas to the Farrell–Jones conjecture. Bartels and Lück (2012) proved the Farrell–Jones conjecture for groups acting properly on CAT(0) spaces and for hyperbolic groups, using controlled algebra and the existence of finite asymptotic dimension. Asymptotic dimension, introduced by Gromov, is another coarse invariant: a metric space has asymptotic dimension ≤ n if for every R > 0 there is a uniformly bounded cover with multiplicity ≤ n+1 and Lebesgue number ≥ R. Groups with finite asymptotic dimension coarsely embed into Hilbert space (by a theorem of Guentner, Higson, and Weinberger), and thus satisfy the Novikov conjecture. In examinations, a typical question asks to state Yu’s theorem and to give examples of groups that satisfy its hypothesis. The answer should list hyperbolic groups, amenable groups, groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes, and groups with finite asymptotic dimension, and briefly explain why each class coarsely embeds (e.g., hyperbolic groups via the boundary embedding, amenable groups via the Folner sequence construction).

  • Confusing quasi‑isometric rigidity with commensurability: rigidity concerns large‑scale geometry, while commensurability is an algebraic finite‑index relation.
  • Assuming all hyperbolic groups have conformal dimension 1: only those with circle or Sierpiński carpet boundaries have dimension 1; free groups have dimension 0, and many 3‑manifold groups have dimension >1.
  • Misstating the density thresholds for random groups: hyperbolicity holds for d < 1/2, property (T) for d > 1/3, triviality for d > 1/2.
  • Equating the Dehn function with the second Dehn function: they measure filling of loops versus spheres and can have drastically different growth rates.
  • Believing Property A is equivalent to coarse embeddability: Property A implies coarse embeddability, but the converse fails (expanders with property (T) provide counterexamples).
  • Overlooking that the Novikov conjecture follows from coarse embeddability, not directly from hyperbolicity: hyperbolicity gives embeddability, which then yields the conjecture via Yu’s theorem.
  • Neglecting the role of asymptotic dimension: finite asymptotic dimension implies coarse embeddability, but many groups (e.g., certain hyperbolic groups) have infinite asymptotic dimension yet still embed.

Automatic Groups and the Fellow Traveler Property

An automatic group is a finitely generated group equipped with a finite state automaton that recognizes a set of normal forms for its elements and verifies multiplication by generators. Formally, let G be a group with finite symmetric generating set S. A regular language L ⊆ S* mapping onto G via the evaluation map is a set of normal forms if the map is bijective. The group is automatic if there exists such an L and, for each s ∈ S ∪ {1}, a finite state automaton accepting the language of pairs (w, w') ∈ L × L such that w = w's in G, where the pairs are read synchronously with a padding symbol. This synchronous fellow traveler property means that the paths in the Cayley graph labeled by w and w' stay within a bounded distance of each other at all times. The constant bounding this distance is the fellow traveler constant. Cannon proved that all hyperbolic groups are automatic, using the language of geodesic words (or shortlex geodesics) and the thin triangles property to bound fellow travel. However, not all automatic groups are hyperbolic: Z² is automatic but not hyperbolic, and mapping class groups of surfaces are automatic (Mosher) but contain Z² subgroups. The class of automatic groups is closed under free products, direct products with finite groups, and extensions with finite kernel, but not under arbitrary direct products (Z² × Z² is not automatic) or free products with amalgamation over infinite subgroups. A stronger notion is biautomaticity, where multiplication on both left and right by generators is recognized by synchronous automata. Hyperbolic groups are biautomatic, as are mapping class groups and fundamental groups of compact non-positively curved manifolds. Biautomatic groups have quadratic Dehn function and solvable word problem in quadratic time. The class of automatic groups is strictly larger than biautomatic groups; the Baumslag-Solitar group BS(1,2) = ⟨a, t | tat⁻¹ = a²⟩ is automatic but not biautomatic. A fundamental open question is whether all CAT(0) groups are automatic. The fellow traveler property has a geometric reformulation: a regular language L of normal forms is a synchronous automatic structure if and only if the set of paths in the Cayley graph labeled by words in L forms a regular set of quasi-geodesics that fellow-travel each other. This connects to the theory of regular languages of geodesics and the growth series of the group, which is rational for automatic groups (Cannon). The growth series rationality implies that the growth function is either polynomial or exponential with a rational generating function, a strong constraint on the group's large-scale geometry.

Ends of Groups and Stallings' Theorem on Splittings

The number of ends e(G) of a finitely generated group G is a quasi-isometry invariant that measures the number of "directions to infinity" in its Cayley graph. Formally, for a locally finite connected graph Γ, the number of ends is the supremum over all finite subgraphs K of the number of infinite components of Γ K. For a finitely generated group, this is independent of the generating set. Freudenthal and Hopf showed that e(G) ∈ {0, 1, 2, ∞}. A group has 0 ends iff it is finite. Stallings' theorem (1968) characterizes groups with more than one end: a finitely generated group has e(G) > 1 if and only if it splits non-trivially as a free product with amalgamation A *_C B or an HNN extension A *_C over a finite subgroup C. In particular, e(G) = 2 if and only if G is virtually Z (i.e., contains Z as a finite index subgroup). Groups with infinitely many ends are precisely those that split over a finite subgroup in a non-trivial way that is not virtually Z. The proof uses the action of G on the set of ends, constructing a tree (the Bass-Serre tree) on which G acts with finite edge stabilizers and more than one orbit of edges. The quotient graph of groups gives the splitting. This theorem is the foundation of Bass-Serre theory, which establishes a bijection between actions of G on trees without edge inversions and graphs of groups with fundamental group G. The number of ends also relates to cohomological dimension: if e(G) = ∞, then the first ℓ²-Betti number β₁⁽²⁾(G) > 0 (Cheeger-Gromov), and G has infinitely many ends iff H¹(G, ℤG) ≠ 0 (Specker). For finitely presented groups, the ends can be computed from the presentation complex: e(G) > 1 iff the universal cover has more than one end, which is detectable from the second homology of the presentation complex with coefficients in the group ring. Stallings' theorem has a relative version: a group G has more than one end relative to a subgroup H if the coset graph has more than one end, leading to splittings relative to H. This underlies the JSJ decomposition theory for finitely presented groups, where one iteratively splits over finite, cyclic, and abelian subgroups to obtain a canonical decomposition. The number of ends is also connected to the growth of the group: groups of polynomial growth have 0 or 2 ends (finite or virtually Z), while groups of exponential growth can have 1 or infinitely many ends. A group with one end cannot split over a finite subgroup; such groups are often called "one-ended" and include all infinite hyperbolic groups that are not virtually free, all higher rank lattices, and all mapping class groups of surfaces of genus ≥ 2.

Worked Problem: Ends and Splittings
Let G = ⟨a, b, t | tat⁻¹ = a², tbt⁻¹ = b³⟩. Determine the number of ends of G and describe its splitting over a finite subgroup, if any.
The group G is an HNN extension of the free group F₂ = ⟨a, b⟩ with stable letter t conjugating a to a² and b to b³. The base group F₂ is infinite and has infinitely many ends (since free groups of rank ≥ 2 have infinitely many ends). However, the HNN extension is over the whole base group, not a proper subgroup. To find ends, consider the Bass-Serre tree T of this HNN extension. Vertices are cosets of F₂, edges are cosets of the associated subgroups (both equal to F₂). Since the associated subgroups equal the base group, the tree T is a line (each vertex has degree 2). The action of G on T has quotient a loop with one vertex and one edge. The vertex stabilizer is F₂, edge stabilizer is F₂. The group G acts on a line with infinite vertex stabilizers. The number of ends of G equals the number of ends of the quotient graph of groups, which is 1 (a loop has one end). Alternatively, G contains F₂ as a subgroup of infinite index, and the action on the line shows G is not virtually Z. Since G is infinite and not virtually Z, e(G) ≠ 0,2. The action on a line with infinite vertex stabilizers implies e(G) = 1 (if vertex stabilizers have one end) or ∞ (if vertex stabilizers have infinitely many ends). F₂ has infinitely many ends, but the HNN extension "fills in" the ends. In fact, G is isomorphic to the Baumslag-Solitar group BS(1,2) × BS(1,3) amalgamated over Z? No — the relations are independent. Actually G ≅ (Z[1/2] ⋊ Z) × (Z[1/3] ⋊ Z) with the Z factors identified? Let's compute: the subgroup ⟨a, t⟩ is BS(1,2) which has one end (it is solvable, not virtually Z). Similarly ⟨b, t⟩ is BS(1,3) with one end. The group G is the free product of BS(1,2) and BS(1,3) amalgamated over the common subgroup ⟨t⟩ ≅ Z. Both factors have one end, amalgamated over Z (two ends). By Stallings' theorem on amalgamated products: if A and B have one end and C has two ends, then A *_C B has one end. Therefore e(G) = 1. G splits over Z (infinite), not over a finite subgroup. So G has one end and does not split over a finite subgroup.
Exam Strategy: Ends and Splittings
When asked to compute ends of a group given by a presentation, first identify if it splits as a free product with amalgamation or HNN extension over a finite subgroup. If yes, it has infinitely many ends (unless virtually Z). If it splits over an infinite subgroup, analyze the vertex groups: one-ended vertex groups amalgamated over two-ended subgroups yield one-ended groups. Remember: virtually Z ⇔ two ends; finite ⇔ zero ends; all other finitely generated groups have one or infinitely many ends. For hyperbolic groups: virtually free ⇔ infinitely many ends; otherwise one end. For CAT(0) groups: virtually Z or finite ⇔ 0 or 2 ends; otherwise one end (no infinite-ended CAT(0) groups).

Relatively Hyperbolic Groups and the Bowditch Boundary

Relative hyperbolicity generalizes Gromov's hyperbolicity by allowing a group to be "hyperbolic relative to a collection of peripheral subgroups." Introduced by Gromov and developed by Farb, Bowditch, Osin, and others, a finitely generated group G is hyperbolic relative to a finite collection of subgroups {H₁, ..., Hₙ} if the coned-off Cayley graph (obtained by adding a vertex for each left coset gHᵢ and connecting it to all elements of the coset) is a hyperbolic graph on which G acts properly and cocompactly, with the additional condition of "bounded coset penetration" (BCP) for geodesics in the original Cayley graph. Equivalently, G acts geometrically finitely on a proper hyperbolic space with the Hᵢ as maximal parabolic subgroups. The Bowditch boundary ∂(G, {Hᵢ}) is the Gromov boundary of the coned-off graph, a compact metrizable space on which G acts as a convergence group. The peripheral subgroups are precisely the stabilizers of the parabolic points in the boundary. Key examples: (1) Fundamental groups of finite-volume hyperbolic manifolds with cusps are hyperbolic relative to their cusp subgroups (virtually Zⁿ). (2) Free products G = A * B are hyperbolic relative to {A, B}. (3) Mapping class groups of punctured surfaces are hyperbolic relative to subgroups fixing the punctures (by Farb). (4) Groups acting geometrically on CAT(0) spaces with isolated flats are hyperbolic relative to the flat stabilizers (Hruska-Kleiner). The Dehn function of a relatively hyperbolic group is at most quadratic if the peripheral subgroups have at most quadratic Dehn function (Osin). The combination theorem of Dahmani-Guirardel-Osin allows building new relatively hyperbolic groups from old ones via amalgamated products and HNN extensions over subgroups that are either finite or parabolic. The Bowditch boundary is a topological invariant of the pair (G, {Hᵢ}); if two relatively hyperbolic groups are quasi-isometric, their boundaries are homeomorphic (under mild conditions). The boundary can be a Sierpiński carpet, a Menger curve, or a Cantor set, depending on the peripheral structure. A group is hyperbolic in the usual sense iff it is hyperbolic relative to the trivial subgroup. Relative hyperbolicity is preserved under taking finite index subgroups and quotients by finite normal subgroups. The class of relatively hyperbolic groups includes all hyperbolic groups, all free products, and many fundamental groups of geometric manifolds. An important algorithmic result: the word problem is solvable in relatively hyperbolic groups if it is solvable in the peripheral subgroups (Osin). The conjugacy problem is also solvable under similar conditions. The asymptotic cones of relatively hyperbolic groups are tree-graded spaces with pieces the asymptotic cones of the peripheral subgroups (Druţu-Sapir). This provides a quasi-isometry invariant: the collection of asymptotic cones of peripheral subgroups up to permutation and bi-Lipschitz equivalence.

Kazhdan's Property (T), Haagerup Property, and Fixed Point Theorems

Kazhdan's Property (T) is a rigidity property for locally compact groups, defined in terms of unitary representations. A group G has Property (T) if every unitary representation of G that has almost invariant vectors has a non-zero invariant vector. Equivalently, the trivial representation is isolated in the unitary dual Ĝ with the Fell topology. For discrete groups, Property (T) is equivalent to the existence of a Kazhdan pair (Q, ε) where Q ⊂ G is finite and ε > 0 such that any unitary representation with a (Q, ε)-invariant vector has an invariant vector. Property (T) implies finite abelianization, finite generation, and that every quotient has Property (T). It is preserved under extensions with compact kernel. Key examples: SL(n, Z) for n ≥ 3 has Property (T) (Kazhdan); Sp(2n, Z) for n ≥ 2 has Property (T); lattices in higher rank simple Lie groups have Property (T) (Margulis). Free groups, surface groups, and amenable groups do not have Property (T). Property (T) has strong geometric consequences: groups with Property (T) have fixed point properties for actions on Hilbert spaces (every affine isometric action on a Hilbert space has a fixed point), on CAT(0) spaces (every action on a complete CAT(0) space has a fixed point or stabilizes a flat), and on median spaces. They cannot act properly on CAT(0) cube complexes (Niblo-Reeves). Property (T) groups have positive first ℓ²-Betti number vanishing (β₁⁽²⁾ = 0) and satisfy a spectral gap for the Laplacian on the Cayley graph. The Haagerup Property (or a-T-menability) is the "opposite" of Property (T): a group has the Haagerup Property if it admits a proper affine isometric action on a Hilbert space. Equivalently, it coarsely embeds into Hilbert space. Amenable groups, free groups, hyperbolic groups, and groups acting properly on CAT(0) cube complexes have the Haagerup Property. Property (T) and Haagerup Property are mutually exclusive for infinite groups. The Baum-Connes conjecture with coefficients holds for groups with the Haagerup Property (Higson-Kasparov) and for groups with Property (T) (Lafforgue, using strong Property (T)). Relative Property (T) for a pair (G, H) means that every unitary representation of G with almost invariant vectors has H-invariant vectors. This is used to prove Property (T) for groups like SL(3, Z) by showing (SL(3, Z), Z² ⋊ SL(2, Z)) has relative Property (T). The fixed point theorem for CAT(0) spaces: if G has Property (T) and acts by isometries on a complete CAT(0) space X, then either G fixes a point in X or G stabilizes a flat subspace isometric to Rⁿ. For CAT(0) cube complexes, Property (T) implies the action has a global fixed point (Niblo-Reeves). This is a key tool in proving that certain groups cannot act on cube complexes, e.g., lattices in Sp(n,1) for n ≥ 2.

The Baum-Connes Conjecture, Assembly Maps, and K-Theory

The Baum-Connes conjecture is a central conjecture in noncommutative geometry and operator K-theory, predicting an isomorphism between the topological K-theory of the classifying space for proper actions of a group G and the K-theory of its reduced C*-algebra C*ᵣ(G). For a discrete group G, let EG be a universal space for proper G-actions (a G-CW complex with contractible fixed point sets for finite subgroups). The conjecture states that the assembly map μ: Kᵢᴳ(EG) → Kᵢ(C*ᵣ(G)) is an isomorphism for i = 0, 1. Here Kᵢᴳ(EG) is the equivariant K-homology of EG, computable via the Baum-Connes assembly map from the topological K-theory of the quotient spaces. The conjecture implies several important consequences: (1) The Novikov conjecture on homotopy invariance of higher signatures for closed manifolds with fundamental group G. (2) The Kadison-Kaplansky conjecture on idempotents in C*ᵣ(G) (no non-trivial idempotents for torsion-free G). (3) The stable Gromov-Lawson-Rosenberg conjecture on positive scalar curvature. The conjecture is known for: (a) Amenable groups (Higson-Kasparov, using the Haagerup Property). (b) Hyperbolic groups (Lafforgue, using strong Property (T) and the Banach algebra version). (c) Groups acting properly on CAT(0) cube complexes (Guentner-Higson-Weinberger, via coarse embeddability). (d) Mapping class groups (Kasparov, using the curve complex). (e) Linear groups over local fields (Lafforgue). (f) Fundamental groups of 3-manifolds (Bartels-Lück-Reich, using the geometrization theorem). The conjecture with coefficients (for any C*-algebra with G-action) is stronger and implies the original. Counterexamples to the Baum-Connes conjecture with coefficients exist (Higson-Lafforgue-Skandalis), using expanders and Property (T) groups. The assembly map can be analyzed via the Dirac-dual Dirac method: one constructs a Dirac element D ∈ KKᴳ(C₀(EG), ℂ) and a dual Dirac element β ∈ KKᴳ(ℂ, C₀(EG)) such that β ⊗ D = 1 in KKᴳ(ℂ, ℂ). The γ-element γ = D ⊗ β ∈ KKᴳ(C₀(EG), C₀(EG)) is an idempotent; the conjecture is equivalent to γ = 1. For groups with the Haagerup Property, γ = 1 is constructed using the proper affine isometric action on Hilbert space. For hyperbolic groups, Lafforgue uses the boundary action and strong Property (T) for the Banach algebra Lᵖ. The Baum-Connes conjecture is a quasi-isometry invariant for groups that are coarsely embeddable into Hilbert space. The K-theory of C*ᵣ(G) for hyperbolic groups is computable via the boundary: K₀(C*ᵣ(G)) ⊕ K₁(C*ᵣ(G)) ≅ K⁰(∂G) ⊕ K¹(∂G) (by the work of Emerson, Meyer, and others). This connects the analytic K-theory to the topology of the Gromov boundary.

  • Confusing relative hyperbolicity with hyperbolicity: A relatively hyperbolic group is not necessarily hyperbolic; it is hyperbolic iff all peripheral subgroups are finite. The coned-off graph is hyperbolic, but the original Cayley graph is not.
  • Assuming all groups with Property (T) are lattices in higher rank Lie groups: There are many Property (T) groups that are not linear, e.g., certain random groups at density > 1/3 (Żuk), and groups acting on buildings. Property (T) does not imply linearity.
  • Equating Haagerup Property with amenability: All amenable groups have the Haagerup Property, but free groups, hyperbolic groups, and mapping class groups have Haagerup without being amenable. Haagerup is strictly weaker.
  • Believing the Baum-Connes conjecture is known for all groups: It is open for many groups, including SL(3, Z) (though known with coefficients by Lafforgue), and for groups with expanders. The counterexamples are to the conjecture with coefficients, not the original conjecture.
  • Misstating the Bowditch boundary for relatively hyperbolic groups: The Bowditch boundary is the Gromov boundary of the coned-off graph, not the original Cayley graph. Parabolic points correspond to cosets of peripheral subgroups; the boundary is not necessarily a topological sphere or Sierpiński carpet — it can be a Menger curve or other compacta.
  • Overlooking bounded coset penetration (BCP): The definition of relative hyperbolicity requires BCP for geodesics in the original Cayley graph. Without BCP, the coned-off graph can be hyperbolic but the group not relatively hyperbolic (e.g., certain graph manifolds).
  • Confusing the assembly map with the Dirac operator: The assembly map is a map in K-theory; the Dirac-dual Dirac method constructs an inverse using Kasparov's KK-theory. The γ-element is the obstruction; γ = 1 is equivalent to the conjecture.

Subgroup Distortion and the Geometry of Subgroups

The distortion of a finitely generated subgroup H ≤ G measures how the intrinsic word metric on H compares to the metric induced from the ambient Cayley graph of G. Formally, if S and T are finite generating sets for G and H respectively, the distortion function DistHG(n) = max{ |h|T : h ∈ H, |h|S ≤ n }. A subgroup is undistorted (or quasi-isometrically embedded) if DistHG(n) ≍ n; otherwise it is distorted. In hyperbolic groups, every quasiconvex subgroup is undistorted, and the converse holds: a finitely generated subgroup of a hyperbolic group is quasiconvex iff it is undistorted. The classic example of exponential distortion is the subgroup ⟨a, t−1at⟩ ≅ F2 inside the Baumslag-Solitar group BS(1,2) = ⟨a, t | tat−1 = a2⟩, where |t−natn|BS = 2n+1 but its length in the free subgroup grows like 2n. Polynomial distortion occurs in nilpotent groups: in the Heisenberg group H3(ℤ) = ⟨x, y, z | [x,y] = z, [x,z] = [y,z] = 1⟩, the center ⟨z⟩ ≅ ℤ has quadratic distortion because zn = [xn, y] has word length ≍ √n in the ambient group. Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth implies that in a nilpotent group, every subgroup has polynomial distortion, and the degree of distortion is bounded by the nilpotency class. The Morse Lemma for quasigeodesics in hyperbolic spaces gives a geometric characterization: a subgroup is undistorted iff its Cayley graph embeds as a quasiconvex subset. For CAT(0) groups, the Flat Torus Theorem implies that any abelian subgroup is undistorted and acts by semisimple isometries on the CAT(0) space. The distortion spectrum of a group — the set of possible distortion functions of its finitely generated subgroups — is a quasi-isometry invariant. Ol'shanskii and Sapir showed that any non-decreasing recursive function can be realized as the distortion of a subgroup in some finitely presented group. In mapping class groups, the distortion of subgroups is closely related to the geometry of the curve complex: a subgroup is undistorted iff its orbit in the curve complex is a quasiconvex subset. The subgroup distortion also controls the Dehn function of the subgroup: if H is finitely presented and undistorted in G, then the Dehn function of H is bounded above by that of G.

The Tits Alternative and Geometric Variants

The Tits Alternative (1972) states that every finitely generated linear group over a field either contains a non-abelian free subgroup or is virtually solvable. This dichotomy has profound geometric generalizations. For groups acting properly on CAT(0) spaces, the Geometric Tits Alternative (Ballmann, Brin, Eberlein) asserts that either the group contains a rank-one isometry (hence a free subgroup via the ping-pong lemma on the visual boundary) or it is virtually abelian. In the context of mapping class groups, the Tits Alternative for Mod(S) (Ivanyos, McCarthy) states that every subgroup either contains a free group of rank two or is virtually abelian; the proof uses the action on the curve complex and the classification of subgroups via the Nielsen-Thurston theory. For Out(Fn), the Tits Alternative holds (Bestvina-Feighn-Handel) using the action on the free splitting complex. In relatively hyperbolic groups, a subgroup either is relatively hyperbolic (with respect to the induced peripheral structure) or contains a free subgroup; this is the Relative Tits Alternative (Osin). The Uniform Tits Alternative (Breuillard-Gelander) strengthens the original: for any d and field k, there exists N = N(d, k) such that any finite subset of GLd(k) either generates a virtually solvable group or contains two elements generating a free subgroup of rank two. This has applications to expansion in finite simple groups and the diameter of Cayley graphs. The Tits Alternative for groups of polynomial growth is trivial (they are virtually nilpotent, hence no free subgroups). For groups with Kazhdan's Property (T), the Tits Alternative fails in general: there exist Property (T) groups without free subgroups (e.g., certain lattices in Sp(n,1) or random groups at density > 1/3). However, for linear groups with Property (T), the Margulis Normal Subgroup Theorem implies that any infinite normal subgroup has finite index, so the Tits Alternative holds vacuously. The Geometric Tits Alternative for CAT(0) cube complexes (Caprace-Sageev) states that a group acting properly and essentially on a finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex either contains a rank-one isometry (hence a free subgroup) or preserves a non-trivial product decomposition. This leads to the classification of such groups via the Rank Rigidity Theorem: an irreducible CAT(0) cube complex with a geometric group action either has a rank-one isometry or is a product of irreducible factors. The Tits Alternative for acylindrically hyperbolic groups (Osin) states that every subgroup either is virtually cyclic or contains a non-abelian free subgroup; this covers mapping class groups, Out(Fn), and many relatively hyperbolic groups. The proof uses the action on a hyperbolic space and the classification of elements as elliptic, loxodromic, or parabolic.

Worked Problem: Distortion in the Heisenberg Group
Let H = ⟨x, y, z | [x,y] = z, [x,z] = [y,z] = 1⟩ be the discrete Heisenberg group. Compute the distortion of the central subgroup ⟨z⟩ ≅ ℤ in H.
In H, every element has a unique normal form xaybzc with a,b,c ∈ ℤ. The word metric with respect to {x,y,z±1} satisfies |xaybzc| ≍ |a| + |b| + √|c|. For zn, we have zn = [xn, y] = x−ny−1xny, so |zn| ≤ 2|n|+2. Conversely, any word representing zn must have exponent sum zero in x and y, and the z-exponent is the signed area enclosed by the path in the xy-plane. By the isoperimetric inequality in ℤ2, a loop of length L encloses area at most L2/4π, so |n| ≤ C|w|2 for some constant C. Hence |w| ≥ √(|n|/C). Thus |zn| ≍ √n. The intrinsic metric on ⟨z⟩ is |zn|⟨z⟩ = |n|. Therefore Dist⟨z⟩H(n) ≍ n2. The central subgroup has quadratic distortion.
Exam Strategy: Distortion and Quasi-isometry Invariants
When asked whether a property is a quasi-isometry invariant, check if it is defined purely in terms of the coarse geometry of the Cayley graph. Subgroup distortion is a quasi-isometry invariant of the pair (G, H), but the distortion function itself depends on the choice of generating sets up to ≍. For the Tits Alternative, remember: it holds for linear groups, CAT(0) groups with rank-one isometries, mapping class groups, Out(Fn), and acylindrically hyperbolic groups. It fails for general Property (T) groups and for groups of polynomial growth (which are virtually nilpotent). A common trap is to assume the Tits Alternative holds for all hyperbolic groups — it does, because hyperbolic groups are acylindrically hyperbolic, but the proof uses the boundary action, not linearity.

Geometric Dimension and the Eilenberg-Ganea Conjecture

The geometric dimension gd(G) of a torsion-free group G is the minimal dimension of a contractible CW-complex on which G acts freely and properly discontinuously (i.e., a K(G,1) complex). For groups with torsion, one considers the geometric dimension for proper actions gdprop(G), the minimal dimension of a contractible G-CW-complex with finite stabilizers. The cohomological dimension cd(G) is the projective dimension of the trivial ℤG-module ℤ; for torsion-free groups, cd(G) ≤ gd(G) with equality if gd(G) ≠ 2. The Eilenberg-Ganea Conjecture (1957) asserts that for any group G with cd(G) ≥ 3, we have gd(G) = cd(G). The only open case is whether there exists a group with cd(G) = 2 but gd(G) = 3. Such a group would be a counterexample to the conjecture. Bestvina and Brady (1997) constructed a group with cd = 2 that does not act freely on any 2-dimensional contractible complex, but their construction uses a non-positively curved cube complex with a Morse function; the group is the kernel of a homomorphism from a right-angled Artin group to ℤ. This group has gd = 3, but it has torsion? No, it is torsion-free. Wait: the Bestvina-Brady group is torsion-free, has cd = 2, and does not act freely on any 2-complex, so gd ≥ 3. It is known that gd ≤ 3 for this group, so gd = 3. This would be a counterexample to Eilenberg-Ganea if the group is of type FP2 but not FP3. Actually, the Bestvina-Brady group is of type FP2 but not FP3, so its cohomological dimension over ℤ is 2, but its geometric dimension is 3. This is a counterexample to the Eilenberg-Ganea conjecture? The conjecture is usually stated for groups of type FP (or FL). The Bestvina-Brady group is not of type FP3, so it does not satisfy the hypotheses of the original conjecture (which requires finite projective dimension). The Eilenberg-Ganea conjecture for groups of type FP remains open. For groups acting on CAT(0) spaces, the geometric dimension equals the dimension of the CAT(0) space if the action is geometric. The Flat Torus Theorem implies that any abelian subgroup of rank r in a CAT(0) group forces the space to contain an isometrically embedded ℝr, so gd(G) ≥ rank(G). For hyperbolic groups, the geometric dimension equals the topological dimension of the Gromov boundary plus one (Bestvina-Mess). The Virtual Cohomological Dimension vcd(G) = cd(H) for any torsion-free finite-index subgroup H ≤ G. For mapping class groups Mod(Sg), vcd = 4g−5 for g ≥ 2 (Harer). For Out(Fn), vcd = 2n−3 for n ≥ 2 (Culler-Vogtmann). The geometric dimension of relatively hyperbolic groups is related to the dimensions of the peripheral subgroups and the coned-off space. If G is hyperbolic relative to {Pi}, then gd(G) = max{ gd(Pi), dim(∂G)+1 } under certain conditions. The Singer Conjecture on the ℓ2-Betti numbers of aspherical manifolds is related: if M is a closed aspherical manifold of dimension 2k, then its ℓ2-Betti numbers vanish except possibly in degree k. This is known for manifolds with non-positive sectional curvature.

Median Spaces, Roller Boundaries, and Cubical Geometry

A median space is a metric space (X, d) such that for any three points x, y, z ∈ X, there exists a unique point m(x,y,z) ∈ X (the median) lying on geodesics between each pair: d(x,y) = d(x,m) + d(m,y), etc. Trees, ℝ-trees, and CAT(0) cube complexes are median spaces. The Roller boundary ∂RX of a CAT(0) cube complex X is the set of ultrafilters on the set of halfspaces of X, equipped with a topology making it a compact totally disconnected space. It refines the visual boundary: there is a continuous surjection ∂RX → ∂visX. The Roller boundary encodes the combinatorial geometry of X. For a group G acting properly on a CAT(0) cube complex X, the action extends to the Roller boundary. The Haglund-Wise Special Cube Complex Theory characterizes when a group acting on a CAT(0) cube complex is virtually special (embeds in a right-angled Artin group). A cube complex is special if its hyperplanes do not self-intersect, self-osculate, or inter-osculate. The Virtual Specialness Theorem (Agol, Wise) states that every hyperbolic group acting properly and cocompactly on a CAT(0) cube complex is virtually special. This implies that such groups are virtually RFRS (residually finite rationally solvable), hence have separable quasiconvex subgroups and satisfy the Virtual Haken Conjecture for 3-manifolds. The Median Rank of a median space is the maximal dimension of a cube isometrically embedded in it. For a group acting on a median space, the median rank bounds the rank of abelian subgroups. The Contact Graph of a CAT(0) cube complex (Hagen) is a hyperbolic graph on which the group acts acylindrically; this provides a powerful tool for studying cubulated groups. The Hierarchically Hyperbolic Groups (Behrstock-Hagen-Sisto) generalize mapping class groups and cubulated groups: a group is HHG if it has a set of hyperbolic spaces (the index set) with projections satisfying certain axioms. The Roller boundary of a median graph is a Stone space (compact, totally disconnected, Hausdorff). The action of G on ∂RX is amenable if G is amenable. For hyperbolic groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes, the Roller boundary is a compactification that is not necessarily metrizable. The Cubical Dimension of a group is the minimal dimension of a CAT(0) cube complex on which it acts properly. For right-angled Artin groups, the cubical dimension equals the size of the largest clique in the defining graph. For mapping class groups, the cubical dimension is infinite (they do not act properly on any finite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex), but they act on the infinite-dimensional cube complex of multicurves.

The Geometry of Right-Angled Artin Groups and Graph Products

A right-angled Artin group (RAAG) A(Γ) is defined by a finite simplicial graph Γ: generators are vertices, relations are [v,w] = 1 for each edge {v,w}. RAAGs interpolate between free groups (Γ edgeless) and free abelian groups (Γ complete). The Salvetti complex S(Γ) is a non-positively curved cube complex with fundamental group A(Γ); its universal cover is a CAT(0) cube complex on which A(Γ) acts geometrically. The dimension of A(Γ) equals the clique number ω(Γ). The cohomological dimension cd(A(Γ)) = ω(Γ). The Dehn function of a RAAG is either linear (if Γ is a join, i.e., A(Γ) splits as a direct product) or quadratic (otherwise). RAAGs are biautomatic and have solvable word and conjugacy problems. The subgroup structure of RAAGs is rich: every finitely generated subgroup is a RAAG (Droms-Servatius) iff Γ is a forest; in general, subgroups can be arbitrarily complicated (e.g., Bestvina-Brady groups). The outer automorphism group Out(A(Γ)) is generated by inversions, transvections, and partial conjugations (Laurence). The Tits Alternative holds for RAAGs: every subgroup either contains a non-abelian free group or is virtually abelian. The geometric dimension of Out(A(Γ)) is known for many Γ. The graph product of groups {Gv} over Γ replaces vertices by groups and edges by direct products: G(Γ) = ∗v Gv / ⟨⟨ [g,h] = 1 for g ∈ Gv, h ∈ Gw, {v,w} ∈ E(Γ) ⟩⟩. If each Gv is hyperbolic, then G(Γ) is hyperbolic iff Γ has no induced squares (i.e., no C4). The CAT(0) geometry of graph products is controlled by the link condition on the Salvetti-like complex. The quasi-isometric classification of RAAGs (Behrstock-Neumann) states that A(Γ) ≃QI A(Γ') iff Γ and Γ' have the same join decomposition up to a certain equivalence. The coarse geometry of RAAGs is captured by the extension graph Γe (Kim-Koberda): vertices are conjugates of generators, edges connect commuting elements. The extension graph is hyperbolic and quasi-isometric to the contact graph of the Salvetti complex. The automorphism group of a RAAG acts on the extension graph with acylindrical action. The virtual cohomological dimension of Out(A(Γ)) is computed by Charney-Vogtmann using the spine of Outer space for RAAGs. The K(π,1) conjecture for RAAGs (that the Salvetti complex is a K(π,1)) is true because the universal cover is CAT(0). The Borel Conjecture for RAAGs (that any homotopy equivalence of closed aspherical manifolds with fundamental group A(Γ) is homotopic to a homeomorphism) is known in many cases via surgery theory and the Farrell-Jones conjecture.

  • Confusing subgroup distortion with quasi-isometric embedding: A subgroup can be undistorted without being quasi-isometrically embedded if the inclusion map is not a quasi-isometric embedding (e.g., the inclusion of a distorted subgroup into its ambient group composed with a quasi-isometry). Distortion is a property of the pair (G, H) with chosen generating sets; quasi-isometric embedding is a property of the map.
  • Assuming the Tits Alternative holds for all groups: It fails for groups of polynomial growth (virtually nilpotent, no free subgroups), for certain Property (T) groups without free subgroups (e.g., random groups at density > 1/3), and for Burnside groups of large odd exponent. It holds for linear groups, hyperbolic groups, mapping class groups, Out(Fn), and acylindrically hyperbolic groups.
  • Misstating the Eilenberg-Ganea Conjecture: The conjecture is that gd(G) = cd(G) for cd(G) ≥ 3. The case cd = 2 is the only open case. The Bestvina-Brady group has cd = 2 and gd = 3, but it is not of type FP3, so it is not a counterexample to the conjecture as usually stated (which requires finite projective dimension).
  • Equating the Roller boundary with the visual boundary: The Roller boundary ∂RX of a CAT(0) cube complex is a compact totally disconnected space that maps continuously onto the visual boundary ∂visX, but the map is not injective in general. Points in the Roller boundary correspond to ultrafilters on halfspaces; points in the visual boundary correspond to equivalence classes of geodesic rays.
  • Believing all RAAGs are hyperbolic: A RAAG is hyperbolic iff its defining graph has no induced squares (no C4). If Γ contains a square, the RAAG contains a ℤ2 subgroup and is not hyperbolic. The Dehn function is quadratic in this case.
  • Confusing virtual specialness with residual finiteness: Every virtually special group is residually finite (since RAAGs are linear), but not every residually finite group acting on a CAT(0) cube complex is virtually special. Virtual specialness implies separability of quasiconvex subgroups, which is stronger than residual finiteness.
  • Overlooking the role of the extension graph in RAAG geometry: The extension graph Γe is hyperbolic and encodes the coarse geometry of the RAAG. The action of Out(A(Γ)) on Γe is acylindrical, which is a key tool for studying the automorphism group. The contact graph of the Salvetti complex is quasi-isometric to Γe.

Asymptotic Cones and Ultralimits in Geometric Group Theory

Asymptotic cones provide a way to capture the large‑scale geometry of a metric space by scaling the metric down to zero and taking an ultralimit. Given a metric space (X, d), a non‑principal ultrafilter ω on ℕ, a sequence of basepoints (x_n) and scaling factors λ_n → ∞, the asymptotic cone Cone_ω(X, (x_n), (λ_n)) is the ultralimit of the rescaled spaces (X, d/λ_n, x_n). Gromov introduced this construction to study groups of polynomial growth; van den Dries and Wilkie later placed it on a firm model‑theoretic foundation using ultrapowers of ℝ. A fundamental theorem states that a finitely generated group has polynomial growth iff all its asymptotic cones are locally compact, and in that case the cones are finite‑dimensional real vector spaces (Pansu). For hyperbolic groups, every asymptotic cone is an ℝ‑tree, reflecting the thin‑triangle condition at all scales. The quasi‑isometry type of a group is not determined by a single asymptotic cone, but the collection of all cones (varying ultrafilters, basepoints, and scalings) is a quasi‑isometry invariant. Applications include the proof that quasi‑isometric nilpotent groups have isomorphic Lie algebras (Pansu), the classification of groups with quadratic Dehn function via their asymptotic cones (Druţu–Sapir), and the use of asymptotic cones to distinguish certain right‑angled Artin groups up to quasi‑isometry (Behrstock–Neumann). The construction also underlies the notion of a “graded” asymptotic cone, where one keeps track of the scaling sequence to obtain a bi‑Lipschitz invariant.

The Geometry of Mapping Class Groups and Curve Complexes

The mapping class group Mod(S) of a finite‑type surface S acts on the curve complex C(S), whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves and simplices correspond to collections of pairwise disjoint curves. Masur and Minsky proved that C(S) is Gromov‑hyperbolic, and they developed the hierarchy machinery: every pair of points in Mod(S) is connected by a hierarchy of geodesics in curve complexes of subsurfaces. This yields a distance formula expressing the word metric on Mod(S) (with respect to a finite generating set) as a sum of projection distances to the various curve complexes, up to a uniform additive and multiplicative error. Consequently, Mod(S) is hierarchically hyperbolic in the sense of Behrstock–Hagen–Sisto. The action of Mod(S) on C(S) is acylindrical but not proper; the stabilizer of a curve is a product of mapping class groups of the complementary subsurfaces. The quasi‑isometric classification of mapping class groups is known: Mod(S) ≃_QI Mod(S') iff S and S' have the same genus and number of punctures (Kida). The curve complex also serves as a model for the Weil–Petersson metric on Teichmüller space; Brock showed that the Weil–Petersson metric is quasi‑isometric to the pants graph, which in turn is quasi‑isometric to C(S). The ending lamination space EL(S), which is the Gromov boundary of C(S), carries a natural topology and a Mod(S)‑invariant measure class (Thurston). These geometric structures have been used to prove that Mod(S) has finite asymptotic dimension (Bell–Fujiwara) and that it satisfies the Farrell–Jones conjecture (Bartels–Bestvina).

Identifying acylindrical hyperbolicity
Show that a non‑elementary relatively hyperbolic group G with respect to a collection of proper subgroups {H_i} is acylindrically hyperbolic.
By definition, G admits a proper, cocompact action on a hyperbolic space X (the coned‑off Cayley graph) such that the peripheral subgroups are precisely the stabilizers of the added cone vertices. The action on X is acylindrical because for any ε > 0 there exist R, N such that the set of group elements moving two points at distance ≥ R by less than ε has cardinality ≤ N; this follows from the relative hyperbolicity axioms (bounded coset penetration). Since G is non‑elementary, X contains a loxodromic element (any element not conjugate into a peripheral subgroup acts loxodromically on X). Osin's theorem then states that a group acting acylindrically on a hyperbolic space with a loxodromic element is acylindrically hyperbolic. Hence G is acylindrically hyperbolic.
Exam strategy
When a question asks to prove acylindrical hyperbolicity, first identify a hyperbolic space on which the group acts (coned‑off graph, curve complex, or a suitable graph of groups). Verify the acylindricity condition using the relevant bounded penetration or projection properties. Then exhibit a loxodromic element — often any element of infinite order not contained in a peripheral or parabolic subgroup. Cite Osin's characterization as the final step.

Hierarchically Hyperbolic Groups

Hierarchically hyperbolic groups (HHGs) were introduced by Behrstock, Hagen, and Sisto to axiomatize the coarse geometry shared by mapping class groups, right‑angled Artin groups, and many other groups. An HHG structure on a group G consists of an index set 𝔖 of hyperbolic spaces {C_U} (each quasi‑isometric to a tree or a hyperbolic graph), projection maps π_U: G → C_U, and a set of relations (nesting, orthogonality, transversality) satisfying axioms that mimic the subsurface projection machinery for mapping class groups. The key consequence is a distance formula: for any finite generating set, the word metric d_G(g, h) is coarsely equivalent to the sum over U ∈ 𝔖 of the projection distances d_U(π_U(g), π_U(h)), truncated at a uniform threshold. This formula implies that HHGs have quadratic Dehn functions, satisfy the Morse lemma for quasigeodesics, and admit a well‑defined boundary ∂G that is a compact metrizable space. Every HHG acts acylindrically on the hyperbolic space associated to the maximal element of 𝔖, so HHGs are acylindrically hyperbolic unless they are virtually abelian. The class is closed under taking finite index subgroups, direct products with finite groups, and graph products over finite graphs. Important examples include mapping class groups (with 𝔖 the set of essential subsurfaces), right‑angled Artin groups (with 𝔖 the set of joins in the defining graph), fundamental groups of non‑geometric 3‑manifolds, and many cubulated groups. The HHG framework has been used to prove quasi‑isometric rigidity for several families, to establish the existence of a finite‑dimensional classifying space for proper actions, and to verify the Farrell–Jones conjecture for HHGs with finite asymptotic dimension.

Acylindrically Hyperbolic Groups

A group G is acylindrically hyperbolic if it admits a non‑elementary acylindrical action on a Gromov‑hyperbolic space. “Non‑elementary” means the action has at least one loxodromic element (infinite order, acting with positive translation length) and the limit set on the Gromov boundary contains more than two points. Osin proved that this definition is equivalent to the existence of a proper, non‑trivial hyperbolically embedded subgroup. The class is vast: it contains all non‑elementary relatively hyperbolic groups, all non‑virtually cyclic groups acting properly on a hyperbolic space (e.g., hyperbolic groups), mapping class groups (via the curve complex), Out(F_n) for n ≥ 2 (via the free factor complex), and many groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes with a rank‑one isometry. Acylindrical hyperbolicity implies strong algebraic properties: the group has infinite conjugacy classes (ICC), it contains non‑abelian free subgroups (Tits alternative holds), it has uncountably many normal subgroups, and it admits non‑trivial homogeneous quasimorphisms (hence infinite dimensional second bounded cohomology). The acylindrical action also yields a “hyperbolically embedded” subgroup that is quasi‑isometrically embedded and has strong separation properties. This structure has been used to prove that acylindrically hyperbolic groups have the Haagerup property iff they are virtually free, and that they satisfy the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients under certain finiteness assumptions. The theory also provides a unified approach to the study of Out(F_n) and mapping class groups via their actions on the free factor complex and the curve complex respectively.

The Coarse Geometry of Out(F_n) and Free Group Automorphisms

The outer automorphism group Out(F_n) of a free group of rank n ≥ 2 shares many coarse geometric features with mapping class groups. Bestvina and Feighn introduced the free factor complex 𝓕_n, whose vertices are conjugacy classes of proper free factors of F_n and simplices correspond to chains of inclusions. They proved that 𝓕_n is Gromov‑hyperbolic and that Out(F_n) acts on it acylindrically. The action is non‑elementary, so Out(F_n) is acylindrically hyperbolic. Handel and Mosher developed a hierarchy machinery for Out(F_n) using the free factor complex and its subcomplexes, yielding a distance formula analogous to that for mapping class groups. The Lipschitz metric on Outer space (the space of marked metric graphs) provides a geodesic metric that is Out(F_n)‑invariant; the action on Outer space is proper but not cocompact. The quotient of Outer space by Out(F_n) is the moduli space of graphs, which is not compact but has a well‑understood compactification (the Culler–Vogtmann compactification). The coarse geometry of Out(F_n) is also captured by the “free splitting complex” and the “cyclic splitting complex”, both hyperbolic. The quasi‑isometric classification of Out(F_n) is known: Out(F_n) ≃_QI Out(F_m) iff n = m (Farb–Handel). The group has exponential growth, quadratic Dehn function (for n ≥ 3), and finite asymptotic dimension (Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara). The Tits alternative holds: every subgroup either contains a non‑abelian free group or is virtually abelian. The boundary of the free factor complex is homeomorphic to the space of arational trees, which plays the role of the ending lamination space for mapping class groups.

  • Confusing asymptotic cones with ultralimits of groups: an asymptotic cone is a metric space, not a group; the group structure is lost unless the original group is nilpotent and the cone inherits a Carnot group structure.
  • Assuming every hyperbolic space on which a group acts yields acylindrical hyperbolicity: the action must be acylindrical and non‑elementary; many groups act on hyperbolic spaces with elementary or non‑acylindrical actions.
  • Thinking that hierarchical hyperbolicity implies hyperbolicity: HHGs are generally not hyperbolic (e.g., ℤ² is an HHG but not hyperbolic); the hierarchy provides a hyperbolic “coarse” structure, not a hyperbolic metric.
  • Believing that Out(F_n) is relatively hyperbolic: it is not relatively hyperbolic for n ≥ 3 (it contains ℤ² subgroups), but it is acylindrically hyperbolic via the free factor complex.
  • Equating the curve complex with the pants graph: they are quasi‑isometric but not isometric; the pants graph has vertices corresponding to pants decompositions, while the curve complex has vertices for curves.
  • Overlooking the role of the threshold in the distance formula: the sum of projection distances is truncated at a uniform constant; ignoring the threshold leads to an incorrect metric estimate.
  • Assuming acylindrical hyperbolicity implies relative hyperbolicity: there are many acylindrically hyperbolic groups (e.g., mapping class groups) that are not relatively hyperbolic.

Small Cancellation Theory and Geometric Applications

Small cancellation theory provides a combinatorial method for constructing groups with prescribed geometric properties by imposing metric conditions on the overlaps of relators in a presentation. The classical conditions C′(λ) and T(q) control the length of common pieces between distinct relators: a presentation satisfies C′(λ) if every piece (a maximal common subword of two cyclic conjugates of relators) has length strictly less than λ times the length of the relator containing it. The condition T(q) requires that no relator can be written as a product of fewer than q pieces. The fundamental theorem of Greendlinger (1960) states that a finite presentation satisfying C′(1/6) yields a group whose Cayley graph is locally a tree of relators, and Dehn’s algorithm solves the word problem in linear time. Lyndon and Schupp extended this to C′(1/4)–T(4) presentations, which also admit a linear Dehn algorithm. Geometrically, these conditions imply that the presentation complex can be metrized as a CAT(0) or even a CAT(−1) space when the relators are sufficiently long and the cancellation is small enough. In particular, Gromov showed that a random presentation at density d < 1/2 satisfies C′(1/6) with overwhelming probability, yielding infinite hyperbolic groups. This probabilistic method underpins the theory of random groups. Moreover, small cancellation techniques are used to construct groups with exotic properties: Ol’shanskii’s Tarski monsters (infinite groups where every proper subgroup is cyclic of prime order) arise from a graded small cancellation argument; the construction of groups with prescribed Dehn functions (e.g., polynomial of any degree ≥ 2) uses a careful layering of relators satisfying C′(1/6) at each stage. The theory also interacts with the geometry of van Kampen diagrams: the isoperimetric inequality for a presentation is equivalent to a linear bound on the area of reduced diagrams, and small cancellation gives a combinatorial proof of linear isoperimetric inequality for hyperbolic groups. In the setting of relatively hyperbolic groups, Osin developed a relative small cancellation theory where the peripheral subgroups play the role of “long” relators, enabling the construction of relatively hyperbolic quotients with controlled peripheral structure.

The Grigorchuk Group and Intermediate Growth

The Grigorchuk group G, constructed by Rostislav Grigorchuk in 1984, is the first example of a finitely generated group with intermediate growth — growth strictly between polynomial and exponential. It is defined as a subgroup of the automorphism group of a rooted binary tree, generated by four automorphisms a, b, c, d satisfying a² = b² = c² = d² = 1, bcd = 1, and the recursive relations b = (a, c), c = (a, d), d = (1, b) where the notation (x, y) denotes the automorphism acting as x on the left subtree and y on the right. The group is a 2‑group (every element has order a power of 2), infinite, finitely generated, and just‑infinite (every proper quotient is finite). Its growth function γ(n) satisfies exp(n^α) ≲ γ(n) ≲ exp(n^β) with α ≈ 0.767 and β ≈ 0.991 (Bartholdi, 1998; improved by Erschler, 2004). The exact growth type remains unknown, but it is known to be subexponential and superpolynomial. The group is amenable (Følner sequences exist) but not elementary amenable, providing a counterexample to the conjecture that all amenable groups are elementary amenable. It also has the congruence subgroup property with respect to the level stabilizers of the tree action, and its profinite completion is a just‑infinite pro‑2 group of finite width. The Grigorchuk group acts on the boundary of the binary tree (a Cantor set) by measure‑preserving transformations, yielding an ergodic action with discrete spectrum. Its Schreier graphs form a sequence of expanders? Actually, the Schreier graphs of the action on levels of the tree are not expanders; they have polynomial volume growth but the group itself has intermediate growth. The group is also a branch group: the rigid stabilizers of subtrees have finite index and their direct product embeds with finite index in the whole group. This self‑similar structure is the prototype for the theory of automaton groups and iterated monodromy groups (Nekrashevych). The Grigorchuk group has solvable word problem (in fact, it is in NC), but its conjugacy problem is open. It does not satisfy the Tits alternative: it contains no non‑abelian free subgroup (being a torsion group) and is not virtually solvable. Its subgroup structure is rich: it contains free abelian subgroups of arbitrarily large rank? No, it is a torsion group, so no infinite cyclic subgroups. All abelian subgroups are finite. The group has infinite conjugacy classes (ICC) and its reduced C*‑algebra is simple (Grigorchuk–Żuk). The spectral measure of the simple random walk on G has a pure point component, a singular continuous component, and no absolutely continuous part (Grigorchuk–Żuk, 2001).

Worked Example: Growth Comparison
Let G be the Grigorchuk group and F₂ the free group of rank 2. Compare the asymptotic growth of the balls B_G(n) and BF₂(n) in the standard generating sets. Explain why G cannot contain a non‑abelian free subgroup.
The growth of F₂ is exponential: |BF₂(n)| ∼ (2·3ⁿ⁻¹) for large n. For G, we have exp(n^α) ≲ |B_G(n)| ≲ exp(n^β) with α, β < 1. Hence |B_G(n)| grows strictly slower than any exponential function. If G contained a non‑abelian free subgroup H, then the growth of H (which is exponential) would be bounded above by the growth of G (since the inclusion is a Lipschitz map). This contradicts the subexponential upper bound for G. Therefore G contains no non‑abelian free subgroup. This also shows that the Tits alternative fails for G.
Exam Strategy: Recognising Intermediate Growth
When a question asks for an example of a group with growth strictly between polynomial and exponential, the Grigorchuk group is the canonical answer. Remember its defining properties: finitely generated, infinite torsion (2‑group), amenable but not elementary amenable, just‑infinite, branch, and solvable word problem. Do not confuse it with the Gupta–Sidki groups (which are 3‑groups on a ternary tree) or with the Basilica group (which has exponential growth). The key phrase “intermediate growth” in a multiple‑choice question almost always points to Grigorchuk.

CAT(0) Spaces Beyond Cube Complexes: Flat Torus Theorem and Rank Rigidity

While CAT(0) cube complexes provide a combinatorial gateway to non‑positive curvature, the general theory of CAT(0) metric spaces yields powerful structural results for groups acting properly and cocompactly by isometries. The Flat Torus Theorem (Gromoll–Wolf, Lawson–Yau, Ballmann–Brin–Eberlein) states that if a free abelian group ℤⁿ acts properly by semisimple isometries on a complete CAT(0) space X, then X contains an n‑dimensional flat (an isometrically embedded Euclidean space ℝⁿ) that is invariant under the action, and the action on this flat is by translations. Consequently, any abelian subgroup of a CAT(0) group is finitely generated and virtually free abelian, and its rank equals the maximal dimension of a flat on which it acts. The Rank Rigidity Theorem (Ballmann, Burns–Spatzier) classifies irreducible CAT(0) spaces with a geometric group action: either the space has a rank‑one geodesic (an axis that does not bound a flat half‑plane), or it is a symmetric space of non‑compact type, a Euclidean building, or a product of such. For a group Γ acting geometrically on an irreducible CAT(0) space X, rank rigidity implies that if Γ does not contain a rank‑one isometry, then X is a symmetric space or building and Γ is a lattice in a semisimple Lie group (or a uniform lattice in a building). This dichotomy underlies the classification of higher‑rank lattices and the proof of Margulis superrigidity in the CAT(0) setting. The Duality Theorem for CAT(0) groups (Bestvina) relates the cohomological dimension of Γ to the topology of the visual boundary ∂X: if Γ is a Poincaré duality group of dimension n, then ∂X is a homology (n−1)‑sphere. The Flat Closing Theorem (Eberlein) guarantees that every maximal flat in a compact locally CAT(0) space is the image of a totally geodesic flat torus. These results are essential for understanding the large‑scale geometry of CAT(0) groups: they imply the Tits alternative (every subgroup either contains a free subgroup or is virtually abelian) for CAT(0) groups with a rank‑one isometry, and they provide the geometric input for the Farrell–Jones conjecture for such groups via the controlled topology approach.

Consequences for Group Structure

  • Centralizers of semisimple elements are virtually abelian and split as a product of a Euclidean factor and a compact group.
  • Solvable subgroups are virtually abelian (by the Flat Torus Theorem applied to the derived series).
  • Rank one isometries produce hyperbolic elements in the sense of Gromov; their axes are Morse quasigeodesics.
  • Visual boundary ∂X with the Tits metric is a CAT(1) space; its spherical building structure appears in the higher‑rank case.

The theory extends to CAT(0) spaces with isolated flats (Hruska–Kleiner), where the space is relatively hyperbolic with respect to the stabilizers of the flats. This framework covers mapping class groups (via the Teichmüller metric) and Out(Fₙ) (via the Lipschitz metric on Outer space) after passing to appropriate subspaces. The Morse Lemma for rank‑one geodesics in CAT(0) spaces (Charney–Sultan) ensures that quasigeodesics stay close to the geodesic, a key tool for proving hyperbolicity of certain subcomplexes.

The Farrell–Jones Conjecture and Geometric Group Theory

The Farrell–Jones Conjecture (1993) is an isomorphism conjecture in algebraic K‑ and L‑theory of group rings. For a group Γ, it predicts that the assembly map Hₙ^Γ(EVCycΓ; 𝕂ℤ) → Kₙ(ℤΓ) is an isomorphism for all n, where EVCycΓ is the classifying space for actions with virtually cyclic stabilizers, and 𝕂ℤ is the algebraic K‑theory spectrum of ℤ. An analogous statement holds for L‑theory (quadratic forms). The conjecture implies several classical conjectures: the Borel Conjecture (topological rigidity of aspherical manifolds), the Kaplansky Conjectures (no non‑trivial idempotents, no zero divisors, units are trivial in torsion‑free groups), and the Novikov Conjecture (homotopy invariance of higher signatures). Geometrically, the conjecture is known for groups acting properly on CAT(0) spaces (Bartels–Lück–Reich, 2008), for hyperbolic groups (Bartels–Lück, 2012), for CAT(0) groups with a rank‑one isometry (Wegner, 2015), and for relatively hyperbolic groups with virtually abelian peripherals (Bartels–Bestvina–Farrell–Lück). The proof uses controlled topology and the construction of flow spaces that encode the dynamics of the group action on the boundary. For hyperbolic groups, the flow space is the space of geodesics in the Cayley graph; for CAT(0) groups, it is the space of geodesics in the CAT(0) space. The transversality and finite asymptotic dimension of these flow spaces allow the application of the Farrell–Jones machinery (the FJ‑assembly map). The conjecture is also known for fundamental groups of 3‑manifolds (Bartels–Lück–Weinberger), for mapping class groups (Bartels–Bestvina–Farrell–Lück), and for Out(Fₙ) (Wegner). A major open case is the conjecture for all linear groups (e.g., GLₙ(ℤ) for n ≥ 3). The Baum–Connes conjecture (with coefficients) is the analytic counterpart; it is known for a larger class of groups (e.g., all a‑T‑menable groups) but fails for some groups with property (T) (Higson–Lafforgue–Skandalis). The Farrell–Jones conjecture is stronger in the sense that it implies the Baum–Connes conjecture for torsion‑free groups via the assembly map comparison. The K‑theoretic Farrell–Jones conjecture for ℤΓ is equivalent to the L‑theoretic one for groups satisfying the orientation character condition. The conjecture has been verified for all groups with finite asymptotic dimension (Guentner–Tessera–Yu) and for groups that coarsely embed into Hilbert space (Yu). However, there exist groups that do not coarsely embed into Hilbert space but still satisfy the conjecture (e.g., certain expander‑based constructions).

Algorithmic Problems in Hyperbolic Groups: Isomorphism, Conjugacy, and Subgroup Membership

Hyperbolic groups enjoy a remarkable algorithmic tractability. The word problem is solvable in linear time via Dehn’s algorithm (or the fellow‑traveler property of automatic structures). The conjugacy problem is also solvable: given two words u, v, one can decide whether they represent conjugate elements by enumerating all cyclically reduced conjugates of bounded length (the bound depends only on the hyperbolicity constant δ and the generating set). The algorithm runs in exponential time in general, but for hyperbolic groups with a biautomatic structure it can be done in quadratic time. The isomorphism problem — given two finite presentations of hyperbolic groups, decide whether they define isomorphic groups — is a major open question. It is known to be decidable for virtually special hyperbolic groups (using the quasi‑isometric classification of special cube complexes and the fact that isomorphism implies quasi‑isometry for hyperbolic groups). For general hyperbolic groups, the problem is open; there is no known algorithm, and it is not known whether it is undecidable. The subgroup membership problem (given a finite set of words generating a subgroup H and a word w, decide whether w ∈ H) is solvable for quasiconvex subgroups (using the fact that quasiconvex subgroups are themselves hyperbolic and the inclusion is a quasi‑isometric embedding). For arbitrary finitely generated subgroups, the problem is undecidable in general hyperbolic groups (Mihailova’s construction embeds the word problem of a finitely presented group into the membership problem of a subgroup of a hyperbolic group). The generalized word problem (membership in a given subgroup) is therefore undecidable in general, but decidable for quasiconvex subgroups. The conjugacy problem for subgroups (decide whether two finitely generated subgroups are conjugate) is also undecidable in general. The orbit decidability problem (given a subgroup H and an element g, decide whether the H‑orbit of g contains a given element) is equivalent to the membership problem for the stabilizer. For hyperbolic groups, the subgroup distortion function can be arbitrarily large (even recursive), but quasiconvex subgroups are undistorted. The automorphism group of a hyperbolic group is not necessarily hyperbolic; its algorithmic properties are largely unknown. The outer automorphism group of a torsion‑free hyperbolic group is known to be finitely generated (Paul–Sela), but its word problem is open. The Sela’s algorithm for the elementary theory of free groups (and more generally torsion‑free hyperbolic groups) uses the Makanin–Razborov diagrams to decide the first‑order theory, but the isomorphism problem remains a key obstacle.

The Geometry of Groups Acting on Buildings and the Bruhat–Tits Fixed Point Theorem

Buildings are simplicial complexes that generalize the notion of a symmetric space to groups over arbitrary fields. A thick building of type (W,S) carries a chamber system on which a group G acts strongly transitively if the action is transitive on pairs consisting of an apartment and a chamber inside it. The Bruhat–Tits fixed point theorem states that any group of automorphisms of a complete building with a bounded orbit fixes a point in the geometric realization. This result is the building analogue of the Cartan fixed point theorem for CAT(0) spaces and underlies the structure theory of reductive algebraic groups over local fields.

When G is a reductive group over a non‑archimedean local field, its Bruhat–Tits building is a locally finite CAT(0) complex on which G acts properly and cocompactly on the set of chambers. The fixed point theorem implies that any compact subgroup of G fixes a vertex, which yields the existence of maximal compact subgroups and the Iwasawa decomposition. Moreover, the theorem provides a geometric proof of the conjugacy of maximal split tori: the apartments correspond to maximal split tori, and the transitivity of the action on apartments translates into conjugacy of the associated tori.

In the context of geometric group theory, buildings serve as model spaces for groups with a BN‑pair. The Davis complex of a Coxeter group is a building when the Coxeter system is right‑angled, and the fixed point theorem extends to actions on such complexes. This connection allows one to transfer rigidity results from buildings to a broader class of groups acting on CAT(0) cube complexes, for instance the Haglund–Wise special cube complexes that embed into right‑angled Artin groups.

The theorem also has algorithmic consequences. For a group acting properly on a locally finite building, the word problem can be solved by constructing a normal form based on the gallery distance in the building. The fellow‑traveler property holds for the associated automatic structure, giving a quadratic Dehn function. These algorithmic features are exploited in the study of arithmetic groups and their lattices.

Finally, the Bruhat–Tits fixed point theorem is a cornerstone in the proof of the Margulis superrigidity theorem for higher rank lattices. The building provides a boundary at infinity whose dynamics encode the algebraic structure of the lattice, and the fixed point property forces any measurable cocycle to be cohomologous to a homomorphism. This geometric input is essential for the classification of irreducible lattices in semisimple groups of rank at least two.

The Coarse Geometry of Braid Groups and Mapping Class Groups

The braid group B_n on n strands admits a natural geometric model as the mapping class group of a disk with n punctures. Its Cayley graph with respect to the standard Artin generators is quasi‑isometric to the configuration space of n distinct points in the plane, equipped with the Euclidean metric. This quasi‑isometry reveals that B_n has exponential growth and contains free subgroups of rank two for n ≥ 3, a fact first proved by Birman and Lubotzky using the action on the curve complex of the punctured disk.

The curve complex C(S) of a finite‑type surface S is a flag complex whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves, with simplices spanned by pairwise disjoint curves. Masur and Minsky proved that C(S) is Gromov hyperbolic, and the mapping class group Mod(S) acts on it by isometries. The action is acylindrical, which places Mod(S) in the class of acylindrically hyperbolic groups. This geometric perspective yields a linear isoperimetric inequality for the word problem in Mod(S) and a quadratic Dehn function for the braid groups.

Subgroup distortion in braid groups is well understood: the pure braid subgroup P_n is undistorted, while the center generated by the full twist is exponentially distorted. The distortion of a subgroup can be read off from the geometry of its orbit in the curve complex. For instance, a pseudo‑Anosov element acts loxodromically on C(S), and its cyclic subgroup is undistorted; a reducible element fixes a multicurve and its centralizer is a product of mapping class groups of the complementary subsurfaces, leading to polynomial distortion of certain abelian subgroups.

The coarse geometry of Mod(S) also controls its large‑scale invariants. The asymptotic dimension of Mod(S) is finite and equals the complexity of the surface, a result of Bestvina, Bromberg, and Fujiwara using the hierarchical hyperbolic structure. This finite asymptotic dimension implies the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture for Mod(S) and yields the Novikov conjecture for its group C*‑algebra. Moreover, the existence of a proper affine isometric action on a Hilbert space (the Haagerup property) is known for mapping class groups of genus zero surfaces but remains open for higher genus.

Algorithmic applications are abundant. The word problem in B_n is solvable in quadratic time via the Garside normal form, which is a geometric normal form derived from the lattice structure of the positive braid monoid. The conjugacy problem is also decidable, with a polynomial‑time algorithm using the ultra‑summit set. For Mod(S), the word problem is solvable in polynomial time using the hierarchy machinery of Masur–Minsky, and the conjugacy problem is decidable by the work of Mosher. These algorithms are direct consequences of the hyperbolicity of the curve complex and the hierarchical hyperbolic structure.

The full twist Δ2 equals (σ1 σ2)3. In the Cayley graph of B3, the word length of Δ2k with respect to {σ1,σ2} is at most 6k because (σ1 σ2)3k is a word of length 6k representing Δ2k. Since the inclusion of the cyclic subgroup ⟨Δ2⟩ into B3 is a quasi‑isometric embedding if and only if the word length grows linearly, the linear upper bound 6k together with the linear lower bound from the curve complex shows that the word length of Δ2k is Θ(k). Cite the curve complex dimension: the three‑punctured disk has a tree as curve complex, while higher complexity surfaces give hyperbolic curve complexes where the full twist acts with logarithmic translation length. This distinction is a frequent source of partial credit loss.

The Isoperimetric Profile and Higher Dimensional Dehn Functions

The classical Dehn function measures the difficulty of filling loops in the Cayley complex of a finitely presented group. Higher dimensional Dehn functions, introduced by Gromov, generalize this notion to filling k‑cycles by (k+1)‑chains. For a group G of type Fk+1, the k‑dimensional Dehn function δ^k_G(n) records the minimal volume of a (k+1)‑chain whose boundary is a given k‑cycle of volume at most n. These functions are quasi‑isometry invariants and capture the large‑scale geometry of the group’s Eilenberg–MacLane space.

For hyperbolic groups, all higher Dehn functions are linear, reflecting the fact that the Cayley complex is a CAT(−1) space after suitable thickening. In contrast, for CAT(0) groups the first Dehn function is quadratic, while higher Dehn functions can be polynomial of higher degree. The work of Young and others shows that for right‑angled Artin groups the k‑dimensional Dehn function is exactly nk+1 when the defining flag complex has non‑trivial homology in dimension k. This precise computation links the algebraic topology of the flag complex to the geometric filling invariants of the group.

The isoperimetric profile of a group is the function that assigns to each volume v the minimal possible boundary size of a region of volume v. For groups of polynomial growth, the isoperimetric profile is of the form v(d-1)/d where d is the degree of growth, a result of Varopoulos and Coulhon–Saloff‑Coste. For groups with exponential growth, the profile can be linear (hyperbolic groups) or sublinear but super‑polynomial (certain solvable groups). The profile determines the return probability of random walks and the spectrum of the Laplacian on the Cayley graph.

Higher Dehn functions also control the algorithmic complexity of the word problem in higher dimensions. The k‑dimensional word problem asks whether a given k‑cycle in the Cayley complex bounds a (k+1)‑chain of bounded volume. For automatic groups, the first Dehn function is quadratic and the higher Dehn functions are at most exponential. For groups with a combing that satisfies a higher dimensional fellow‑traveler property, all higher Dehn functions are polynomial. These algorithmic bounds are used in the study of the homological finiteness properties of subgroups.

Recent advances connect higher Dehn functions to the geometry of asymptotic cones. A group has polynomial k‑dimensional Dehn function if and only if its asymptotic cones are simply connected up to dimension k. This characterization, due to Drutu and Sapir, translates filling questions into the language of ultralimits and allows the use of model‑theoretic tools. It also yields a new proof of Gromov’s theorem on groups of polynomial growth: if all higher Dehn functions are polynomial, the asymptotic cones are nilpotent Lie groups, forcing the group to be virtually nilpotent.

The Theory of Group Actions on R‑trees and the Rips Machine

An R‑tree is a metric space in which any two points are joined by a unique arc isometric to a real interval. Groups acting on R‑trees arise naturally as limits of actions on hyperbolic spaces after rescaling the metric. The Rips machine, developed by Bestvina and Feighn, analyzes stable actions of finitely presented groups on R‑trees by decomposing the tree into a graph of actions with elementary vertex stabilizers. This decomposition is the analogue of the JSJ decomposition for 3‑manifolds and provides a canonical way to understand splittings of the group.

A stable action is one where arc stabilizers are finitely generated and do not properly contain each other along a descending chain. The Rips machine shows that any stable action on an R‑tree can be reduced to a finite graph of actions where each vertex action is either trivial, axial (the group acts by translations on a line), or of surface type (the group is a surface group acting on the dual tree of a measured foliation). This classification yields a powerful tool for proving that certain groups do not act non‑trivially on R‑trees, for instance groups with Kazhdan’s property (T).

The theory has deep connections with the boundary of hyperbolic groups. The Gromov boundary of a hyperbolic group can be equipped with a visual metric, and the action of the group on its boundary extends to an action on the space of measured geodesic currents. The space of projective measured currents is homeomorphic to the space of projectivized length functions on the group, which in turn is the boundary of the outer space of the free group. The Rips machine provides a combinatorial description of the limit points of sequences of length functions, linking the dynamics on the boundary to splittings of the group.

Algorithmic applications include the decidability of the isomorphism problem for torsion‑free hyperbolic groups with a given JSJ decomposition. Sela’s algorithm uses the Rips machine to analyze the canonical JSJ decomposition of a hyperbolic group over virtually cyclic subgroups. The machine also underlies the proof that the elementary theory of a free group is decidable: the Makanin–Razborov diagrams encode all homomorphisms from a finitely generated group into a free group, and the Rips machine controls the limiting actions that appear in the construction of these diagrams.

Beyond hyperbolic groups, the Rips machine has been extended to relatively hyperbolic groups by Dahmani and to groups acting on CAT(0) spaces by Kapovich and Leeb. In the CAT(0) setting, the machine analyzes the structure of the Tits boundary and yields a rank‑rigidity theorem for groups with a geometric action on a CAT(0) space without Euclidean de Rham factor. These extensions demonstrate the versatility of the Rips machine as a unifying framework for understanding splittings across a wide range of geometric group actions.

L²‑Betti Numbers, the Atiyah Conjecture, and the Geometry of Groups

L²‑Betti numbers are von Neumann algebraic invariants of a group G defined as the dimensions of the L²‑homology of the universal cover of a classifying space BG with respect to the group von Neumann algebra. They are quasi‑isometry invariants and vanish for amenable groups, a theorem of Cheeger and Gromov. For a group acting properly and cocompactly on a contractible CW‑complex X, the L²‑Betti numbers of G equal the L²‑Betti numbers of the complex X, providing a bridge between geometric actions and analytic invariants.

The Atiyah conjecture predicts that for a torsion‑free group G, the L²‑Betti numbers are integers. More generally, for a group with finite torsion, the L²‑Betti numbers are rational numbers with denominators bounded by the least common multiple of the orders of finite subgroups. The conjecture is known for many classes: free groups, surface groups, right‑angled Artin groups, and more generally for groups that are limit groups over free groups. It fails for certain groups with exotic torsion, such as the Higman group, where L²‑Betti numbers can be irrational.

The L²‑Betti numbers are intimately related to the cost of a group, a measure‑theoretic invariant introduced by Gaboriau. For a free ergodic measure‑preserving action of G on a probability space, the cost equals 1 plus the sum of the L²‑Betti numbers of G. This equality, proved by Gaboriau, implies that groups with fixed price 1 (e.g., higher rank lattices) have vanishing first L²‑Betti number. Conversely, groups with positive first L²‑Betti number have cost >1 and admit free actions with infinitely many generators.

In the context of the Baum–Connes conjecture, the L²‑Betti numbers appear as the indices of certain elliptic operators on the classifying space for proper actions. The conjecture predicts that the assembly map from the equivariant K‑homology of the classifying space to the K‑theory of the reduced group C*‑algebra is an isomorphism. For groups satisfying the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients, the L²‑Betti numbers can be computed via the trace on the group von Neumann algebra, and the Atiyah conjecture becomes a statement about the rationality of the trace of spectral projections of the Laplacian.

Recent work of Lück and others has extended the theory to the setting of L²‑invariants for quantum groups and for groups acting on buildings. The L²‑Betti numbers of a building are determined by the Coxeter diagram and the thickness parameters, and they satisfy a product formula for join buildings. These computations provide explicit examples where the Atiyah conjecture can be tested, and they reveal new connections between the combinatorics of Coxeter systems and the analytic invariants of the associated groups.

The Geometry of Thompson's Groups F, T, and V

Thompson's groups F, T, and V are finitely presented infinite groups of piecewise linear homeomorphisms of the unit interval [0,1] (for F) and the circle S1 (for T and V), with slopes powers of 2 and breakpoints at dyadic rationals. Group F consists of orientation-preserving homeomorphisms of [0,1] fixing endpoints; T extends this to the circle by identifying endpoints; V allows discontinuous "cut-and-paste" maps on the Cantor set. All three are finitely presented: F = ⟨x0, x1 | x1x0 = x2, x1x02 = x3⟩ where xn+1 = xnx0. The group F is torsion-free, has exponential growth, and contains no non-abelian free subgroups — it is a candidate for a non-amenable group without free subgroups, though amenability of F remains a major open problem. The group T and V are simple (Brinkmann, 1994; Brown, 1987). Geometrically, F acts on an infinite-dimensional CAT(0) cube complex (the Stein complex) whose vertices correspond to dyadic subdivisions of [0,1]; this action is proper but not cocompact. The Dehn function of F is quadratic (Guba, Sapir), while T and V have linear Dehn functions. The asymptotic dimension of F is infinite (Brodskiy, Dydak, Lang), contrasting with hyperbolic groups. The group F embeds in the diffeomorphism group of the circle but not in any linear group over a field of characteristic zero (Brin, Squier). The Brin-Thompson groups nV generalize V to the n-dimensional Cantor set and provide a rich family of simple, finitely presented groups with diverse geometric properties.

The Coarse Geometry of Solvable Groups and the Milnor-Wolf Theorem

The Milnor-Wolf theorem (1968) states that a finitely generated solvable group has polynomial growth if and only if it is virtually nilpotent; otherwise it has exponential growth. This dichotomy is sharp: there are no finitely generated solvable groups of intermediate growth. For a polycyclic group G (a solvable group with all subquotients cyclic), the growth degree is given by the Bass-Guivarc'h formula: if G has Hirsch length h and the derived series quotients G(i)/G(i+1) have ranks ri, then the growth degree is Σ i·ri. The group is virtually nilpotent exactly when this sum equals h. The geometry of solvable groups is captured by their action on the associated Lie group via the Mal'cev completion. Every polycyclic group embeds as a lattice in a simply connected solvable Lie group S, and the word metric on G is quasi-isometric to a left-invariant metric on S. The exponential growth of non-nilpotent solvable groups arises from the presence of expanding eigenvalues in the adjoint action. The lamplighter group ℤ ≀ ℤ = (⊕ℤ ℤ/2ℤ) ⋊ ℤ is a metabelian group of exponential growth with a quadratic Dehn function; its Cayley graph is quasi-isometric to the Diestel-Leader graph DL(2,2), a horocyclic product of two trees. The wreath product ℤ ≀ ℤd has growth type exp(nd/(d+1)) (Erschler), showing that solvable groups can have growth strictly between polynomial and exponential in the sense of growth functions, though the Milnor-Wolf theorem concerns the dichotomy of polynomial vs exponential growth rates. The isoperimetric profile of solvable groups of exponential growth is typically sublinear but super-polynomial; for the lamplighter group it is ≍ n/log n. The coarse geometry of solvable groups is rigid: any group quasi-isometric to a polycyclic group is virtually polycyclic (Farb-Mosher, Eskin-Fisher-Whyte). The classification of solvable Lie groups up to quasi-isometry reduces to the classification of their nilradicals and the eigenvalues of the acting torus.

Worked Problem: Growth Degree of a Polycyclic Group
Let G be the group of upper triangular 3×3 integer matrices with 1s on the diagonal (the discrete Heisenberg group H₃(ℤ)). Compute its growth degree using the Bass-Guivarc'h formula and verify it matches the known polynomial growth of degree 4.
The Heisenberg group H₃(ℤ) has presentation ⟨x, y, z | [x, y] = z, [x, z] = [y, z] = 1⟩. Its lower central series is G¹ = G, G² = ⟨z⟩ ≅ ℤ, G³ = {1}. The quotients are G/G² ≅ ℤ² (rank r₁ = 2) and G²/G³ ≅ ℤ (rank r₂ = 1). Hirsch length h = r₁ + r₂ = 3. Bass-Guivarc'h formula: growth degree = Σ i·rᵢ = 1·r₁ + 2·r₂ = 1·2 + 2·1 = 4. If the group is a wreath product A ≀ B with A finite and B infinite, expect exponential growth with sublinear isoperimetric profile. The lamplighter group ℤ/2ℤ ≀ ℤ is the canonical example — know its Dehn function (quadratic), growth (exponential), and asymptotic dimension (infinite). For metabelian groups, the Dehn function is at most quadratic; if it is linear, the group is virtually abelian.

The Geometry of Coxeter Groups and the Davis Complex

A Coxeter group W is defined by a presentation ⟨S | (sisj)mij = 1⟩ where mii = 1 and mij ∈ {2,3,…,∞} for i ≠ j. The Coxeter diagram has vertices S and edges labeled mij ≥ 3 (unlabeled for 3, omitted for 2). Moussong's theorem (1988) states that every Coxeter group acts properly and cocompactly by isometries on a CAT(0) cell complex, the Davis complex ΣW, constructed by gluing copies of a fundamental chamber (a convex polytope) according to the Coxeter diagram. The Davis complex is CAT(0) if and only if the Coxeter group is "Moussong", which holds for all Coxeter groups. The geometry of W is encoded in the nerve L, a flag complex whose vertices are S and simplices correspond to finite special subgroups. The group W is hyperbolic if and only if L contains no induced 4-cycle and no infinite Euclidean subdiagram (Moussong's criterion). The visual boundary of ΣW is the Davis complex of the "hyperbolic" part of W. Right-angled Coxeter groups (RACGs), where mij ∈ {2,∞}, are fundamental: every Coxeter group virtually embeds in a RACG (Davis-Januszkiewicz). RACGs are precisely the fundamental groups of non-positively curved cube complexes (the Salvetti complex). The growth series of a Coxeter group is rational (Steinberg, 1968): Σw∈W tℓ(w) = 1/W(t) where W(t) = ΣJ⊆S, |WJ|<∞ (-1)|J| / Πi∈J (1-tmi). The growth rate is the reciprocal of the smallest positive root of the denominator polynomial. Coxeter groups satisfy the Tits alternative (Tits, 1972): they either contain a non-abelian free subgroup or are virtually abelian. The virtual cohomological dimension of W equals the dimension of the Davis complex, which is the maximal size of a spherical subset of S minus 1.

The Geometry of Amalgamated Free Products and HNN Extensions

Bass-Serre theory describes groups acting on trees via graphs of groups. An amalgamated free product G = A ∗C B and an HNN extension G = ⟨A, t | t-1ct = φ(c) ∀c∈C⟩ correspond to graphs of groups with one edge. The Bass-Serre tree T has vertex stabilizers conjugate to A, B (or A for HNN) and edge stabilizers conjugate to C. The action is without edge inversions. The combination theorems of Bestvina-Feighn (1992) and Dahmani (2003) give conditions under which the fundamental group of a graph of hyperbolic groups is hyperbolic: the edge groups must be quasi-convex in the vertex groups, and the inclusion maps must satisfy a "flaring" condition (no long sequences of edges with bounded projection). For CAT(0) groups, the Bridson-Haefliger combination theorem requires the edge groups to be convex in the vertex groups and the gluing maps to be isometries. The geometry of the resulting group is reflected in the geometry of the tree of spaces: the universal cover is a tree of copies of the universal covers of the vertex spaces, glued along the universal covers of the edge spaces. The Dehn function of an amalgamated product can be much larger than those of the factors: if A and B are hyperbolic and C is quasi-convex, the Dehn function is quadratic; if C is distorted, the Dehn function can be exponential (Brady, 1999). The subgroup distortion in amalgamated products is controlled by the distortion of the edge groups in the vertex groups. The acylindrical accessibility theorem (Dunwoody, Bestvina-Feighn) states that a finitely presented group has a finite graph of groups decomposition with finite or virtually cyclic edge groups and at most one-ended vertex groups. The JSJ decomposition over virtually cyclic subgroups (Rips-Sela, Bowditch, Fujiwara-Papasoglu) provides a canonical splitting encoding all splittings over virtually cyclic groups. For relatively hyperbolic groups, the combination theorems of Dahmani and Osin require the edge groups to be parabolic or relatively quasi-convex.

The Geometry of Groups Acting on Hyperbolic Spaces and the Classification of Elements

Let G act by isometries on a proper geodesic hyperbolic space X. Every element g ∈ G falls into one of three types: elliptic (fixes a point in X), loxodromic (acts as a translation on a quasi-geodesic axis, with positive translation length τ(g) = limn→∞ d(x, gnx)/n > 0), or parabolic (fixes exactly one point on the boundary ∂X and has zero translation length). For a proper action on a proper hyperbolic space, parabolic elements cannot exist; all infinite-order elements are loxodromic. The translation length satisfies τ(gn) = |n|·τ(g) and τ(hgh-1) = τ(g). The set of loxodromic elements is conjugation-invariant. The North-South dynamics on ∂X: a loxodromic g has an attracting fixed point g+ and a repelling fixed point g-; for any open neighborhoods U+ of g+ and U- of g-, there exists N such that gn(∂X U-) ⊂ U+ for all n ≥ N. The action is elementary if the limit set (accumulation points of an orbit in ∂X) has cardinality ≤ 2; otherwise it is non-elementary. A non-elementary action contains a loxodromic element (Tits alternative for hyperbolic spaces). The action is acylindrical if for every ε > 0 there exist R, N such that for any x, y with d(x, y) ≥ R, the number of g ∈ G with d(x, gx) ≤ ε and d(y, gy) ≤ ε is at most N. Acylindrical hyperbolicity (Osin, 2016) means G acts acylindrically on some hyperbolic space with unbounded orbits. Mapping class groups, Out(Fn), and non-elementary relatively hyperbolic groups are acylindrically hyperbolic. The classification of elements extends to relatively hyperbolic groups: an element is elliptic if it is conjugate into a peripheral subgroup, loxodromic if it acts loxodromically on the coned-off graph, and parabolic if it is infinite-order and elliptic but not conjugate into a peripheral subgroup (impossible in the standard definition). The growth of conjugacy classes of loxodromic elements is exponential for non-elementary acylindrically hyperbolic groups.

The Geometry of Lattices in Higher Rank Semisimple Lie Groups and Margulis Superrigidity

Let G be a connected semisimple Lie group with finite centre and no compact factors, and let Γ < G be an irreducible lattice. When the real rank of G is at least two, the geometry of the symmetric space X = G/K (where K is a maximal compact subgroup) forces strong algebraic rigidity on Γ. The Margulis Superrigidity Theorem (1975) states that any linear representation ρ : Γ → GLn(ℝ) with Zariski‑dense image extends uniquely to a continuous homomorphism ρ̃ : G → GLn(ℝ). Consequently, every such representation factors through the ambient Lie group, and the lattice inherits the representation theory of G. A corollary is the Margulis Normal Subgroup Theorem: any normal subgroup of Γ is either finite or of finite index. This dichotomy implies that irreducible higher‑rank lattices are virtually simple modulo their centres.

The geometric input is the existence of a CAT(0) symmetric space of non‑positive curvature on which G acts by isometries. The higher rank condition provides flats of dimension ≥ 2, and the Flat Torus Theorem guarantees that any abelian subgroup of rank ≥ 2 stabilises a flat. Using the dynamics of the G‑action on the Furstenberg boundary G/P (where P is a minimal parabolic), Margulis proved that any measurable Γ‑equivariant map from the boundary to a projective space is essentially algebraic. This boundary map is the key to extending representations. The theorem fails for rank‑one groups (e.g. SO(n,1), SU(n,1)) where lattices admit many deformations (Teichmüller space, bending constructions). Superrigidity also underpins the arithmeticity theorem: every irreducible lattice in a higher‑rank semisimple Lie group is arithmetic (Margulis, 1975). The proof combines superrigidity with the Borel–Harish‑Chandra theorem on finiteness of covolume for arithmetic subgroups.

Applications extend to rigidity of group actions. Zimmer’s programme asks which groups can act by diffeomorphisms on compact manifolds. For higher‑rank lattices, any volume‑preserving action on a compact manifold of dimension less than the rank of G is essentially trivial (Zimmer, 1980s). The geometric mechanism is the same: the existence of higher‑rank flats forces invariant measures on the boundary, which then constrain the action. In the context of geometric group theory, superrigidity provides a quasi‑isometric classification: any group quasi‑isometric to a higher‑rank lattice is virtually a lattice in the same Lie group (Kleiner–Leeb, 1997; Eskin–Farb, 1997). This quasi‑isometric rigidity is a cornerstone of the programme to classify groups up to quasi‑isometry.

The Geometry of Arithmetic Groups and the Congruence Subgroup Property

An arithmetic group is a subgroup of GLn(ℚ) commensurable with GLn(ℤ). More generally, for a semisimple algebraic group G defined over a number field k, the group G(OS) of S‑integral points (where OS is the ring of S‑integers) is an arithmetic lattice in the product of completions ∏v∈S G(kv). The Congruence Subgroup Property (CSP) asks whether every finite‑index subgroup of G(OS) contains a principal congruence subgroup G(OS, I) = ker(G(OS) → G(OS/I)) for some non‑zero ideal I ⊂ OS. For higher‑rank groups (e.g. SLn with n ≥ 3, Sp2n with n ≥ 2), the CSP holds (Bass–Milnor–Serre, 1967; Raghunathan, 1976). For rank‑one groups such as SL2(ℤ) or SL2(OK) with K an imaginary quadratic field, the CSP fails dramatically: there are many non‑congruence subgroups (e.g. the classical modular group has uncountably many finite‑index subgroups not containing any principal congruence subgroup).

The geometric proof of CSP for higher‑rank groups uses the action on the associated Bruhat–Tits building (for p‑adic factors) or symmetric space (for archimedean factors). The building is a CAT(0) simplicial complex on which the arithmetic group acts with finite stabilisers. The key is the existence of a Mayer–Vietoris sequence for the group cohomology with coefficients in the Steinberg module, which is built from the spherical building at infinity. The vanishing of H1 of the arithmetic group with coefficients in the Steinberg module (proved using the Solomon–Tits theorem on the homology of spherical buildings) implies that every finite‑index subgroup is detected by congruence conditions. In rank one, the building is a tree, and the Steinberg module is the space of ends; its cohomology does not vanish, allowing non‑congruence subgroups.

The CSP has profound consequences for the profinite completion Γ̂ of an arithmetic group Γ. When CSP holds, the natural map Γ̂ → ∏v∈S G(Ov) has finite kernel, so the profinite completion is essentially the product of the p‑adic completions. This yields strong constraints on the finite quotients of Γ and on its representation growth. For example, the number of irreducible representations of dimension ≤ n grows polynomially (Larsen–Lubotzky, 2008). In contrast, for SL2(ℤ) the profinite completion is much larger, and representation growth is exponential. The CSP also interacts with the Ramanujan conjecture for automorphic forms: the spectral gap of the Laplacian on the locally symmetric space Γ\X is related to the expansion properties of the Cayley graphs of the finite quotients Γ/Γ(I). When CSP holds, these graphs form a family of expanders (Margulis, 1973; Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak, 1988).

Worked Example: Detecting Congruence Subgroups in SL₃(ℤ)
Show that the subgroup H = { A ∈ SL₃(ℤ) : A ≡ I (mod 2) } is a congruence subgroup of level 2, and argue why every finite‑index subgroup of SL₃(ℤ) contains some H(I) = ker(SL₃(ℤ) → SL₃(ℤ/Iℤ)).
The map φ₂ : SL₃(ℤ) → SL₃(ℤ/2ℤ) is a homomorphism because reduction mod 2 respects matrix multiplication and determinant. Its kernel is exactly H, so H is a principal congruence subgroup of level 2. Since SL₃ has real rank 2, the Congruence Subgroup Property holds (Bass–Milnor–Serre). Therefore any finite‑index subgroup K ≤ SL₃(ℤ) contains some principal congruence subgroup H(I). The proof uses the action of SL₃(ℤ) on the Bruhat–Tits building for SL₃(ℚₚ) (a 2‑dimensional simplicial complex) and the vanishing of H¹ with coefficients in the Steinberg module, which forces K to be detected by congruence conditions modulo some integer I.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks about the Congruence Subgroup Property, first identify the rank of the algebraic group. For rank ≥ 2, CSP holds (cite Bass–Milnor–Serre, Raghunathan). For rank 1, CSP typically fails (give SL₂(ℤ) as counterexample). Link the geometric reason: higher‑rank buildings have spherical buildings at infinity with vanishing reduced homology, while trees have non‑trivial H⁰ of the space of ends. This distinction is a frequent source of short‑answer marks.

The Geometry of Bounded Cohomology, Quasi‑morphisms, and Stable Commutator Length

Bounded cohomology H•b(G; ℝ) is the cohomology of the complex of bounded cochains on a group G. The comparison map c : H•b(G; ℝ) → H•(G; ℝ) measures which ordinary cohomology classes admit bounded representatives. In degree two, the kernel of c is isomorphic to the space of homogeneous quasi‑morphisms QH(G) modulo homomorphisms. A quasi‑morphism is a map φ : G → ℝ such that |φ(gh) − φ(g) − φ(h)| ≤ D(φ) for some defect D(φ). Homogenisation φ̄(g) = limn→∞ φ(gn)/n yields a homogeneous quasi‑morphism (satisfying φ̄(gn) = nφ̄(g)). The vector space QH(G) is infinite‑dimensional for non‑elementary hyperbolic groups (Epstein–Fujiwara, 1997) and for mapping class groups (Bestvina–Fujiwara, 2002).

The stable commutator length (scl) of an element g ∈ [G,G] is defined as scl(g) = limn→∞ cl(gn)/n, where cl(x) is the minimal number of commutators whose product equals x. Bavard’s Duality Theorem (1991) states that scl(g) = ½ supφ∈QH(G) φ(g)/D(φ). This duality connects the algebraic notion of commutator length with the analytic space of quasi‑morphisms. For hyperbolic groups, scl takes values in (1/12)ℤ (Calegari–Fujiwara, 2010) and the set of values is well‑ordered. In free groups, scl is rational with denominator bounded by the length of the word (Calegari, 2009). The geometry enters via the interpretation of scl as the minimal genus of a surface mapping to a K(G,1) with boundary representing g; this is a filling problem in the Cayley complex.

Bounded cohomology also classifies actions on the circle. The Euler class of a representation ρ : G → Homeo+(S1) lives in H2b(G; ℤ). For hyperbolic groups, every such action either has a finite orbit or is semi‑conjugate to a Fuchsian representation (Ghys, 1987). The space of quasi‑morphisms on a hyperbolic group is large enough to separate conjugacy classes, which implies that the comparison map in degree two is surjective for many groups (e.g. mapping class groups, Out(Fn)). The theory of scl has algorithmic applications: computing scl in free groups reduces to a linear programming problem on the space of admissible surfaces (Calegari’s scallop algorithm).

The Geometry of Groups Acting on the Circle and the Theory of Left‑Orderable Groups

A group G is left‑orderable if there exists a total order ≺ invariant under left multiplication: g ≺ h ⇒ kg ≺ kh for all k ∈ G. Equivalently, G embeds into Homeo+(ℝ) (or Homeo+(S1) with a fixed point). The space of left orders LO(G) is a compact, totally disconnected space on which G acts by conjugation. For countable groups, LO(G) is either empty or a Cantor set. A fundamental theorem of Burns–Hale (1972) states that G is left‑orderable iff every finitely generated subgroup admits a non‑trivial homomorphism to a left‑orderable group. This reduces the problem to finitely generated subgroups.

Groups acting faithfully on the circle by orientation‑preserving homeomorphisms are precisely the subgroups of Homeo+(S1). The rotation number rot(f) ∈ ℝ/ℤ of an element f ∈ Homeo+(S1) is a homogeneous quasi‑morphism. For a group G acting on S1, the map g ↦ rot(ρ(g)) is a homomorphism if the action lifts to ℝ; otherwise it is a quasi‑morphism with defect ≤ 1 (Poincaré). The Euler class e(ρ) ∈ H2(G; ℤ) measures the obstruction to lifting. For left‑orderable groups, the Euler class vanishes in bounded cohomology, which implies the existence of many quasi‑morphisms (Calegari, 2004).

Notable examples: braid groups Bn are left‑orderable (Dehornoy, 1994) via the Dehornoy order, which is defined using the action on the set of braids. The fundamental group of any closed 3‑manifold that fibres over the circle with pseudo‑Anosov monodromy is left‑orderable (Boyer–Rolfsen–Wiest, 2005). Conversely, groups with Property (T) (e.g. SLn(ℤ) for n ≥ 3) are not left‑orderable because any action on S1 has a finite orbit (Witte Morris, 2006). The L‑space conjecture in 3‑manifold topology predicts that an irreducible rational homology 3‑sphere is an L‑space iff its fundamental group is not left‑orderable. This connects the geometric notion of left‑orderability with Heegaard Floer homology.

The Geometry of Expander Graphs, Property (τ), and the Lubotzky–Weiss Criterion

A family of finite k‑regular graphs {Xi} is an expander family if there exists ε > 0 such that the Cheeger constant h(Xi) ≥ ε for all i. Equivalently, the spectral gap λ1(Xi) of the normalised Laplacian is bounded below by a positive constant. For a finitely generated group Γ with a descending chain of finite‑index normal subgroups Γ = Γ0 ⊵ Γ1 ⊵ … with ∩i Γi = {1}, the Schreier graphs Sch(Γ/Γi, S) form an expander family iff Γ has Property (τ) with respect to that chain. Property (τ) means that the trivial representation is isolated in the unitary dual of Γ restricted to representations that factor through some Γ/Γi. This is weaker than Kazhdan’s Property (T), which requires isolation in the full unitary dual.

The Lubotzky–Weiss criterion (1993) gives a combinatorial characterisation: a sequence of finite k‑regular graphs is an expander family iff the diameter grows logarithmically in the number of vertices, i.e. diam(Xi) ≤ C log |V(Xi)| for some constant C. This links expansion to the geometry of the Cayley graphs of the finite quotients. For arithmetic groups with the Congruence Subgroup Property, the congruence quotients G(O/I) yield expander graphs (Margulis, 1973; Lubotzky–Phillips–Sarnak, 1988 gave explicit Ramanujan graphs from SL2(ℤ[i])). The Ramanujan property means the non‑trivial eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix satisfy |λ| ≤ 2√(k−1), which is the optimal spectral gap for k‑regular graphs (Alon–Boppana bound).

Property (τ) has strong consequences for the profinite completion Γ̂. If Γ has Property (τ) with respect to all finite‑index subgroups, then Γ̂ has a spectral gap, which implies that Γ has finite abelianisation growth and that the first ℓ2‑Betti number of Γ vanishes (Lubotzky, 1994). For lattices in higher‑rank Lie groups, Property (T) implies Property (τ) for any chain of congruence subgroups. For SL2(ℤ), which lacks Property (T), Selberg’s eigenvalue conjecture (proved for congruence subgroups by Selberg, 1965) gives a spectral gap 3/16 for the Laplacian on the modular surface, which translates to Property (τ) for the congruence chain. However, SL2(ℤ) does not have Property (τ) with respect to all finite‑index subgroups because of the existence of non‑congruence subgroups with small eigenvalues (Gamburd, 2002). The distinction between congruence and non‑congruence expanders is a central theme in the theory of thin groups (groups of infinite index in arithmetic groups that still produce expanders, e.g. the Apollonian group).

  • Confusing Property (τ) with Property (T): Property (T) is a global representation‑theoretic condition, while Property (τ) only concerns representations factoring through a given chain of finite quotients. A group can have Property (τ) for its congruence quotients but not Property (T) (e.g. SL₂(ℤ)).
  • Assuming every finite‑index subgroup of an arithmetic group is a congruence subgroup: this is exactly the Congruence Subgroup Property, which holds only for higher‑rank groups. For rank‑one arithmetic groups (SL₂ over number fields) there are many non‑congruence subgroups.
  • Thinking that bounded cohomology vanishes for all hyperbolic groups: H²_b is infinite‑dimensional for non‑elementary hyperbolic groups, and the comparison map is not injective. The space of homogeneous quasi‑morphisms is a large quotient of H²_b.
  • Equating left‑orderability with the existence of a faithful action on ℝ: a left‑orderable group embeds in Homeo⁺(ℝ), but the converse requires the action to have no global fixed point. A group acting on ℝ with a fixed point need not be left‑orderable (e.g. ℤ² acting by translations on ℝ with a fixed point is impossible, but actions with fixed points can be trivial).
  • Believing that all expander families come from arithmetic groups: there are explicit constructions of expanders from non‑arithmetic groups (e.g. zig‑zag product, Ramanujan graphs from PGL₂ over finite fields) and from thin groups (infinite‑index subgroups of arithmetic groups that still have spectral gap).
  • Overlooking the role of the Steinberg module in the proof of CSP: the vanishing of H¹(Γ; St) is the cohomological heart of the argument, and it relies on the spherical building at infinity being a union of apartments with the homotopy type of a wedge of spheres.
  • Misapplying Bavard duality: the supremum is taken over all homogeneous quasi‑morphisms, not just those with defect 1. The defect D(φ) appears in the denominator, so scaling a quasi‑morphism does not change the ratio φ(g)/D(φ).

The Geometry of Groups with Property (RD) and Rapid Decay

Property (RD), introduced by Haagerup in 1979 and later refined by Jolissaint, is a quantitative weakening of Kazhdan's Property (T). A finitely generated group Γ with word length |·| has Property (RD) if there exists a polynomial P such that for every finitely supported function f : Γ → ℂ the operator norm of the convolution operator λ(f) on ℓ²(Γ) satisfies ‖λ(f)‖ ≤ P(‖f‖₂), where ‖f‖₂ is the ℓ²‑norm weighted by the length function. Equivalently, the group algebra ℂ[Γ] embeds continuously into the reduced C*‑algebra C*_r(Γ) with a polynomial control. Groups with Property (RD) include all hyperbolic groups (Jolissaint, 1990), all groups acting properly on a CAT(0) cube complex with a uniform bound on the dimension of cubes (Ramagge–Robertson–Steger, 2015), and all lattices in semisimple Lie groups of real rank one (Lafforgue, 2002). The property is preserved under free products with amalgamation over finite subgroups and under HNN extensions with finite associated subgroups (Jolissaint, 1990).

Rapid decay is the analytic manifestation: the matrix coefficients of the regular representation decay faster than any polynomial when restricted to the length‑spheres. This has striking consequences for the Baum–Connes assembly map. For a group with Property (RD) and the Haagerup property, the assembly map is injective on K‑theory (Lafforgue, 2002). Moreover, Property (RD) implies that the group has finite asymptotic dimension if it also has finite decomposition complexity (Guentner–Higson–Weinberger, 2005). The polynomial P can be taken of degree d/2 where d is the topological dimension of a proper CAT(0) space on which the group acts geometrically (Ji–Ramagge, 2016).

Key examples: SL₂(ℤ) has Property (RD) (Jolissaint, 1990) but not Property (T). The free group F_n has Property (RD) with polynomial of degree 1 (Haagerup, 1979). The Grigorchuk group, despite intermediate growth, fails Property (RD) because its ℓ²‑spectral radius does not admit polynomial control (Bartholdi–Erschler, 2012). For lattices in higher rank Lie groups, Property (RD) is unknown in general; it holds for uniform lattices in Sp(n,1) and F₄(−20) (Lafforgue, 2002) but is open for SL_n(ℤ) with n ≥ 3.

The Geometry of Groups with Strong Property (T) and Lafforgue's Theorem

Strong Property (T), introduced by Lafforgue in 2008, strengthens Kazhdan's Property (T) by requiring a uniform spectral gap for all unitary representations on Banach spaces of non‑trivial type. A group Γ has Strong Property (T) if there exists a constant κ > 0 such that for every unitary representation π on a Banach space B of type p ∈ (1,2] without invariant vectors, the operator norm of the averaging operator over a symmetric generating set S satisfies ‖(1/|S|) Σs∈S π(s)‖ ≤ 1 − κ. This condition implies Property (T) and Property (RD) simultaneously. Lafforgue proved that all lattices in higher rank semisimple Lie groups (real rank ≥ 2) have Strong Property (T). In particular, SL_n(ℤ) for n ≥ 3 and Sp2n(ℤ) for n ≥ 2 satisfy Strong Property (T). The proof uses the geometry of the associated Bruhat–Tits building and a sophisticated induction on the rank of parabolic subgroups.

Consequences are far‑reaching. Strong Property (T) yields a quantitative version of the Novikov conjecture: the assembly map in L‑theory is split injective for groups with Strong Property (T) (Lafforgue, 2008). It also implies that the group has no non‑trivial amenable actions on measure spaces (Bader–Furman–Sauer, 2013). For expander graphs, Strong Property (T) gives a uniform spectral gap for the Laplacian on the Schreier graphs of any chain of finite‑index subgroups, not only congruence subgroups (Lubotzky–Weiss, 1993). The constant κ can be made explicit for SL_n(ℤ) using the work of Kassabov (2007) on symmetric generating sets of bounded size. However, Strong Property (T) fails for rank‑one lattices such as SL₂(ℤ) and for hyperbolic groups (Ozawa, 2010). It is also known that a group with Strong Property (T) cannot be coarsely embeddable into a Hilbert space (Lafforgue, 2008), providing a large class of counterexamples to the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture.

Worked Example: Verifying Property (RD) for a Hyperbolic Group
Let Γ = ⟨a,b | a² = b³ = (ab)⁷ = 1⟩ be the (2,3,7) triangle group, which is a cocompact lattice in PSL₂(ℝ) and hence hyperbolic. Show that Γ has Property (RD) with a polynomial of degree at most 1.

Step 1: Recognise that Γ acts geometrically on the hyperbolic plane ℍ². The word metric with respect to the generating set {a,b} is quasi‑isometric to the hyperbolic distance.

Step 2: By a theorem of Jolissaint (1990), every hyperbolic group has Property (RD). The proof constructs a length function L and shows that the convolution operator norm is bounded by a polynomial in the ℓ²‑norm. For hyperbolic groups the polynomial can be taken of degree 1 because the volume growth of balls is exponential, but the length function satisfies a linear isoperimetric inequality.

Step 3: Explicitly, for any finitely supported f : Γ → ℂ, one has ‖λ(f)‖ ≤ C (‖f‖₂ + ‖L f‖₂) where L(g) = |g| is the word length. Since ‖L f‖₂ ≤ maxg∈supp f |g| · ‖f‖₂, the bound is linear in the support radius, giving a polynomial of degree 1.

Conclusion: Γ has Property (RD) with polynomial P(t) = C t.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks to distinguish Property (T), Property (RD), and Strong Property (T), remember the hierarchy: Strong Property (T) ⇒ Property (T) and Property (RD); Property (T) does not imply Property (RD) (e.g. SL₃(ℤ) has Property (T) but Property (RD) is unknown); Property (RD) does not imply Property (T) (e.g. free groups). Cite the key names: Kazhdan (1967) for Property (T), Haagerup (1979) and Jolissaint (1990) for Property (RD), Lafforgue (2008) for Strong Property (T). A short table comparing the three properties earns full marks.

The Geometry of Groups Acting on Hyperbolic Buildings and the Bourdon Building

Hyperbolic buildings are higher‑dimensional analogues of trees, introduced by Bourdon (1997) and further developed by Haglund–Paulin (1998). A hyperbolic building is a CAT(−1) polygonal complex whose links are spherical buildings of type Āq−1 or Ĉq−1. The most studied example is the Bourdon building Ip,q, a regular right‑angled hyperbolic building whose apartments are the hyperbolic plane tiled by regular right‑angled p‑gons, with q polygons meeting at each vertex. The automorphism group Aut(Ip,q) is a locally compact group acting properly and cocompactly on the building. When p ≥ 6 and q ≥ 3, the building is a CAT(−1) space, and its boundary at infinity is a Cantor set.

Groups acting geometrically on hyperbolic buildings include certain uniform lattices in Aut(Ip,q) constructed by Bourdon–Pajot (1999) and by Haglund–Wise (2008) using small cancellation theory. These groups are hyperbolic in the sense of Gromov and have Property (RD) (Jolissaint, 1990). Their boundaries are homeomorphic to the Menger curve when p = 6, q = 4 (Bourdon–Kleiner, 2000). The visual metric on the boundary is Ahlfors regular of dimension log(q−1)/log(p−2). This provides a rich supply of hyperbolic groups with prescribed conformal dimension.

The geometry of the building yields strong rigidity results. Bourdon–Pajot (1999) proved that any quasi‑isometry between two Bourdon buildings Ip,q and Ip',q' forces p = p' and q = q'. Moreover, the quasi‑isometry group of Ip,q is commensurable with Aut(Ip,q). For lattices Γ ≤ Aut(Ip,q), the Mostow‑type rigidity holds: any isomorphism between two such lattices extends to an isomorphism of the ambient automorphism groups (Haglund–Paulin, 1998). These groups also serve as counterexamples to the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture when they fail Property (RD) (Lafforgue, 2002).

The Geometry of Groups Acting on Right‑Angled Artin Groups and the Extension Graph

Right‑angled Artin groups (RAAGs) are defined by a finite simplicial graph Γ: generators correspond to vertices, and commutation relations correspond to edges. The Salvetti complex provides a CAT(0) cube complex on which the RAAG acts freely and cocompactly. The extension graph Γ^e, introduced by Kim–Koberda (2013), has vertices the conjugacy classes of standard generators, with edges joining vertices that commute in the RAAG. This graph is quasi‑isometric to the contact graph of the associated CAT(0) cube complex and encodes the coarse geometry of the RAAG.

Key results: The extension graph is hyperbolic (Kim–Koberda, 2013) and its Gromov boundary is homeomorphic to the space of ending laminations on the Salvetti complex. The action of the RAAG on Γ^e is acylindrical (Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara, 2015), which implies that the RAAG is acylindrically hyperbolic unless it is a direct product of a free abelian group and a finite group. The stable commutator length (scl) of elements in a RAAG can be computed via the geometry of Γ^e (Calegari–Walker, 2015). Moreover, the quasi‑isometry classification of RAAGs reduces to the isomorphism classification of their defining graphs up to certain moves (Behrstock–Charney, 2011).

Applications: The extension graph provides a uniform framework for studying subgroup distortion in RAAGs. For a subgroup H ≤ A_Γ, the distortion function is polynomial of degree at most the rank of the centraliser of H (Hagen–Sisto, 2016). The graph also yields a new proof of the Tits alternative for RAAGs: every subgroup either contains a non‑abelian free subgroup or is virtually abelian (Charney–Davis, 1995). Finally, the extension graph is central to the proof that the outer automorphism group Out(A_Γ) is hierarchically hyperbolic (Durham–Hagen–Sisto, 2017).

  • Trap 1: Confusing Property (RD) with rapid decay of matrix coefficients on the group itself; Property (RD) concerns the operator norm of convolution on ℓ²(Γ), not pointwise decay.
  • Trap 2: Assuming Strong Property (T) holds for all lattices in semisimple Lie groups; it is known only for real rank ≥ 2.
  • Trap 3: Thinking that every hyperbolic building is a Bourdon building; there are many other types, e.g. buildings of type Ĉq−1.
  • Trap 4: Believing the extension graph of a RAAG is always a tree; it is hyperbolic but generally not a tree unless the defining graph is a join of cliques.
  • Trap 5: Overlooking that Property (RD) for hyperbolic groups uses a length function quasi‑isometric to the word metric, not the word metric itself.
  • Trap 6: Stating that Lafforgue's Strong Property (T) implies the Baum–Connes conjecture; it implies the conjecture with coefficients for the groups that have it, but the full conjecture remains open for many groups.
  • Trap 7: Confusing the visual metric on the boundary of a hyperbolic building with the standard metric on the Cantor set; the visual metric is Ahlfors regular with a non‑integer Hausdorff dimension.

These advanced topics illustrate how analytic, geometric, and combinatorial methods intertwine in modern geometric group theory.

Bass–Serre Theory and Graphs of Groups

Bass–Serre theory provides the foundational dictionary between groups acting on trees and algebraic decompositions of groups as graphs of groups. A graph of groups consists of a connected graph, a vertex group assigned to each vertex, an edge group assigned to each edge, and injective homomorphisms from each edge group into the vertex groups at its endpoints. The fundamental group of a graph of groups is constructed by taking the free product of the vertex groups amalgamated along the edge groups, together with stable letters that encode the graph’s cycles. The central theorem states that a group acts on a tree without edge inversions if and only if it is isomorphic to the fundamental group of a graph of groups. The quotient graph of groups is obtained by collapsing each orbit of vertices and edges; the vertex and edge stabilisers become the vertex and edge groups.

Key consequences include the structure theorem for groups acting on trees: any such group splits as an amalgamated free product or an HNN extension over a subgroup that fixes an edge. This yields a geometric proof of the Kurosh subgroup theorem for free products and of the Grushko decomposition theorem. The theory also gives a precise description of the action on the Bass–Serre tree: vertices correspond to cosets of vertex groups, edges to cosets of edge groups, and the group acts by left multiplication. The tree is uniquely determined up to equivariant isomorphism by the graph of groups.

Applications abound. For a finitely generated group, the existence of a non‑trivial splitting over a finite subgroup is equivalent to having more than one end (Stallings’ theorem). The theory underlies the JSJ decomposition of finitely presented groups over virtually cyclic subgroups, which is a canonical splitting that captures all essential splittings. In the context of hyperbolic groups, the Bestvina–Feighn combination theorem uses Bass–Serre trees to build new hyperbolic groups from pieces that are hyperbolic relative to the edge groups. The theory also provides the language for the study of group actions on ℝ‑trees via the Rips machine, where the limiting tree is analysed through its transverse covering by simplicial trees.

Computational aspects: given a finite presentation, one can algorithmically construct a graph of groups decomposition over finite or virtually cyclic subgroups when it exists (Dahmani–Guirardel–Osin). The complexity of the decomposition is measured by the number of vertices and edges, and by the ranks of the vertex groups. The theory also informs the study of subgroup distortion: a subgroup that is elliptic in the Bass–Serre tree (fixes a vertex) is undistorted, while a hyperbolic subgroup (acts with a translation axis) can be exponentially distorted.

The Coarse Baum–Connes Conjecture and Counterexamples

The coarse Baum–Connes conjecture, formulated by Yu in 2000, asserts that for a bounded geometry metric space X the coarse assembly map from the coarse K‑homology of X to the K‑theory of the Roe algebra C*(X) is an isomorphism. When X is a finitely generated group equipped with a word metric, the conjecture implies the Novikov conjecture on homotopy invariance of higher signatures for the group. Groups with Property A (Yu, 2000) satisfy the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture; this includes all amenable groups and all hyperbolic groups. The conjecture is also known for groups that coarsely embed into Hilbert space (Yu, 2000) and for linear groups over fields of characteristic zero (Guoliang Yu, 2005).

Counterexamples were first constructed by Gromov (2003) using expander graphs. He showed that a disjoint union of expanders, equipped with the metric that makes each component an expander and distances between components infinite, does not satisfy the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture. Later, Higson–Lafforgue–Skandalis (2002) proved that the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture fails for certain groups that contain a sequence of expanders as a coarse subspace. In particular, Gromov’s random groups at density > 1/3 contain expanders and thus provide finitely generated groups that are counterexamples. Lafforgue (2002) strengthened this by showing that some of these groups also fail the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients, and that they have Strong Property (T), which obstructs the coarse assembly map.

The failure of the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture is closely tied to the existence of ghost projections in the Roe algebra. A ghost operator is a bounded operator on ℓ²(X) whose matrix entries tend to zero uniformly at infinity. On spaces with Property A, every ghost operator is compact, which forces the assembly map to be injective. On expander sequences, there exist non‑compact ghost projections, which produce non‑trivial elements in the kernel of the assembly map. The quantitative version of the conjecture, involving the quantitative K‑theory of Oyono‑Oyono and Yu, refines the statement by controlling the propagation of operators.

Recent work by Willett and Yu (2017) introduced the notion of geometric Property (T) for metric spaces, which provides a uniform obstruction to the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture. They showed that a sequence of expanders has geometric Property (T), and that any space with geometric Property (T) fails the conjecture. This gives a clean geometric criterion that applies to many groups, including certain lattices in higher rank Lie groups that do not have Property (T) themselves but contain expander sequences in their Cayley graphs.

Worked Example: Detecting Failure of the Coarse Baum–Connes Conjecture
Let Γ be a finitely generated group that contains a sequence of finite subgraphs {X_n} which form an expander family with respect to the word metric. Show that Γ does not satisfy the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture.
  1. Recall that a family of finite graphs {X_n} is an expander family if there exists ε > 0 such that for every n, the Cheeger constant h(X_n) ≥ ε.
  2. By a theorem of Higson–Lafforgue–Skandalis (2002), if a bounded geometry metric space contains a coarse disjoint union of an expander family, then the coarse assembly map μ: KX_*(X) → K_*(C*(X)) is not injective.
  3. Since Γ contains the expander family {X_n} as a coarse subspace, the inclusion map induces a map on coarse K‑homology that is injective on the summand generated by the expanders.
  4. The Roe algebra of Γ contains the Roe algebra of the disjoint union of the X_n as a subalgebra. The ghost projection constructed on the expander union (a non‑compact operator with uniformly small matrix entries) remains a ghost projection in C*(Γ).
  5. This ghost projection represents a non‑zero class in K0(C*(Γ)) that lies in the kernel of the coarse assembly map, because its image under the dual map to K‑homology is zero (the propagation can be made arbitrarily small).
  6. Therefore μ is not injective, and the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture fails for Γ.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture, first identify whether the group or space has Property A, coarsely embeds into Hilbert space, or contains an expander sequence. Property A ⇒ conjecture holds; expander sequence ⇒ conjecture fails. Remember that Gromov’s random groups at density > 1/3 are the standard source of finitely generated counterexamples. Cite the Higson–Lafforgue–Skandalis theorem for the implication “expander ⇒ failure”.

Asymptotic Dimension, Property A, and the Novikov Conjecture

Asymptotic dimension, introduced by Gromov in 1993, is a coarse analogue of covering dimension. A metric space X has asymptotic dimension ≤ n if for every R > 0 there exists a uniformly bounded cover of X with multiplicity ≤ n+1 such that each set in the cover has diameter bounded by a function of R. Groups of finite asymptotic dimension include hyperbolic groups (Buyalo–Lebedeva, 2005), mapping class groups (Bell–Fujiwara, 2008), and fundamental groups of non‑positively curved manifolds. The asymptotic dimension of a group is a quasi‑isometry invariant.

Yu’s Property A, defined in 2000, is a weak amenability condition at the coarse level. A discrete metric space X has Property A if for every ε > 0 and R > 0 there exists a map ξ: X → ℓ¹(X) with ‖ξ(x)‖₁ = 1, support of ξ(x) contained in the R‑ball around x, and ‖ξ(x) − ξ(y)‖₁ < ε whenever d(x,y) ≤ R. Equivalently, X admits a sequence of almost invariant, finitely supported probability measures. Amenable groups have Property A; hyperbolic groups have Property A (Yu, 2000); groups that coarsely embed into Hilbert space have Property A. Property A implies the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture (Yu, 2000) and hence the Novikov conjecture for the group.

The Novikov conjecture states that the higher signatures of a closed oriented manifold are homotopy invariants. For a group Γ, the conjecture is equivalent to the rational injectivity of the assembly map in K‑theory of the group C*-algebra. The coarse Baum–Connes conjecture for the classifying space BΓ implies the Novikov conjecture for Γ. Thus any group with finite asymptotic dimension (which implies Property A by a result of Guentner–Kaminker, 2004) satisfies the Novikov conjecture. This covers a vast class of groups, including all linear groups over fields of characteristic zero (by work of Ji, 2005) and all groups acting properly on finite‑dimensional CAT(0) cube complexes (by work of Wright, 2012).

Quantitative refinements: the asymptotic dimension of a group can be bounded by the dimension of a model space on which it acts. For example, a group acting properly on an n‑dimensional CAT(0) cube complex has asymptotic dimension ≤ n. The asymptotic dimension of a hyperbolic group is bounded by the topological dimension of its Gromov boundary plus one (Buyalo–Lebedeva). These bounds are often sharp; for instance, the mapping class group of a genus g surface has asymptotic dimension 4g−5 (Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara, 2015). The interplay between asymptotic dimension, Property A, and the Novikov conjecture remains a central theme in coarse geometry.

The Morse Boundary and Contracting Elements in CAT(0) Groups

The Morse boundary, introduced by Charney and Sultan in 2015, provides a quasi‑isometry invariant boundary for any proper geodesic metric space, generalising the Gromov boundary of hyperbolic spaces. A geodesic ray is Morse if every (λ,ε)‑quasi‑geodesic with endpoints on the ray stays within a bounded neighbourhood of the ray, where the bound depends only on λ and ε. The Morse boundary ∂ₘX is the set of equivalence classes of Morse rays, equipped with a topology that makes it a metrisable space when X is proper. For hyperbolic spaces, every geodesic is Morse, so ∂ₘX coincides with the Gromov boundary.

In CAT(0) spaces, Morse geodesics are exactly the rank‑one geodesics: those that do not bound a flat half‑plane. An isometry of a CAT(0) space is called contracting if it has a Morse axis. The set of contracting elements in a group acting geometrically on a CAT(0) space is closely related to the dynamics on the Morse boundary. Cashen and Mackay (2019) showed that the Morse boundary of a proper CAT(0) space is a quasi‑isometry invariant and that the action of a group on its Morse boundary is a convergence action if and only if the group is acylindrically hyperbolic.

Key results: For a right‑angled Artin group A_Γ, the Morse boundary is homeomorphic to the space of ending laminations on the Salvetti complex (Charney–Sultan). For mapping class groups, the Morse boundary coincides with the subspace of the Thurston boundary consisting of arational measured foliations (Cashen–Mackay). The Morse boundary of a relatively hyperbolic group is the Bowditch boundary with the parabolic points removed. The topology of the Morse boundary can be wild; for instance, the Morse boundary of a CAT(0) space with isolated flats is a Cantor set.

Applications to group structure: If a group acts geometrically on a CAT(0) space and contains a contracting element, then the group is either virtually cyclic or acylindrically hyperbolic (Sisto, 2018). The existence of a rank‑one element in a CAT(0) group implies the group has a non‑trivial Morse boundary, which yields a non‑elementary convergence action. This provides a geometric criterion for acylindrical hyperbolicity that is often easier to verify than the original definition. The Morse boundary also plays a role in the study of the Poisson boundary of random walks on CAT(0) groups (Kaimanovich, 2019).

Quasi‑trees and the Manning–Bowditch Construction

Quasi‑trees are metric spaces that are quasi‑isometric to a tree. They appear naturally as the coned‑off graphs of relatively hyperbolic groups, as the contact graphs of CAT(0) cube complexes, and as the graphs of hierarchically hyperbolic spaces. Manning (2005) and Bowditch (2013) independently developed a construction that, given a group G acting on a hyperbolic space X and a collection of quasiconvex subgroups, produces a quasi‑tree on which G acts acylindrically. The vertices of the quasi‑tree correspond to cosets of the peripheral subgroups, and edges encode intersections of these cosets.

The Manning–Bowditch quasi‑tree is built by taking the hyperbolic space X, coning off each peripheral coset to a point, and then taking the resulting graph. The key property is that the action of G on this quasi‑tree is acylindrical: for any ε > 0 there exist R, N such that the set of group elements moving two points at distance ≥ R by ≤ ε has cardinality ≤ N. This construction provides a uniform proof that relatively hyperbolic groups are acylindrically hyperbolic (unless they are virtually cyclic or parabolic). It also yields a quasi‑tree model for the action of the mapping class group on the curve complex, where the peripheral subgroups are the stabilisers of multicurves.

Applications: The quasi‑tree gives a geometric model for the relative Dehn function of a relatively hyperbolic group. The distance in the quasi‑tree between two peripheral cosets is coarsely equal to the length of a shortest relative geodesic connecting them. This leads to a linear relative isoperimetric inequality. The construction also underlies the proof that the outer automorphism group of a free group Out(F_n) acts acylindrically on the free splitting complex (Bestvina–Feighn, 2014). In the setting of hierarchically hyperbolic groups, the quasi‑tree associated to a domain is used to define the projection maps that satisfy the Behrstock inequality.

Algorithmic consequences: The quasi‑tree can be used to solve the word problem in relatively hyperbolic groups in quadratic time (Dahmani–Guirardel, 2011). The acylindrical action provides a uniform bound on the number of short elements in a given conjugacy class, which is essential for the algorithmic classification of subgroups up to conjugacy. The Manning–Bowditch construction thus bridges the geometric, dynamical, and algorithmic aspects of relative hyperbolicity.

  • Assuming that Property A is equivalent to amenability; there are non‑amenable groups with Property A (e.g., hyperbolic groups).
  • Thinking that asymptotic dimension is a homotopy invariant; it is only a quasi‑isometry invariant.
  • Believing that every CAT(0) group has a non‑empty Morse boundary; groups that are virtually abelian or that act on a product of trees without rank‑one elements have empty Morse boundary.
  • Stating that the Manning–Bowditch quasi‑tree is always a tree; it is only quasi‑isometric to a tree, and its hyperbolicity constant depends on the quasiconvexity constants of the peripheral subgroups.
  • Overlooking that the coarse assembly map can be an isomorphism even when the space does not have Property A; there are spaces without Property A that still satisfy the coarse Baum–Connes conjecture (e.g., certain groups with finite asymptotic dimension but no Property A are not known).
  • Confusing the Morse boundary with the contracting boundary of Charney–Murray; they coincide for proper CAT(0) spaces but differ in general metric spaces.

The Geometry of Outer Space and the Lipschitz Metric

Outer space, introduced by Culler and Vogtmann in 1986, is a contractible, finite‑dimensional simplicial complex on which the outer automorphism group Out(Fn) acts properly discontinuously with finite stabilisers. A point of Outer space is a homotopy equivalence class of a marked metric graph of rank n and total volume 1; the marking is a homotopy equivalence from a fixed rose Rn to the graph. The simplicial structure comes from collapsing edges of length zero, and the topology is the quotient of the space of all such marked graphs.

The Lipschitz metric dL on Outer space is defined asymmetrically: for two points X, Y the distance dL(X,Y) is the logarithm of the infimum of Lipschitz constants of maps f: X → Y that respect the markings. This metric is not symmetric, but it is a geodesic metric in the sense that for any X, Y there exists a directed geodesic from X to Y. The action of Out(Fn) is by isometries for dL. The asymmetry reflects the fact that stretching a graph in one direction is easier than compressing it in the opposite direction.

Key geometric features include:

  • The space is not CAT(0); however, it admits a natural non‑positively curved structure in the sense of the asymmetric metric, and the directed geodesics are unique up to reparameterisation.
  • Translation lengths of elements φ ∈ Out(Fn) are given by the logarithm of the stretch factor λ(φ) (the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of a train‑track representative). For a fully irreducible φ, the axis is a directed line on which φ acts by translation.
  • The Lipschitz metric is closely related to the free splitting complex and the free factor complex; the projection maps to these hyperbolic complexes are coarsely Lipschitz and are used to prove that Out(Fn) is not relatively hyperbolic for n ≥ 3.
  • Algorithmic applications: the Lipschitz metric gives a quadratic‑time algorithm for the word problem in Out(Fn) (Francaviglia–Martino, 2011) and a method to compute the translation length of an automorphism from a train‑track map.

Recent work of Algom‑Kfir, Bestvina–Feighn, and Handel has shown that the asymmetric metric space has a well‑defined horofunction boundary, which coincides with the space of projective classes of very small actions on ℝ‑trees. This boundary provides a compactification that is Out(Fn)‑equivariant and is used to study the dynamics of Out(Fn) on the space of trees.

The Geometry of Groups Acting on the Space of Projective Measured Laminations and the Thurston Boundary

For a closed orientable surface S of genus g ≥ 2, the Teichmüller space Teich(S) is the space of marked hyperbolic structures on S. Thurston compactified Teich(S) by the space of projective measured laminations PML(S), which is homeomorphic to a sphere of dimension 6g−7. The mapping class group Mod(S) acts on Teich(S) by isometries for the Teichmüller metric, and this action extends continuously to the Thurston boundary.

The geometry of PML(S) is governed by the intersection form i(·,·): PML(S) × PML(S) → ℝ≥0, which is continuous and homogeneous. A measured lamination λ is a closed subset of S foliated by geodesics together with a transverse measure; its projective class [λ] ∈ PML(S) forgets the overall scale. The action of Mod(S) on PML(S) is minimal and ergodic with respect to the natural Lebesgue measure class.

Classification of mapping classes via boundary dynamics:

  • Periodic elements act with finite order on PML(S) and have no fixed points in the interior of Teich(S).
  • Reducible elements preserve a multicurve; they fix the projective classes of the curves in the multicurve and act on the complementary subsurfaces.
  • Pseudo‑Anosov elements φ have exactly two fixed points in PML(S): the stable lamination λ+ and the unstable lamination λ−. The action on PML(S) is north‑south dynamics: for any open neighbourhoods U+ of λ+ and U− of λ−, there exists N such that φn(PML(S) U−) ⊂ U+ for all n ≥ N.

The stretch factor λ(φ) > 1 is the exponential growth rate of the length of curves under iteration; it equals the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of the transition matrix of a train‑track map representing φ. The stable and unstable laminations are uniquely ergodic and fill the surface.

The Thurston boundary is also the visual boundary of the Teichmüller metric (which is an asymmetric Finsler metric). The work of Masur and Minsky on the hierarchy of curve complexes uses the projection of Teichmüller geodesics to the curve complex, and the ending lamination theorem of Brock–Canary–Minsky identifies the ends of hyperbolic 3‑manifolds with points in PML(S).

Identifying pseudo‑Anosov dynamics on the Thurston boundary
Let φ be a pseudo‑Anosov mapping class on a closed surface of genus 2 with stretch factor λ > 1. Describe the action of φ on the space of projective measured laminations PML(S) and explain how the stable and unstable laminations determine the translation length of φ in the Teichmüller metric.
The action of φ on PML(S) has exactly two fixed points: the stable lamination λ+ and the unstable lamination λ−. For any projective measured lamination [μ] ≠ λ−, the iterates φn([μ]) converge to λ+ as n → +∞; similarly φ−n([μ]) → λ−. This north‑south dynamics characterises pseudo‑Anosov elements. The translation length of φ in the Teichmüller metric is log λ, where λ is the stretch factor. Indeed, a Teichmüller geodesic axis for φ is determined by the pair (λ−, λ+), and φ translates along this axis by distance log λ per iteration. The stretch factor is the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue of any train‑track representative of φ, and it equals the exponential growth rate of the intersection number i(φn(α), β) for any filling pair of curves α, β.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks to distinguish mapping class types, recall that only pseudo‑Anosov elements exhibit north‑south dynamics on the Thurston boundary with exactly two fixed points. Periodic elements have finite orbits; reducible elements fix a multicurve but do not act with north‑south dynamics on the whole boundary. The stretch factor λ > 1 is the translation length in the Teichmüller metric, and log λ is the minimal displacement of φ on Teich(S).

The Geometry of Artin Groups and the Deligne Complex

Artin groups AΓ are defined by a Coxeter matrix M = (mij) with mii = 1 and mij ∈ {2,3,…,∞} for i ≠ j. The presentation has generators si and relations of the form sisjsi… = sjsisj… with mij factors on each side. When all mij ∈ {2,∞} the group is a right‑angled Artin group (RAAG); the general case includes braid groups (type An) and many other families.

The Deligne complex (also called the Salvetti complex for RAAGs) is a simplicial complex constructed from the Coxeter complex of the associated Coxeter group WΓ. Its vertices correspond to cosets of standard parabolic subgroups that are finite; simplices correspond to chains of such cosets. The fundamental group of the Deligne complex is AΓ, and the complex is a classifying space K(AΓ,1) whenever the K(π,1) conjecture holds. The conjecture is known for:

  • All right‑angled Artin groups (the Salvetti complex is a non‑positively curved cube complex).
  • Artin groups of FC type (Charney–Davis, 1995) and of large type (mij ≥ 3 for all i ≠ j) (Cumplido–Gebhardt–González‑Meneses–Wiener, 2019).
  • Many low‑dimensional cases and all Artin groups of rank ≤ 3.

Geometric properties of the Deligne complex:

  • For RAAGs the complex is CAT(0) and the group acts geometrically on a CAT(0) cube complex; this yields biautomaticity, solvable word and conjugacy problems, and the Haagerup property.
  • For general Artin groups the complex carries a piecewise Euclidean metric that is locally CAT(0) only in special cases. The complex is contractible whenever the K(π,1) conjecture holds, but non‑positive curvature is not guaranteed.
  • The center of an Artin group is infinite cyclic when the Coxeter graph is irreducible and not of affine type; the generator of the center corresponds to the Garside element in the associated Garside monoid (for spherical type).

Algorithmic consequences: the Deligne complex provides a combinatorial model for the word problem via van Kampen diagrams. For Artin groups of FC type the word problem is solvable in quadratic time using the normal form of the Garside structure. The conjugacy problem is open in general but solved for several classes using the geometry of the complex and the theory of cyclically reduced words.

The Geometry of Groups with the Haagerup Property and Weak Amenability

A locally compact group G has the Haagerup property (or a‑T‑menability) if it admits a proper affine isometric action on a Hilbert space. Equivalently, there exists a sequence of continuous positive‑definite functions φn: G → ℂ that converge pointwise to 1 and vanish at infinity. This property is a strong negation of Kazhdan’s Property (T): a group with Property (T) has the Haagerup property only if it is compact.

Important classes of groups with the Haagerup property include:

  • Amenable groups (the regular representation gives a proper action on ℓ2(G)).
  • Free groups and more generally groups acting properly on CAT(0) cube complexes (Chatterji–Druţu–Haglund, 2010).
  • Coxeter groups (Bożejko–Januszkiewicz–Spatzier, 1988).
  • Groups acting properly on trees (via the boundary action).
  • Many lattices in rank‑one Lie groups (e.g., SO(n,1), SU(n,1)).

Weak amenability, introduced by Cowling and Haagerup, means there exists a sequence of Herz–Schur multipliers un on G such that un → 1 pointwise and the completely bounded norms ‖un‖cb are uniformly bounded. The infimum of such bounds is the Cowling–Haagerup constant Λcb(G). Weak amenability does not imply the Haagerup property, nor vice versa:

  • Simple Lie groups of real rank ≥ 2 (e.g., SL(3,ℝ)) have Property (T) and fail both.
  • The group Sp(n,1) (n ≥ 2) has weak amenability with Λcb = 2n−1 but does not have the Haagerup property.
  • Certain lattices in Sp(n,1) inherit weak amenability but not the Haagerup property.
  • Free groups have both properties with Λcb = 1.

The Haagerup property implies the Baum–Connes conjecture with coefficients (Higson–Kasparov, 2001). Weak amenability is a key ingredient in the proof of the Baum–Connes conjecture for groups acting on trees (Haagerup–Kraus) and for Sp(n,1) (Lafforgue). The geometry behind these properties is encoded in conditionally negative definite kernels: a proper affine isometric action on Hilbert space corresponds to a proper conditionally negative definite function ψ: G → ℝ≥0. For weak amenability one studies the Schur product of the kernel e−tψ with the group algebra.

The Geometry of Groups Acting on the Space of Quadratic Differentials and the Teichmüller Metric

The Teichmüller space Teich(S) of a Riemann surface S of genus g ≥ 2 can be identified with the space of marked conformal structures. Its tangent space at a point X is naturally isomorphic to the space of holomorphic quadratic differentials Q(X) on X. The Teichmüller metric is an asymmetric Finsler metric defined by the norm ‖q‖T = supα (∫α |q|) / ExtX(α)1/2, where the supremum runs over all simple closed curves α and ExtX(α) is the extremal length of α on X.

Geodesics in the Teichmüller metric are given by Teichmüller maps: for a quadratic differential q, the map ft stretches the horizontal foliation of q by et and contracts the vertical foliation by e−t. The distance dT(X,Y) = ½ log K, where K is the minimal dilatation of a quasiconformal map in the homotopy class of the marking. The mapping class group Mod(S) acts by isometries on (Teich(S), dT).

Key geometric features:

  • The Teichmüller metric is not Riemannian but is a Busemann non‑positively curved metric: geodesic triangles satisfy the CAT(0) inequality for the comparison triangles in the Euclidean plane, although the space is not a CAT(0) space because the metric is asymmetric.
  • The boundary of Teich(S) in the Teichmüller metric can be identified with the space of projective measured foliations PMF(S), which is homeomorphic to the Thurston boundary PML(S) via the natural correspondence between measured foliations and measured laminations.
  • The SL(2,ℝ) action on the space of quadratic differentials (the Teichmüller flow) preserves the Teichmüller metric and is ergodic with respect to the Masur–Veech measure. This flow is central to the study of billiards in rational polygons and interval exchange transformations.
  • The work of Masur and Minsky on the hierarchy of curve complexes uses the projection of Teichmüller geodesics to the curve complex C(S). The distance in C(S) between the projections of two points is coarsely equal to the length of the thick part of the Teichmüller geodesic connecting them.

Comparison with the Weil–Petersson metric: the Weil–Petersson metric is a Kähler metric with negative sectional curvature but incomplete; its completion adds strata corresponding to noded Riemann surfaces. The Teichmüller metric is complete and has a different asymptotic geometry; the two metrics are not quasi‑isometric. The geometry of quadratic differentials also underlies the construction of the Gardiner–Masur boundary and the Thurston compactification.

  • Confusing the asymmetric Lipschitz metric on Outer space with a symmetric metric; the directed distance is not reversible and geodesics are directed.
  • Assuming the Thurston boundary is a manifold; it is a sphere of dimension 6g−7 but carries a natural piecewise linear structure only after choosing a train‑track atlas.
  • Believing that all Artin groups have a CAT(0) Deligne complex; non‑positive curvature is known only for RAAGs, FC type, and large type, and is open in general.
  • Thinking that the Haagerup property implies weak amenability; Sp(n,1) provides a counterexample (weak amenability without Haagerup).
  • Equating the Teichmüller metric with the Weil–Petersson metric; they have different curvature properties, completeness, and boundary behaviour.
  • Overlooking that the stretch factor of a pseudo‑Anosov element is the translation length in the Teichmüller metric, not in the Weil–Petersson metric.
  • Assuming the mapping class group acts properly discontinuously on the Thurston boundary; the action is minimal and has dense orbits, so it is not properly discontinuous.

These advanced geometric models illustrate how group actions on metric spaces encode algebraic properties and algorithmic decidability.

The Geometry of the Weil–Petersson Metric and its Completion

The Weil–Petersson metric is a Kähler metric on Teichmüller space Teich(S) defined by the L2 inner product on harmonic Beltrami differentials. Unlike the Teichmüller metric, it has negative sectional curvature everywhere, but it is incomplete: geodesics can leave the space in finite time by pinching curves to zero length. The completion, identified by Masur (1976) and Wolpert (1985), is the Deligne–Mumford compactification of the moduli space Mg. Strata correspond to noded Riemann surfaces where a collection of disjoint simple closed curves has been pinched; each stratum is a product of lower‑dimensional Teichmüller spaces with a Weil–Petersson metric.

Wolpert proved that the Weil–Petersson metric has sectional curvature bounded between −C and −c < 0 on the thick part of moduli space, and that the metric is geodesically convex on the thick part. The completion is a CAT(0) space in the sense of Alexandrov, although the metric is only a metric space completion, not a Riemannian manifold. The mapping class group Mod(S) acts by isometries on the completion, and the action extends continuously to the boundary strata. This action is not properly discontinuous on the whole completion because stabilizers of boundary points are infinite (they contain Dehn twists about the pinched curves).

Key applications include Brock’s proof (2003) that the Weil–Petersson volume of moduli space grows like (4π2)3g−3 (3g−3)! and Mirzakhani’s recursion for Weil–Petersson volumes via integration over the boundary strata. The metric is also central to the study of the Weil–Petersson geodesic flow, which is ergodic with respect to the Liouville measure (Burns–Masur–Wilkinson, 2010). The Weil–Petersson metric is not quasi‑isometric to the Teichmüller metric; their asymptotic geometries differ dramatically, for instance the Weil–Petersson metric has finite diameter on the thick part while the Teichmüller metric has infinite diameter.

Important references: Wolpert, “On the Weil–Petersson geometry of the moduli space of curves” (1985); Masur, “Extension of the Weil–Petersson metric” (1976); Brock, “Weil–Petersson volumes and the geometry of moduli space” (2003); Mirzakhani, “Simple geodesics and Weil–Petersson volumes” (2007).

The Geometry of the Curve Graph and its Hyperbolicity

The curve graph C(S) of a finite‑type surface S has vertices the isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves, with an edge between two vertices when the curves can be realized disjointly. Masur and Minsky (1999) proved that C(S) is Gromov hyperbolic for all surfaces of complexity ξ(S) = 3g−3+n ≥ 2. The hyperbolicity constant depends only on the topological type of S; explicit bounds were later given by Bowditch (2008) and by Hensel, Przytycki, and Webb (2015). The mapping class group Mod(S) acts on C(S) by isometries, and this action is acylindrical: for any ε > 0 there exist R, N such that for any two vertices at distance ≥ R, at most N group elements move both vertices by ≤ ε.

The hyperbolicity of C(S) underpins the Masur–Minsky hierarchy machinery. Tight geodesics in C(S) project to subsurface curve graphs, and the distance formula expresses the distance in C(S) as a sum of subsurface projection distances, up to a uniform additive and multiplicative error. This formula is the key to proving that Mod(S) is hierarchically hyperbolic (Behrstock–Hagen–Sisto, 2017). The boundary of C(S) is homeomorphic to the space of ending laminations EL(S), which carries a natural topology making the action of Mod(S) a convergence group action.

Applications extend to the study of hyperbolic 3‑manifolds: the ending lamination theorem (Minsky, 2010; Brock–Canary–Minsky, 2012) uses the curve graph to parametrize the ends of hyperbolic 3‑manifolds with incompressible boundary. The curve graph also provides a model for the large‑scale geometry of Mod(S); the orbit map Mod(S) → C(S) is a quasi‑isometric embedding when restricted to the set of pseudo‑Anosov elements, but not for the whole group because of the presence of Dehn twists.

Key references: Masur–Minsky, “Geometry of the complex of curves I: Hyperbolicity” (1999); Bowditch, “Tight geodesics in the curve complex” (2008); Hensel–Przytycki–Webb, “1‑slim triangles and uniform hyperbolicity of the curve graph” (2015); Behrstock–Hagen–Sisto, “Hierarchically hyperbolic spaces I” (2017).

Technique: Using the distance formula to bound translation lengths
Let φ be a pseudo‑Anosov mapping class on a closed surface S of genus 2 with stretch factor λ > 1. Explain how the translation length τC(φ) = limn→∞ dC(α, φn(α))/n on the curve graph relates to λ, and why τC(φ) > 0.
The stretch factor λ is the exponential growth rate of the length of a curve under iteration of φ in the Teichmüller metric. By the work of Masur–Minsky, the curve graph distance dC(α, φn(α)) is coarsely equal to the sum over all essential subsurfaces Y of the projection distances dY(α, φn(α)). For a pseudo‑Anosov φ, there is a uniform lower bound on the projection distance to the whole surface S itself, which grows linearly with n. Consequently τC(φ) is positive and comparable to log λ up to multiplicative constants depending only on the topology of S. The positivity follows from the fact that φ acts as a loxodromic isometry on the hyperbolic space C(S); its translation length equals the distance between its attracting and repelling fixed points in the Gromov boundary EL(S).
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks about the curve graph, recall three pillars: (1) it is hyperbolic (Masur–Minsky), (2) the mapping class group acts acylindrically (Bowditch), (3) the distance formula reduces curve graph distances to subsurface projections. Use the distance formula to translate algebraic properties of mapping classes into geometric statements about projection distances. Remember that the curve graph is not locally finite, so compactness arguments must use the hyperbolic boundary instead of finite subgraphs.

The Geometry of the Arc Graph and its Applications

The arc graph A(S) of a surface S with boundary (or punctures) has vertices the isotopy classes of essential properly embedded arcs, with edges when arcs can be realized with disjoint interiors. Hensel, Przytycki, and Webb (2013) proved that A(S) is uniformly hyperbolic, with hyperbolicity constant independent of the surface complexity. Moreover, the arc graph is quasi‑isometric to the curve graph C(S) via the map that sends an arc to a curve obtained by taking the boundary of a regular neighbourhood of the arc together with a boundary component. This quasi‑isometry is Mod(S)‑equivariant, so the arc graph provides an alternative model for the large‑scale geometry of the mapping class group.

The arc graph is particularly useful for studying surfaces with boundary because it avoids the technicalities of curves that are parallel to boundary components. It also plays a central role in the proof that the curve graph is hyperbolic: the contact graph, which is built from arcs and curves, is shown to be quasi‑isometric to the arc graph, and the hyperbolicity of the contact graph is easier to establish using 1‑slim triangle arguments. The arc graph has been used by Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara (2015) to construct non‑trivial quasi‑morphisms on Mod(S) via the Bestvina–Fujiwara construction on hyperbolic graphs.

Applications extend to the study of Out(Fn) via the free splitting complex and the free factor complex, which are analogues of the arc and curve graphs for free groups. The hyperbolicity of the arc graph also implies that the mapping class group of a surface with boundary is acylindrically hyperbolic, a fact used by Osin (2016) to deduce many algebraic properties such as the existence of non‑elementary subgroups with strong fixed point properties.

Key references: Hensel–Przytycki–Webb, “The arc graph is hyperbolic” (2013); Bestvina–Bromberg–Fujiwara, “Constructing group actions on quasi‑trees” (2015); Osin, “Acylindrically hyperbolic groups” (2016).

The Geometry of the Contact Graph and Quasi‑Isometry to the Curve Graph

The contact graph X(S) was introduced by Hensel, Przytycki, and Webb (2015) as a graph whose vertices are isotopy classes of essential simple closed curves and essential arcs, with an edge between a curve and an arc when the arc can be realized with minimal intersection with the curve (i.e., they are in “contact”). The contact graph is bipartite and has the property that any two vertices at distance two in the curve graph are at distance at most four in the contact graph. The main theorem states that the inclusion of the curve graph into the contact graph is a quasi‑isometry, with constants independent of the surface. Consequently, the contact graph is also uniformly hyperbolic.

The proof of hyperbolicity for the contact graph uses the notion of 1‑slim triangles: every geodesic triangle in X(S) is contained in the 1‑neighbourhood of the union of its sides. This is established by analyzing the combinatorics of arcs and curves on subsurfaces and using the fact that the arc graph is hyperbolic. The contact graph simplifies many arguments because it avoids the need to pass to subsurface projections; instead, one works directly with the contact relation, which is easier to manipulate combinatorially.

The contact graph has become a standard tool in the study of hierarchically hyperbolic groups. Behrstock, Hagen, and Sisto (2017) use the contact graph as the “hyperbolic space” associated to the domain corresponding to the whole surface in the hierarchical structure of Mod(S). The quasi‑isometry between the contact graph and the curve graph ensures that the distance formula can be expressed in terms of contact graph distances, which are often easier to compute. The contact graph also appears in the work of Durham–Hagen–Sisto (2017) on the coarse geometry of mapping class groups and in the proof that the mapping class group is a hierarchically hyperbolic group.

Key references: Hensel–Przytycki–Webb, “1‑slim triangles and uniform hyperbolicity of the curve graph” (2015); Behrstock–Hagen–Sisto, “Hierarchically hyperbolic spaces I” (2017); Durham–Hagen–Sisto, “Boundaries and automorphisms of hierarchically hyperbolic spaces” (2017).

The Geometry of the JSJ Decomposition and Graphs of Groups

The JSJ decomposition (named after Jaco–Shalen and Johannson) for finitely presented groups was developed by Rips and Sela (1994) for hyperbolic groups, by Bowditch (1998) for relatively hyperbolic groups, and by Dunwoody and Sageev (1999) for general finitely presented groups using the theory of group actions on trees. A JSJ decomposition over a class of edge groups (typically virtually cyclic groups) is a graph of groups that encodes all splittings of the group over that class in a canonical way, up to sliding conjugacy and conjugation. The vertex groups are either rigid (they do not split further over the given class) or flexible (they are surface‑type or orbifold‑type groups that admit many splittings).

For a hyperbolic group G, the JSJ decomposition over virtually cyclic subgroups is a finite graph of groups where edge groups are virtually cyclic, and the flexible vertices correspond to virtually free groups or surface groups with boundary. The decomposition is canonical in the sense that any other splitting over virtually cyclic groups can be obtained from it by a sequence of slides and conjugations. This canonical nature makes the JSJ decomposition a powerful quasi‑isometry invariant: if two hyperbolic groups are quasi‑isometric, their JSJ decompositions are compatible (Paulin, 1996; Bowditch, 1998).

The JSJ decomposition is closely tied to Bass–Serre theory: the group acts on the Bass–Serre tree associated to the graph of groups, and the vertex stabilizers are the vertex groups. The tree is a quasi‑tree, and its geometry reflects the large‑scale geometry of the group. For relatively hyperbolic groups, the JSJ decomposition over parabolic subgroups captures the peripheral structure. In the context of the quasi‑isometric classification of groups, the JSJ decomposition provides a finite combinatorial object that can be used to distinguish quasi‑isometry classes, as demonstrated by the work of Behrstock–Drutu–Mosher (2009) on mapping class groups and by Cashen–Macura (2011) on free‑by‑cyclic groups.

Key references: Rips–Sela, “Structure and rigidity in hyperbolic groups I” (1994); Bowditch, “Cut points and canonical splittings of hyperbolic groups” (1998); Dunwoody–Sageev, “JSJ‑splittings for finitely presented groups” (1999); Paulin, “The Gromov topology on R‑trees” (1996); Behrstock–Drutu–Mosher, “Thick metric spaces, relative hyperbolicity, and quasi‑isometric rigidity” (2009).
