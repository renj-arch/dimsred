Harmonic Analysis — Decomposing Functions into Frequencies

Harmonic analysis decomposes functions into basic waves (harmonics). It includes Fourier series (periodic), Fourier transform (non-periodic), distribution theory (generalized functions), Calderón-Zygmund theory (singular integrals), Littlewood-Paley theory (frequency decomposition), BMO (bounded mean oscillation), and Hardy spaces. It's the mathematical language of signal processing, quantum mechanics, and PDE analysis.

"Fourier analysis is the art of decomposing a function into its constituent frequencies — the music of mathematics."
— Joseph Fourier
Part 1

🎵 Fourier Series — Periodicity and Convergence

Harmonics
Fourier Series — Decomposing Periodic Functions into Sinusoids
Any sufficiently nice periodic function f(x+2π)=f(x) can be written as a Fourier series: f(x) = a₀/2 + Σ_{n=1}^∞ (a_n cos(nx) + b_n sin(nx)), with coefficients a_n = (1/π)∫ f(x) cos(nx) dx, b_n = (1/π)∫ f(x) sin(nx) dx. The complex form: f(x) = Σ ĉ_n e^{inx} with ĉ_n = (1/2π)∫ f(x) e^{-inx} dx. Dirichlet's theorem: if f is piecewise smooth, the Fourier series converges pointwise to f at continuity points and to the average of left/right limits at jumps (Gibbs phenomenon). Fejér's theorem: Cesàro means of Fourier series converge uniformly for continuous f. The L² convergence theorem: Fourier series converge in L² norm (Parseval/Plancherel): (1/2π)∫|f|² = Σ |ĉ_n|². The Riemann-Lebesgue lemma: ĉ_n → 0 as |n|→∞ for f ∈ L¹.
Fourier series resolve periodic functions into pure frequencies — the building blocks of all periodic phenomena.
Part 2

🌊 Fourier Transform — From Periodic to Continuous

Transform
The Fourier Transform — The Heart of Modern Analysis
For f ∈ L¹(ℝⁿ), the Fourier transform is f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x) e^{-2πix·ξ} dx. The inversion formula: f(x) = ∫ f̂(ξ) e^{2πix·ξ} dξ. The transform converts convolution to multiplication: (f*g)̂ = f̂ ĝ. The Plancherel theorem: ∥f̂∥_{L²} = ∥f∥_{L²} — the Fourier transform extends to an isometry on L². The uncertainty principle: ∥xf∥₂∥ξf̂∥₂ ≥ ∥f∥₂²/4π — a function and its transform cannot both be sharply localized. Schwartz space S(ℝⁿ) of rapidly decreasing functions is invariant under Fourier transform. Tempered distributions S' extend the transform to generalized functions like the Dirac delta δ̂₀ = 1. The Fourier transform of δ_a is e^{-2πia·ξ} — a pure frequency. The Poisson summation formula: Σ f(n) = Σ f̂(n) links sums of a function to sums of its transform.
The Fourier transform turns differential equations into algebraic equations, convolution into multiplication, and uncertainty into a precise inequality.

Part 4

🔬 Hardy Spaces, Weighted Inequalities, and Modern Directions

Refinements
Hardy Spaces, A_p Weights, and the T(1) Theorem
Hardy spaces Hᵖ (0A_p weights (Muckenhoupt weights) are functions w ≥ 0 such that the Hardy-Littlewood maximal function is bounded on Lᵖ(w). The Hilbert transform is bounded on Lᵖ(w) iff w ∈ A_p. The weighted norm inequalities give sharp constants (A₂ conjecture, solved by Hytönen). The T(1) theorem (David-Journé) gives necessary and sufficient conditions for a Calderón-Zygmund operator to be bounded on L²: T(1), T*(1) ∈ BMO, and weak boundedness. Wavelets (Daubechies, Meyer) provide orthonormal bases of L² with simultaneous time-frequency localization — a discrete Littlewood-Paley theory.
Hardy spaces, A_p weights, and the T(1) theorem represent the modern synthesis — harmonic analysis at its most powerful and refined.
