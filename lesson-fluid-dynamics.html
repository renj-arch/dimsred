Part 1

🌊 Fundamentals and Conservation Laws

Conservation Laws
The Continuum Hypothesis, the Reynolds Transport Theorem, and the Governing Equations
The continuum hypothesis treats a fluid as a continuous medium, valid when the mean free path λ is much smaller than the characteristic length scale L (Knudsen number Kn = λ/L ≪ 1). The Eulerian description observes fluid properties at fixed spatial points; the Lagrangian description follows individual fluid parcels. The material derivative D/Dt = ∂/∂t + v·∇ gives the rate of change following a fluid element. The Reynolds transport theorem d/dt ∫_V f dV = ∫_V ∂f/∂t dV + ∫_S f v·n dS relates the rate of change of a quantity in a control volume to fluxes through its surface. Mass conservation: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρv) = 0. For incompressible flow (ρ = const), ∇·v = 0. Momentum conservation: the Cauchy momentum equation ρ Dv/Dt = ∇·σ + ρf, where σ = −pI + τ is the total stress tensor and f are body forces (typically gravity). Angular momentum conservation implies the stress tensor is symmetric: σ_{ij} = σ_{ji}. The vorticity ω = ∇ × v measures local rotation. The vorticity equation Dω/Dt = (ω·∇)v − ω(∇·v) + (1/ρ²) ∇ρ × ∇p + ν∇²ω describes the evolution of vorticity — the first term represents vortex stretching (crucial in turbulence), the second is compression/expansion, the third is baroclinic torque (important in stratified flows), and the fourth is viscous diffusion. The circulation Γ = ∮ v·dl = ∫_S ω·n dS around a closed loop evolves according to Kelvin's circulation theorem DΓ/Dt = 0 for an inviscid, barotropic fluid with conservative body forces — explaining the persistence of vortices.
The governing equations of fluid dynamics express the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy — the Navier-Stokes equations are the momentum equation with the Newtonian constitutive relation.
Part 2

🛩 Bernoulli's Equation and Potential Flow

Inviscid Flow
Bernoulli's Principle, the Velocity Potential, Streamfunction, and the Kutta-Joukowski Theorem
Bernoulli's equation p + ½ρv² + ρgz = constant along a streamline for steady, inviscid, incompressible flow. It follows from integrating the Euler equation along a streamline. For irrotational flow (∇×v = 0, valid everywhere in an inviscid flow starting from rest), Bernoulli's constant is the same everywhere. Stagnation pressure p₀ = p + ½ρv² is the pressure when the fluid is brought to rest isentropically. The Pitot-static tube measures flow velocity via the stagnation and static pressure difference: v = √(2(p₀−p)/ρ). For compressible flow, p₀/p = (1 + (γ−1)M²/2)^{γ/(γ−1)} where M = v/c is the Mach number. Potential flow assumes the flow is irrotational and incompressible, so the velocity field can be written as v = ∇φ, where φ satisfies Laplace's equation ∇²φ = 0. The streamfunction ψ for 2D incompressible flow satisfies v_x = ∂ψ/∂y, v_y = −∂ψ/∂x, and lines of constant ψ are streamlines. For irrotational flow, ψ also satisfies Laplace's equation ∇²ψ = 0. Complex analysis provides powerful tools: the complex potential w(z) = φ + iψ is an analytic function of z = x + iy. Elementary flows: uniform flow w = Uz, source/sink w = (m/2π) ln z, point vortex w = −(iΓ/2π) ln z, doublet w = −κ/(2πz). The Joukowski transformation ζ = z + a²/z maps a circle to an airfoil profile. The Kutta condition fixes the circulation around an airfoil such that the flow leaves the trailing edge smoothly. The Kutta-Joukowski theorem: lift per unit span L = ρUΓ, where Γ is the circulation. The d'Alembert paradox: potential flow predicts zero drag on a body in steady inviscid flow — drag arises from viscosity and wake formation. The added mass effect: a body accelerating in an inviscid fluid experiences an apparent added mass equal to the mass of the displaced fluid times a shape-dependent coefficient.
Bernoulli's equation is the workhorse of practical fluid dynamics; potential flow theory elegantly describes inviscid flows using harmonic functions but fails to predict drag — the d'Alembert paradox.
Part 3

🧬 Viscous Flow and Boundary Layers

Viscous Flows
The No-Slip Condition, the Blasius Solution, Boundary Layer Separation, and Pipe Flow
The no-slip boundary condition at a solid wall: the fluid velocity equals the wall velocity (v_fluid = v_wall). This creates a boundary layer — a thin region near the wall where viscous effects are important. Prandtl's boundary layer theory (1904): at high Re, viscous effects are confined to a thin layer of thickness δ ∼ L/√Re adjacent to the wall. Outside this layer, the flow is essentially inviscid. The Blasius solution (1908) for the laminar boundary layer on a flat plate uses the similarity variable η = y √(U/(νx)) and the streamfunction ψ = √(νUx) f(η). The Blasius equation: f''' + ½ f f'' = 0 with f(0) = f'(0) = 0, f'(∞) = 1. The boundary layer thickness δ_99 = 5.0 x/√Re_x, the displacement thickness δ* = 1.7208 x/√Re_x, and the momentum thickness θ = 0.664 x/√Re_x. The skin friction coefficient c_f = τ_w/(½ρU²) = 0.664/√Re_x for the Blasius profile. The Falkner-Skan solutions generalize Blasius to flows with pressure gradients (similarity solutions for wedge flows U(x) = C x^m). Flow separation occurs when an adverse pressure gradient (∂p/∂x > 0, i.e., pressure increasing downstream) decelerates the near-wall flow until the wall shear stress vanishes: (∂u/∂y)_{y=0} = 0. Beyond the separation point, reversed flow creates a recirculation region and a wake. The separation bubble significantly increases drag (pressure drag due to the low-pressure wake). The Hagen-Poiseuille flow in a pipe: for fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe, the velocity profile is parabolic u(r) = (R²/4μ)(−dp/dx)(1 − r²/R²), giving a volumetric flow rate Q = πR⁴(−dp/dx)/(8μ) (the Hagen-Poiseuille law). The Darcy friction factor f = 64/Re for laminar pipe flow. The Couette flow between parallel plates: u(y) = U y/h (linear profile for simple shear). The Stokes flow (Re ≪ 1, also called creeping flow) neglects inertial terms: ∇p = μ∇²v. The Stokes drag on a sphere: F_D = 6π μ a U (valid for Re < 1). The Oseen correction improves Stokes drag at small but finite Re: F_D = 6π μ a U (1 + 3Re/16).
Prandtl's boundary layer concept resolved the d'Alembert paradox by showing that viscosity, though confined to thin layers, controls flow separation and thus determines the drag on bodies.
Part 4

🌀 Turbulence: The Kolmogorov Theory

Turbulence
The Transition to Turbulence, the Energy Cascade, Kolmogorov's k⁻⁵/³ Spectrum, and Turbulent Boundary Layers
The transition to turbulence occurs when Re exceeds a critical value (Re_c ≈ 2300 for pipe flow, Re_c ≈ 5×10⁵ for a flat plate boundary layer). The linear stage: the Orr-Sommerfeld equation (φ'''' − 2α²φ'' + α⁴φ) − iα Re[(U−c)(φ'' − α²φ) − U''φ] = 0 describes the growth of small disturbances φ(y) e^{iα(x−ct)}. Tollmien-Schlichting (T-S) waves are the unstable eigenmodes that grow into turbulence. Richardson's energy cascade (1922): kinetic energy is injected at large scales L (the integral scale), transferred through successively smaller eddies, and dissipated at the Kolmogorov microscale η. The Kolmogorov hypotheses (1941): at sufficiently high Re, small-scale turbulence is statistically isotropic and universal, determined solely by the dissipation rate ε and the kinematic viscosity ν. K41 theory: the energy spectrum E(k) = C_K ε^{2/3} k^{-5/3} in the inertial range (1/L ≪ k ≪ 1/η), where C_K ≈ 1.5 is the Kolmogorov constant. The Kolmogorov microscales: length η = (ν³/ε)^{1/4}, time τ_η = (ν/ε)^{1/2}, velocity v_η = (νε)^{1/4}. The ratio of largest to smallest scales: L/η ∼ Re^{3/4}. The turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) equation Dk/Dt = P − ε + D + T, where P = −u_i'u_j' ∂U_i/∂x_j is production, ε = ν (∂u_i'/∂x_j)(∂u_i'/∂x_j) is dissipation, D is viscous diffusion, and T is turbulent transport. The turbulent boundary layer: the law of the wall gives u⁺ = y⁺ in the viscous sublayer (y⁺ < 5) and u⁺ = (1/κ) ln y⁺ + B in the log layer (y⁺ > 30), where u⁺ = u/u_τ, y⁺ = y u_τ/ν, u_τ = √(τ_w/ρ) is the friction velocity, κ ≈ 0.41 is the von Kármán constant, and B ≈ 5.0. The Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations decompose v = V + v' and introduce the Reynolds stress tensor τ_{ij}^{R} = −ρ v_i' v_j'. The k-ε model (Launder-Spalding) solves transport equations for k and ε with eddy viscosity ν_t = C_μ k²/ε. The large eddy simulation (LES) resolves large eddies directly and models subgrid scales. The Smagorinsky model: τ_{ij}^{SGS} = −2 (C_s Δ)² |S| S_{ij} where Δ is the filter width. Direct numerical simulation (DNS) resolves all scales down to η but is limited to low Re due to the Re^{9/4} scaling of computational cost.
Turbulence is the last great unsolved problem of classical physics — Kolmogorov's 1941 theory provides a universal description of small scales, but predicting turbulent flows from first principles remains a formidable challenge.
