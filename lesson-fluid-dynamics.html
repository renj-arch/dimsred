Part 1

🌊 Fundamentals and Conservation Laws

Conservation Laws
The Continuum Hypothesis, the Reynolds Transport Theorem, and the Governing Equations
The continuum hypothesis treats a fluid as a continuous medium, valid when the mean free path λ is much smaller than the characteristic length scale L (Knudsen number Kn = λ/L ≪ 1). The Eulerian description observes fluid properties at fixed spatial points; the Lagrangian description follows individual fluid parcels. The material derivative D/Dt = ∂/∂t + v·∇ gives the rate of change following a fluid element. The Reynolds transport theorem d/dt ∫_V f dV = ∫_V ∂f/∂t dV + ∫_S f v·n dS relates the rate of change of a quantity in a control volume to fluxes through its surface. Mass conservation: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρv) = 0. For incompressible flow (ρ = const), ∇·v = 0. Momentum conservation: the Cauchy momentum equation ρ Dv/Dt = ∇·σ + ρf, where σ = −pI + τ is the total stress tensor and f are body forces (typically gravity). Angular momentum conservation implies the stress tensor is symmetric: σ_{ij} = σ_{ji}. The vorticity ω = ∇ × v measures local rotation. The vorticity equation Dω/Dt = (ω·∇)v − ω(∇·v) + (1/ρ²) ∇ρ × ∇p + ν∇²ω describes the evolution of vorticity — the first term represents vortex stretching (crucial in turbulence), the second is compression/expansion, the third is baroclinic torque (important in stratified flows), and the fourth is viscous diffusion. The circulation Γ = ∮ v·dl = ∫_S ω·n dS around a closed loop evolves according to Kelvin's circulation theorem DΓ/Dt = 0 for an inviscid, barotropic fluid with conservative body forces — explaining the persistence of vortices.
The governing equations of fluid dynamics express the conservation of mass, momentum, and energy — the Navier-Stokes equations are the momentum equation with the Newtonian constitutive relation.
Part 2

🛩 Bernoulli's Equation and Potential Flow

Inviscid Flow
Bernoulli's Principle, the Velocity Potential, Streamfunction, and the Kutta-Joukowski Theorem
Bernoulli's equation p + ½ρv² + ρgz = constant along a streamline for steady, inviscid, incompressible flow. It follows from integrating the Euler equation along a streamline. For irrotational flow (∇×v = 0, valid everywhere in an inviscid flow starting from rest), Bernoulli's constant is the same everywhere. Stagnation pressure p₀ = p + ½ρv² is the pressure when the fluid is brought to rest isentropically. The Pitot-static tube measures flow velocity via the stagnation and static pressure difference: v = √(2(p₀−p)/ρ). For compressible flow, p₀/p = (1 + (γ−1)M²/2)^{γ/(γ−1)} where M = v/c is the Mach number. Potential flow assumes the flow is irrotational and incompressible, so the velocity field can be written as v = ∇φ, where φ satisfies Laplace's equation ∇²φ = 0. The streamfunction ψ for 2D incompressible flow satisfies v_x = ∂ψ/∂y, v_y = −∂ψ/∂x, and lines of constant ψ are streamlines. For irrotational flow, ψ also satisfies Laplace's equation ∇²ψ = 0. Complex analysis provides powerful tools: the complex potential w(z) = φ + iψ is an analytic function of z = x + iy. Elementary flows: uniform flow w = Uz, source/sink w = (m/2π) ln z, point vortex w = −(iΓ/2π) ln z, doublet w = −κ/(2πz). The Joukowski transformation ζ = z + a²/z maps a circle to an airfoil profile. The Kutta condition fixes the circulation around an airfoil such that the flow leaves the trailing edge smoothly. The Kutta-Joukowski theorem: lift per unit span L = ρUΓ, where Γ is the circulation. The d'Alembert paradox: potential flow predicts zero drag on a body in steady inviscid flow — drag arises from viscosity and wake formation. The added mass effect: a body accelerating in an inviscid fluid experiences an apparent added mass equal to the mass of the displaced fluid times a shape-dependent coefficient.
Bernoulli's equation is the workhorse of practical fluid dynamics; potential flow theory elegantly describes inviscid flows using harmonic functions but fails to predict drag — the d'Alembert paradox.
Part 3

🧬 Viscous Flow and Boundary Layers

Viscous Flows
The No-Slip Condition, the Blasius Solution, Boundary Layer Separation, and Pipe Flow
The no-slip boundary condition at a solid wall: the fluid velocity equals the wall velocity (v_fluid = v_wall). This creates a boundary layer — a thin region near the wall where viscous effects are important. Prandtl's boundary layer theory (1904): at high Re, viscous effects are confined to a thin layer of thickness δ ∼ L/√Re adjacent to the wall. Outside this layer, the flow is essentially inviscid. The Blasius solution (1908) for the laminar boundary layer on a flat plate uses the similarity variable η = y √(U/(νx)) and the streamfunction ψ = √(νUx) f(η). The Blasius equation: f''' + ½ f f'' = 0 with f(0) = f'(0) = 0, f'(∞) = 1. The boundary layer thickness δ_99 = 5.0 x/√Re_x, the displacement thickness δ* = 1.7208 x/√Re_x, and the momentum thickness θ = 0.664 x/√Re_x. The skin friction coefficient c_f = τ_w/(½ρU²) = 0.664/√Re_x for the Blasius profile. The Falkner-Skan solutions generalize Blasius to flows with pressure gradients (similarity solutions for wedge flows U(x) = C x^m). Flow separation occurs when an adverse pressure gradient (∂p/∂x > 0, i.e., pressure increasing downstream) decelerates the near-wall flow until the wall shear stress vanishes: (∂u/∂y)_{y=0} = 0. Beyond the separation point, reversed flow creates a recirculation region and a wake. The separation bubble significantly increases drag (pressure drag due to the low-pressure wake). The Hagen-Poiseuille flow in a pipe: for fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe, the velocity profile is parabolic u(r) = (R²/4μ)(−dp/dx)(1 − r²/R²), giving a volumetric flow rate Q = πR⁴(−dp/dx)/(8μ) (the Hagen-Poiseuille law). The Darcy friction factor f = 64/Re for laminar pipe flow. The Couette flow between parallel plates: u(y) = U y/h (linear profile for simple shear). The Stokes flow (Re ≪ 1, also called creeping flow) neglects inertial terms: ∇p = μ∇²v. The Stokes drag on a sphere: F_D = 6π μ a U (valid for Re < 1). The Oseen correction improves Stokes drag at small but finite Re: F_D = 6π μ a U (1 + 3Re/16).
Prandtl's boundary layer concept resolved the d'Alembert paradox by showing that viscosity, though confined to thin layers, controls flow separation and thus determines the drag on bodies.
Part 4

🌀 Turbulence: The Kolmogorov Theory

Turbulence
The Transition to Turbulence, the Energy Cascade, Kolmogorov's k⁻⁵/³ Spectrum, and Turbulent Boundary Layers
The transition to turbulence occurs when Re exceeds a critical value (Re_c ≈ 2300 for pipe flow, Re_c ≈ 5×10⁵ for a flat plate boundary layer). The linear stage: the Orr-Sommerfeld equation (φ'''' − 2α²φ'' + α⁴φ) − iα Re[(U−c)(φ'' − α²φ) − U''φ] = 0 describes the growth of small disturbances φ(y) e^{iα(x−ct)}. Tollmien-Schlichting (T-S) waves are the unstable eigenmodes that grow into turbulence. Richardson's energy cascade (1922): kinetic energy is injected at large scales L (the integral scale), transferred through successively smaller eddies, and dissipated at the Kolmogorov microscale η. The Kolmogorov hypotheses (1941): at sufficiently high Re, small-scale turbulence is statistically isotropic and universal, determined solely by the dissipation rate ε and the kinematic viscosity ν. K41 theory: the energy spectrum E(k) = C_K ε^{2/3} k^{-5/3} in the inertial range (1/L ≪ k ≪ 1/η), where C_K ≈ 1.5 is the Kolmogorov constant. The Kolmogorov microscales: length η = (ν³/ε)^{1/4}, time τ_η = (ν/ε)^{1/2}, velocity v_η = (νε)^{1/4}. The ratio of largest to smallest scales: L/η ∼ Re^{3/4}. The turbulent kinetic energy (TKE) equation Dk/Dt = P − ε + D + T, where P = −u_i'u_j' ∂U_i/∂x_j is production, ε = ν (∂u_i'/∂x_j)(∂u_i'/∂x_j) is dissipation, D is viscous diffusion, and T is turbulent transport. The turbulent boundary layer: the law of the wall gives u⁺ = y⁺ in the viscous sublayer (y⁺ < 5) and u⁺ = (1/κ) ln y⁺ + B in the log layer (y⁺ > 30), where u⁺ = u/u_τ, y⁺ = y u_τ/ν, u_τ = √(τ_w/ρ) is the friction velocity, κ ≈ 0.41 is the von Kármán constant, and B ≈ 5.0. The Reynolds-averaged Navier-Stokes (RANS) equations decompose v = V + v' and introduce the Reynolds stress tensor τ_{ij}^{R} = −ρ v_i' v_j'. The k-ε model (Launder-Spalding) solves transport equations for k and ε with eddy viscosity ν_t = C_μ k²/ε. The large eddy simulation (LES) resolves large eddies directly and models subgrid scales. The Smagorinsky model: τ_{ij}^{SGS} = −2 (C_s Δ)² |S| S_{ij} where Δ is the filter width. Direct numerical simulation (DNS) resolves all scales down to η but is limited to low Re due to the Re^{9/4} scaling of computational cost.
Turbulence is the last great unsolved problem of classical physics — Kolmogorov's 1941 theory provides a universal description of small scales, but predicting turbulent flows from first principles remains a formidable challenge.

Compressible Flow and Shock Wave Structure

When the Mach number exceeds unity, density variations become inseparable from pressure and temperature fields, and the governing equations must retain the full energy equation together with an equation of state. For a perfect gas the specific heat ratio γ = cp/cv links pressure p, density ρ, and temperature T through p = ρRT and h = cpT. The one‑dimensional steady Euler equations reduce to the Rankine‑Hugoniot jump conditions across a normal shock: ρ₁u₁ = ρ₂u₂, p₁ + ρ₁u₁² = p₂ + ρ₂u₂², and h₁ + ½u₁² = h₂ + ½u₂². Solving these yields the classic pressure ratio p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ/(γ+1)(M₁²−1) and the downstream Mach number M₂² = [(γ−1)M₁²+2]/[2γM₁²−(γ−1)]. The entropy increase across the shock, Δs = cv ln(p₂/p₁) − cp ln(ρ₂/ρ₁), is positive for M₁>1, confirming the irreversible nature of shock compression.

Oblique Shocks and Expansion Fans

In two‑dimensional supersonic flow, a wedge of half‑angle θ generates an attached oblique shock at angle β satisfying the θ‑β‑M relation: tanθ = 2cotβ (M₁²sin²β−1)/[M₁²(γ+cos2β)+2]. For a given M₁ and θ there are two possible β values — the weak‑shock and strong‑shock branches — with the weak branch usually realized in steady external flows. When the wall turns away from the flow, a centered Prandtl‑Meyer expansion fan appears. The flow turns isentropically through a continuous fan of Mach waves, and the Prandtl‑Meyer function ν(M) = √[(γ+1)/(γ−1)] arctan√[(γ−1)/(γ+1)(M²−1)] − arctan√(M²−1) gives the total turning angle Δθ = ν(M₂)−ν(M₁). These relations are the backbone of supersonic nozzle design and the method of characteristics for solving inviscid supersonic fields.

Shock‑Boundary Layer Interaction

In practical high‑speed vehicles, shock waves impinge on turbulent boundary layers, causing separation, unsteady pressure loads, and increased heating. The interaction length scales with the incoming boundary‑layer thickness δ and the shock strength. For a compression ramp, the separation bubble size correlates with the parameter Λ = (δ*/θ)(Δp/p), where δ* is displacement thickness and θ momentum thickness. Modern prediction relies on Reynolds‑averaged Navier‑Stokes (RANS) with shock‑capturing schemes, but the strong adverse pressure gradient often triggers massive separation that challenges standard eddy‑viscosity models. Hybrid RANS‑LES (DES, IDDES) and wall‑modeled LES are currently the most reliable approaches for capturing the unsteady separation shock motion and the resulting fluctuating heat‑flux peaks.

Magnetohydrodynamics and the Induction Equation

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) couples the Navier‑Stokes equations with Maxwell’s equations under the low‑frequency, non‑relativistic approximation. The fluid is assumed electrically conducting, so the Lorentz force J × B appears in the momentum equation, where J = σ(E + v × B) is the current density (σ electrical conductivity) and B the magnetic field. The magnetic field evolves according to the induction equation ∂B/∂t = ∇ × (v × B) + η∇²B, with magnetic diffusivity η = 1/(μ₀σ). The dimensionless magnetic Reynolds number Rm = UL/η measures the relative importance of advection versus diffusion of magnetic flux. For liquid metals Rm ≪ 1, the field is essentially imposed; for astrophysical plasmas Rm ≫ 1, the field is frozen into the fluid (Alfvén’s theorem) and magnetic flux tubes move with the flow.

Alfvén Waves and MHD Turbulence

Linearising the ideal MHD equations about a uniform field B₀ yields three wave modes: the fast and slow magnetosonic waves (compressive) and the Alfvén wave (incompressive, transverse). The Alfvén speed v_A = B₀/√(μ₀ρ) sets the characteristic propagation speed of magnetic disturbances. In strong MHD turbulence with a mean field, energy cascades anisotropically: the Goldreich‑Sridhar critical‑balance conjecture predicts that the parallel correlation length ℓ∥ scales with the perpendicular length ℓ⊥ as ℓ∥ ∼ ℓ⊥2/3 (for a Kolmogorov‑like perpendicular spectrum E(k⊥) ∼ k⊥−5/3). This anisotropy has been confirmed in high‑resolution direct numerical simulations and in solar‑wind measurements. The turbulent dynamo problem — how small‑scale magnetic fields are amplified by chaotic stretching — remains a central topic; the Kazantsev model predicts a k3/2 magnetic energy spectrum at sub‑viscous scales for large magnetic Prandtl numbers Pm = ν/η ≫ 1.

Applications and Numerical Challenges

MHD is essential for liquid‑metal blankets in fusion reactors, electromagnetic casting, and geophysical dynamo modelling. Numerically, the divergence‑free constraint ∇·B = 0 must be preserved; constrained‑transport (CT) methods and divergence‑cleaning (hyperbolic or parabolic) are standard. Implicit‑explicit (IMEX) time stepping treats the stiff Alfvén wave term implicitly while advancing the hydrodynamic part explicitly, allowing larger Courant numbers. For high‑Rm flows, the magnetic Prandtl number often forces extremely fine grids to resolve the resistive scale η, motivating subgrid‑scale models for the electromotive force ⟨u' × b'⟩ in large‑eddy simulations of MHD turbulence.

Worked Example: Normal Shock Relations
Air (γ = 1.4) approaches a normal shock at Mach 2.5. Compute the downstream Mach number, static pressure ratio, and temperature ratio.
Using the normal‑shock formulas: M₂² = [(γ−1)M₁²+2]/[2γM₁²−(γ−1)] = [(0.4)(6.25)+2]/[2(1.4)(6.25)−0.4] = (2.5+2)/(17.5−0.4) = 4.5/17.1 ≈ 0.263, so M₂ ≈ 0.513. Pressure ratio p₂/p₁ = 1 + 2γ/(γ+1)(M₁²−1) = 1 + 2(1.4)/2.4 (6.25−1) = 1 + 1.1667×5.25 ≈ 7.125. Temperature ratio T₂/T₁ = (p₂/p₁)(ρ₁/ρ₂) with density ratio ρ₂/ρ₁ = (γ+1)M₁²/[(γ−1)M₁²+2] = 2.4×6.25/(0.4×6.25+2) = 15/(2.5+2) = 15/4.5 ≈ 3.333. Hence T₂/T₁ = 7.125/3.333 ≈ 2.14.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three normal‑shock algebraic relations (M₂, p₂/p₁, ρ₂/ρ₁) and the Prandtl‑Meyer function ν(M). In multiple‑choice questions, a quick sanity check is that M₂ < 1 for any M₁ > 1, and p₂/p₁ > 1. For oblique shocks, remember the weak‑shock branch is the physical one unless the problem explicitly states a strong shock. Write the θ‑β‑M equation in the form tanθ = 2cotβ (M₁²sin²β−1)/[M₁²(γ+cos2β)+2] to avoid sign errors.

Non‑Newtonian Constitutive Models and Rheological Classification

Many industrial and biological fluids deviate from the linear Newtonian relation τ = μγ̇. The generalized Newtonian model retains a scalar viscosity that depends on the shear‑rate magnitude: τ = μ(γ̇)γ̇, with μ(γ̇) given by empirical fits such as the power‑law μ = Kγ̇n−1 (n < 1 shear‑thinning, n > 1 shear‑thickening) or the Carreau‑Yasuda model μ(γ̇) = μ∞ + (μ₀−μ∞)[1+(λγ̇)^a](n−1)/a. These models capture steady shear behaviour but cannot predict normal stress differences or memory effects. For viscoelastic fluids, differential constitutive equations are required. The Oldroyd‑B model splits the stress into a Newtonian solvent part and a polymeric part τ_p governed by the upper‑convected Maxwell equation τ_p + λ(∇τ_p) = η_pγ̇, where λ is the relaxation time and ∇ denotes the upper‑convected derivative. The Giesekus and Phan‑Thien‑Tanner (PTT) models introduce a mobility factor to bound the extensional viscosity and predict shear‑thinning of the first normal stress difference N₁ = τ_xx − τ_yy.

Yield‑Stress Fluids and Thixotropy

Materials such as toothpaste, drilling muds, and blood exhibit a yield stress τ_y: they behave as solids for τ < τ_y and flow like a fluid above it. The Bingham model τ = τ_y + μ_pγ̇ (for τ > τ_y) and the Herschel‑Bulkley model τ = τ_y + Kγ̇^n are widely used. Thixotropic fluids show time‑dependent structure breakdown and recovery; a common structural kinetics approach introduces a scalar structure parameter λ_s ∈ [0,1] with evolution dλ_s/dt = −k₁γ̇λ_s + k₂(1−λ_s), and viscosity μ = μ₀λ_s^m. This framework captures hysteresis loops in shear‑rate sweeps and the gradual viscosity recovery after cessation of flow.

Numerical Treatment and Dimensionless Groups

In computational rheology, the high‑Weissenberg number problem (Wi = λU/L ≫ 1) causes loss of positive definiteness of the conformation tensor and numerical instability. The log‑conformation reformulation (Fattal & Kupferman) writes the conformation tensor A = exp(Ψ) and evolves Ψ, guaranteeing positive definiteness. The Deborah number De = λ/τ_flow distinguishes elastic (De ≫ 1) from viscous (De ≪ 1) dominated regimes. For yield‑stress fluids, the Bingham number Bn = τ_yL/(μU) measures the relative importance of yield stress. Accurate simulation of complex geometries often employs the regularised Papanastasiou model τ = τ_y[1−exp(−mγ̇)] + μγ̇ with large m to approximate the discontinuous yield surface while retaining differentiability for Newton‑type solvers.

Advanced Turbulence Modeling: Reynolds Stress and Large‑Eddy Approaches

The Boussinesq eddy‑viscosity hypothesis τij^R = −2ν_tSij + ⅔kδij assumes alignment of the Reynolds stress tensor with the mean strain rate, which fails in flows with strong curvature, rotation, or separation. Reynolds‑stress transport models (RSM) solve transport equations for each component of τij^R, closing the pressure‑strain correlation φij and the dissipation tensor εij with models such as the Launder‑Reece‑Rodi (LRR) or the Speziale‑Sarkar‑Gatski (SSG) formulations. The pressure‑strain term redistributes energy among components and is split into rapid (linear in mean velocity gradients) and slow (proportional to anisotropy) parts. RSM typically requires seven additional PDEs (six stresses plus dissipation) and is sensitive to wall‑boundary conditions for the pressure‑strain term.

Large‑Eddy Simulation Subgrid Models

In LES the filtered Navier‑Stokes equations contain the subgrid‑scale (SGS) stress τijSGS = ū_iū_j − ū_iū_j. The classic Smagorinsky model τijSGS = −2(C_sΔ)²|S̄|S̄ij uses a constant C_s ≈ 0.17 and the filter width Δ = (ΔxΔyΔz)1/3. Dynamic procedures (Germano identity) compute C_s locally from the resolved scales, allowing it to vanish near walls and in laminar regions. The Wall‑Adapting Local Eddy‑viscosity (WALE) model uses the traceless symmetric part of the square of the velocity gradient tensor, providing correct near‑wall scaling without explicit damping functions. For compressible flows, the SGS stress is split into a deviatoric part modeled by eddy viscosity and a trace part related to the SGS kinetic energy, which is often transported via a separate equation.

Hybrid RANS‑LES and Wall‑Modeled LES

Detached‑eddy simulation (DES) blends RANS in the near‑wall region with LES in separated zones by modifying the length scale: d_DES = min(d_RANS, C_DESΔ). Improved variants (DDES, IDDES) protect the RANS layer from premature switching. Wall‑modeled LES (WMLES) resolves the outer logarithmic layer while modelling the inner viscous sublayer with an equilibrium wall‑stress model (log‑law or a thin‑layer RANS). The key challenge is the matching of the LES resolved stress to the wall model at the first off‑wall grid point; the required grid spacing in wall units is Δx⁺ ≈ 50–100, Δz⁺ ≈ 20–30, dramatically cheaper than DNS (Δx⁺ ≈ 10, Δz⁺ ≈ 5). Current research focuses on non‑equilibrium wall models that incorporate pressure‑gradient and unsteady effects for high‑speed and separated flows.

Multiphase Flow Interface Tracking and Volume‑of‑Fluid Methods

Multiphase flows involve distinct fluid regions separated by sharp interfaces where density and viscosity jump. The Volume‑of‑Fluid (VOF) method defines a colour function C(x,t) ∈ [0,1] representing the volume fraction of one phase in each cell. The advection equation ∂C/∂t + ∇·(vC) = 0 is solved with geometric reconstruction (PLIC — Piecewise Linear Interface Calculation) to maintain a sharp interface and conserve mass. Surface tension is modelled as a continuum force F_σ = σκ∇C, where σ is the surface tension coefficient and κ = −∇·(∇C/|∇C|) the curvature computed from the volume fraction gradient. The Continuum Surface Force (CSF) model of Brackbill et al. distributes the singular force over a few grid cells, but spurious currents (parasitic currents) arise from curvature discretisation errors; balanced‑force algorithms and height‑function curvature evaluation reduce these artefacts.

Level‑Set and Coupled Level‑Set/VOF

The Level‑Set method represents the interface as the zero contour of a signed distance function φ(x,t), advected by ∂φ/∂t + v·∇φ = 0. Re‑initialisation maintains the distance property. Level‑Set provides smooth curvature and normal vectors but suffers from mass loss. The Coupled Level‑Set/VOF (CLSVOF) method uses VOF for mass conservation and Level‑Set for geometric quantities, combining the strengths of both. In the coupled approach, the VOF fraction is used to correct the Level‑Set function periodically, while the Level‑Set normal and curvature feed the surface‑tension force in the momentum equation.

Phase‑Change and Boiling Models

Boiling and condensation introduce mass transfer across the interface. The interfacial mass flux ṁ = q_latent/h_fg is linked to the local heat flux q_latent = k∇T·n. In VOF frameworks, the phase‑change source term appears in the volume‑fraction equation as ∂C/∂t + ∇·(vC) = ṁ/ρ. The energy equation includes a latent‑heat sink/source at the interface. Accurate prediction of nucleation site density, bubble departure diameter, and frequency requires subgrid models; the Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute (RPI) wall‑boiling model partitions the wall heat flux into single‑phase convection, quenching, and evaporation components, each correlated with local wall superheat and bubble dynamics. For high‑fidelity simulation of droplet impact, film boiling, or spray combustion, adaptive mesh refinement (AMR) concentrates resolution near the moving interface while keeping the overall cell count tractable.

  • Assuming the Boussinesq eddy‑viscosity hypothesis holds in strongly curved or rotating flows, leading to gross misprediction of Reynolds stress anisotropy.
  • Using a constant Smagorinsky coefficient in wall‑bounded LES without a dynamic or wall‑adapting procedure, which over‑damps near‑wall turbulence and yields incorrect skin‑friction.
  • Neglecting the upper‑convected derivative in viscoelastic constitutive equations, thereby missing normal stress differences and elastic recoil effects.
  • Applying the standard VOF curvature formula κ = −∇·(∇C/|∇C|) on coarse grids without height‑function correction, producing large parasitic currents that corrupt capillary‑driven flows.
  • Treating the magnetic field as divergence‑free only at the initial condition and not enforcing ∇·B = 0 during time integration, causing unphysical magnetic monopoles that corrupt MHD forces.
  • Using the Bingham model without regularisation in implicit solvers, resulting in a non‑differentiable yield surface that stalls Newton iterations.
  • Confusing the Deborah number (ratio of relaxation time to flow time) with the Weissenberg number (ratio of relaxation time to shear time) when characterising viscoelastic flow regimes.
