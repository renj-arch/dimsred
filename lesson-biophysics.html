Biophysics — Molecular Motors, Ion Channels, Protein Folding & Neural Biophysics

Deep dive into Biophysics — the physics of biological systems: molecular motors (kinesin, myosin, ATP synthase and their mechanochemical cycles), ion channels and membrane biophysics (the Nernst-Planck equation, Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz voltage, channel gating, the Hodgkin-Huxley model), protein folding thermodynamics (the Levinthal paradox, the folding funnel, the HP model, the Ising-like models), membrane biophysics (lipid bilayers, elasticity, the Helfrich Hamiltonian, poration), and neural biophysics (action potential propagation, the cable equation, the FitzHugh-Nagumo model, dendritic integration, and synaptic transmission).

Part 1

🧬 Molecular Motors and Mechanochemistry

Motor Proteins
Kinesin, Myosin, ATP Synthase, and the Ratchet Mechanism
Molecular motors are proteins that convert chemical energy (typically ATP hydrolysis) into mechanical work. Kinesin walks along microtubules toward the plus end, carrying cargo vesicles, with each step consuming one ATP and moving 8 nm (the hand-over-hand mechanism). Kinesin's two heads cycle through ATP binding, hydrolysis, Pi release, and ADP release, coordinated so that at least one head remains bound (the duty ratio). Myosin walks along actin filaments; muscle myosin II operates via the power stroke — a 5–10 nm swing of the lever arm upon Pi release. The Lymn-Taylor cycle describes the ATPase cycle of myosin: ATP binds, myosin releases actin, ATP hydrolyzes, myosin rebinds actin weakly, Pi release triggers the power stroke, and ADP release completes the cycle. ATP synthase (F₀F₁-ATPase) is a rotary motor: the F₁ subunit hydrolyzes ATP to rotate the γ-subunit, while the F₀ subunit uses the proton motive force to drive rotation in reverse for ATP synthesis. The Brownian ratchet model describes how thermal fluctuations are rectified by asymmetric potentials and energy input (the Feynman ratchet). The stall force of kinesin is ~7 pN; myosin II stalls at ~3–4 pN. The fluctuation theorem (the Jarzynski equality and Crooks relation) relates work distributions to free energy differences in non-equilibrium single-molecule experiments. The optical tweezers technique measures forces and displacements of single motors with sub-pN resolution, revealing step sizes, dwell times, and the force-velocity relationship. The processivity of a motor (the number of steps before detaching) depends on the duty ratio and the mechanical coupling efficiency. The Fokker-Planck equation for biased diffusion on a periodic potential describes the average velocity of a motor as a function of load and ATP concentration.
Molecular motors are nano-scale machines that operate far from equilibrium, using ATP hydrolysis to rectify Brownian motion and produce directed movement against piconewton loads.

⚡ Ion Channels and Membrane Biophysics

Electrophysiology
The Nernst-Planck Equation, Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz Voltage, and Channel Gating
The Nernst-Planck equation describes ion flux J = −D (∇c + (ze/k_B T) c ∇V), combining diffusion (Fick's law) and drift (Ohm's law). The Nernst potential for ion species i is V_i = (k_B T / z_i e) ln([i]_out / [i]_in). The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz (GHK) voltage equation gives the resting membrane potential when multiple ions permeate: V_m = (RT/F) ln((P_K[K⁺]_out + P_Na[Na⁺]_out + P_Cl[Cl⁻]_in) / (P_K[K⁺]_in + P_Na[Na⁺]_in + P_Cl[Cl⁻]_out)). The patch clamp technique (Neher and Sakmann, 1976) measures single-channel currents, revealing that channels open and close stochastically with conductance typically 1–100 pS. The Hodgkin-Huxley model (1952) describes the action potential via voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels: C_m dV/dt = −g_Na m³h (V − V_Na) − g_K n⁴ (V − V_K) − g_L (V − V_L) + I_ext, where m, h, n are gating variables satisfying first-order kinetics with voltage-dependent rate constants α(V) and β(V). The sodium inactivation (h-gate) is what repolarizes the membrane. The cable equation for a passive dendrite: λ² ∂²V/∂x² − τ ∂V/∂t − V = 0, where λ = √(r_m / r_a) is the length constant and τ = r_m c_m is the time constant. The Hodgkin-Huxley model exhibits threshold behavior (a separatrix in phase space) and refractoriness. The FitzHugh-Nagumo model simplifies the dynamics to two variables (excitable and recovery). The Markov model of channel gating represents each channel as a finite-state Markov chain with voltage-dependent transition rates; the master equation dP/dt = K(V)P gives the probability distribution over states. The Leaky integrate-and-fire model is a phenomenological spiking neuron model: C dV/dt = (V_rest − V)/R + I, resetting V to V_reset when V reaches V_thresh.
Ion channels are selective, voltage-gated pores that generate electrical signals in neurons; the Hodgkin-Huxley model's gating variables capture the nonlinear dynamics of action potentials.

🔬 Protein Folding Thermodynamics

Folding Physics
The Levinthal Paradox, the Folding Funnel, and Lattice Models
Protein folding is the process by which a linear chain of amino acids adopts a unique native three-dimensional structure. The Levinthal paradox (1969): if a 100-residue protein explored all conformations randomly at 10¹³/s, it would take ~10⁵⁰ years to find the native state — yet proteins fold in milliseconds. The resolution is that folding is not random: there is a folding funnel (energy landscape) with a bias toward the native state. The principle of minimal frustration (Bryngelson and Wolynes, 1989): the energy landscape is relatively smooth, with native interactions being mostly stabilizing, so the protein is not trapped in deep non-native minima. The Gō model (1975) includes only native contacts, producing a perfectly funneled landscape. The HP model (Dill, 1985) represents proteins as a chain of H (hydrophobic) and P (polar) monomers on a lattice, with the driving force being the hydrophobic effect: H-H contacts are energetically favorable. The hydrophobic effect (the release of water from ordered clathrate cages around exposed nonpolar groups) is the dominant driving force for folding (ΔG ≈ −ΔS_hydrophobic · T). The thermodynamics of folding: ΔG_fold = G_native − G_unfolded = ΔH − TΔS. For a typical globular protein, ΔG_fold ≈ −20 to −60 kJ/mol at 300 K, with ΔH ≈ −200 to −500 kJ/mol (favorable) and −TΔS ≈ +200 to +400 kJ/mol (unfavorable, dominated by the conformational entropy loss). The two-state folder model: many small proteins fold with a single exponential kinetics (no stable intermediates), K_fold = [native]/[unfolded] = exp(−ΔG/RT). The Ising-like model (the Wako-Saitô-Muñoz-Eaton model) treats each residue as a binary variable (native/unfolded) with a cooperative interaction between adjacent structured regions. The Φ-value analysis (Fersht, 1990s) uses site-directed mutagenesis to probe the transition state of folding: Φ = (ΔΔG‡)/(ΔΔG_U), where Φ ≈ 1 indicates fully native-like structure in the transition state and Φ ≈ 0 indicates unfolded-like. The Chevron plot (log k vs [denaturant]) yields the folding and unfolding arms: log k_f = log k_f⁰ − m_f [D], log k_u = log k_u⁰ + m_u [D], with the intersection at the denaturation midpoint. The nucleation-propagation mechanism: folding begins with a small nucleus (a few native contacts) followed by rapid propagation. The chaperonin proteins (GroEL/GroES) assist folding by providing an isolated cage that prevents aggregation and allows repeated folding attempts (the Anfinsen cage).
Protein folding is guided by a funnel-shaped energy landscape where the hydrophobic effect provides the primary driving force, and the Levinthal paradox is resolved by the non-random, biased nature of the search.

🧠 Neural Biophysics and Networks

Neural Dynamics
Action Potential Propagation, Dendritic Integration, and Synaptic Transmission
The action potential (spike) propagates along axons via the cable equation with active Hodgkin-Huxley conductances. The propagation velocity for an unmyelinated axon scales as v ∝ √(d/2R_m C_m) where d is the diameter. Myelination by Schwann cells (PNS) or oligodendrocytes (CNS) increases velocity by a factor of ~50 through saltatory conduction at the nodes of Ranvier, where voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are concentrated. The length constant λ = √(r_m / (r_a + r_o)) determines how far a subthreshold signal propagates passively. Dendritic integration: dendrites are not passive cables — they contain voltage-gated Na⁺, Ca²⁺, and K⁺ channels that amplify and shape synaptic inputs. The cable model with branching geometry (Rall's model) shows that the equivalent cylinder approximation works when the dendrites satisfy the d³/² rule at branch points. Synaptic transmission: an action potential arrives at the presynaptic terminal, opening voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels. The Ca²⁺ influx triggers vesicle fusion (exocytosis) of neurotransmitter-filled vesicles (the SNARE complex: syntaxin, SNAP-25, synaptobrevin). The quantal hypothesis (Katz, 1950s): neurotransmitter is released in discrete packets (quanta) of ~5000 molecules per vesicle. The postsynaptic potential (EPSP or IPSP) amplitude depends on the number of released vesicles (the binomial model: release probability p, number of release sites N, quantal size q). The short-term plasticity includes facilitation (increased release probability due to residual Ca²⁺) and depression (vesicle depletion). The long-term potentiation (LTP) at the CA3-CA1 synapse in the hippocampus is the cellular basis of learning: high-frequency stimulation activates NMDA receptors, allowing Ca²⁺ influx that triggers CaMKII activation and AMPA receptor insertion. The Bienenstock-Cooper-Munro (BCM) theory of synaptic plasticity: the modification threshold θ_M varies with the average postsynaptic activity, providing a sliding scale for LTP/LTD induction. The neural code: information is carried by the firing rate (rate coding) and by the precise spike timing (temporal coding). The Fano factor F = σ²/μ (typically ~1 for Poisson-like spiking) measures spike count variability. The balanced network theory (van Vreeswijk and Sompolinsky, 1996): cortical networks operate in a regime where excitation and inhibition are tightly balanced, producing asynchronous, irregular firing as observed in vivo.
Neural biophysics spans from single-ion-channel stochasticity to network-level dynamics; the action potential is the fundamental unit of neural communication, while synaptic plasticity underlies learning and memory.
