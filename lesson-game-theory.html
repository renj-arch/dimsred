Game Theory — The Mathematics of Strategy

Game theory studies strategic decision-making — how rational agents interact when each person's outcome depends on everyone's choices. It began with a poker problem in 1928 (von Neumann's minimax theorem) and grew into a universal language for economics, biology, political science, and artificial intelligence. At its heart is one revolutionary idea: that we can model conflict and cooperation mathematically, and find the stable outcomes that rational players will settle on.

"Game theory is the study of mathematical models of strategic interaction among rational decision-makers."
— John von Neumann & Oskar Morgenstern, Theory of Games and Economic Behavior (1944)
Part 1

🎲 Normal Form Games and Nash Equilibrium

The Prisoner's Dilemma
The Game That Changed Social Science
Two criminals are arrested and interrogated separately. Each can either cooperate (stay silent) or defect (betray the other). If both cooperate, they each get 1 year. If one defects and the other cooperates, the defector goes free and the cooperator gets 5 years. If both defect, they each get 3 years. What should a rational player do? Defecting gives a strictly better outcome regardless of what the other does — it's a dominant strategy. So both defect and get 3 years, even though mutual cooperation (1 year each) would make both better off. This is the tragedy of the prisoner's dilemma: individual rationality leads to collective disaster. It explains arms races, price wars, the tragedy of the commons, and why cooperation is so fragile — and so remarkable when it emerges.
What's rational for each person individually can be terrible for everyone collectively.

Payoff Matrices and Strategy Sets

A game in normal form has three components: players, strategies, and payoffs. For two players, we write a payoff matrix where rows are Player 1's strategies, columns are Player 2's, and each cell contains the payoff pair (u₁, u₂). A strategy s* is a dominant strategy if it gives a strictly higher payoff than any other strategy no matter what the opponent does. A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where no player can improve by unilaterally changing their strategy — each player's strategy is a best response to the others'. In the prisoner's dilemma, (Defect, Defect) is the unique Nash equilibrium.

Solving a 2×2 Coordination Game ▶
Consider two drivers approaching each other. Each can drive on the Left or Right. If both choose the same side, they avoid collision (payoff 1 each). If they choose different sides, they crash (payoff 0 each).

The payoff matrix:
       L    R
L: (1,1) (0,0)
R: (0,0) (1,1)

There are two pure Nash equilibria: (L,L) and (R,R). There's also a mixed equilibrium where each player randomizes 50-50 — but that gives expected payoff 0.5, worse than the pure equilibria. This is why societies converge on one convention (drive on the left in the UK, on the right in the US) — coordination problems have multiple solutions, and history decides which one we pick.

Elimination of Dominated Strategies

Not every game has a dominant strategy. But we can often simplify games by iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS). A strategy is strictly dominated if there's another strategy that always does better. Removing dominated strategies may reveal new dominated ones. This process converges to a set of rationalizable strategies — those that survive common knowledge of rationality. In some games, this uniquely identifies the outcome; in others (like coordination games), it doesn't narrow things down much.

Part 2

🎰 Mixed Strategies and the Minimax Theorem

Randomize to Win
Why Poker Players Bluff — and Why It's Mathematical
Not every game has a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium. Consider Rock-Paper-Scissors: whatever you pick, your opponent can counter it. The only stable strategy is to randomize uniformly — play each option with probability 1/3. This is a mixed strategy. Von Neumann's minimax theorem (1928) showed that in any two-player zero-sum game (one player's gain equals the other's loss), there exists a value V such that Player 1 can guarantee at least V and Player 2 can guarantee at most V. The optimal strategies are mixed, and the value V is unique. This is the birth of game theory — von Neumann proved it before he even knew what to call the field. Today, the minimax theorem underlies adversarial training in AI (GANs), security games, and poker-solving algorithms.
When every pure strategy can be beaten, randomization is the rational choice.

Mixed Strategy Nash Equilibrium

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies. A mixed strategy Nash equilibrium exists when each player's mixed strategy makes the other players indifferent among their pure strategies (so they're willing to randomize too). Nash's existence theorem (1950) proved that every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium (possibly in mixed strategies). This result — proved in Nash's 27-page PhD thesis — earned him the Nobel Prize and became the foundation of modern game theory. To find a mixed equilibrium, solve for probabilities where each player's expected payoff is equal across all pure strategies they play with positive probability.

Nash's Existence Theorem
Every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium
A brilliant application of Kakutani's fixed-point theorem to economics. Nash's proof took just two pages and changed the world.

🌳 Sequential Games and Backward Induction

The Power of Commitment
Backward Induction — Solving Games by Thinking Backward
In extensive form games, players move in sequence, and later players know what earlier players did. These are represented as game trees. The solution concept is subgame perfect equilibrium — found by backward induction: start at the terminal nodes, determine the optimal choice at each decision node, and work backward. Consider a game where you can either take $1 now or let me take $0.50 and give you $2 tomorrow. If I'm rational, I'll take the $0.50 and you get $2 — but if you don't trust me, you take the $1. The ultimatum game shows this beautifully: one player proposes a split of $10, the other accepts or rejects (both get nothing if rejected). Standard theory says the proposer offers $0.01 and the responder accepts (something > nothing). But in real life, people reject unfair offers — because humans care about fairness, not just money.
Backward induction assumes perfect rationality and perfect trust. Real humans are more complex — and more interesting.

Subgame Perfect Equilibrium

A subgame perfect equilibrium (SPE) is a strategy profile that induces a Nash equilibrium in every subgame of the original game. It eliminates non-credible threats — promises to punish that wouldn't actually be carried out. Backward induction finds SPE in finite perfect-information games. The centipede game illustrates a paradox: backward induction says the first player should defect immediately, but if both players are even slightly altruistic, they can cooperate for many rounds and both come out ahead.

Repeated Games and the Folk Theorem

When the same game is played repeatedly, cooperation can emerge even in the prisoner's dilemma. The Folk theorem says that in infinitely repeated games, any feasible payoff vector that's better for each player than their minimax value can be sustained as a Nash equilibrium — provided players are patient enough. Tit-for-tat (cooperate on the first move, then copy the opponent's previous move) is remarkably effective: it's nice, retaliatory, forgiving, and clear. In Axelrod's tournaments, tit-for-tat won against far more complex strategies.

The Folk Theorem
Any feasible, individually rational payoff can be sustained in an infinitely repeated game with sufficiently patient players
The "folk" part comes from it being common knowledge among game theorists before anyone formally proved it. It shows that repetition enables cooperation.
Tit-for-Tat in the Prisoner's Dilemma
In a repeated prisoner's dilemma with tit-for-tat, both players start by cooperating. If one defects, the other defects next round — punishment is immediate but reversible. This creates a stable cooperative equilibrium as long as players care enough about future payoffs (formalized by the discount factor δ > 1/2 for typical payoffs).

🌍 Evolution, Auctions, and Mechanism Design

Darwin's Game
Evolutionary Game Theory — Survival of the Fittest Strategy
John Maynard Smith realized that game theory applies not just to rational humans but to evolving populations. An evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS) is a strategy such that if the whole population uses it, no mutant strategy can invade. The classic example: in a population of aggressive Hawks and peaceful Doves, the ESS is a mix where the payoff for playing Hawk equals the payoff for playing Dove. This explains why animals don't always fight to the death — ritualized displays (Dove behavior) persist because they're evolutionarily stable. The Hawk-Dove game models territorial disputes, mate competition, and even corporate pricing strategies. Evolutionary game theory replaces rationality with selection — strategies that do well proliferate; those that do poorly die out.
Rationality isn't required for strategic behavior — evolution finds the equilibrium through trial and error over generations.

Auction Theory — The Winner's Curse

Auctions are games with rules designed by the seller. In a first-price sealed-bid auction, the highest bidder pays their bid. In a second-price auction (Vickrey), the highest bidder pays the second-highest bid — which makes truthful bidding a dominant strategy (you should bid exactly what the item is worth to you). The revenue equivalence theorem says that under certain conditions, all standard auction formats yield the same expected revenue. The winner's curse occurs in common-value auctions (like oil drilling rights): the winner tends to be the one who overestimated the value, so winning the auction is bad news. Sophisticated bidders shade their bids downward to avoid this — but that's a strategic choice, and getting it wrong can bankrupt you.

Mechanism Design — Reverse Game Theory

In mechanism design, you design the rules of the game to achieve a desired outcome, assuming players will act strategically. The revelation principle says any outcome achievable by some mechanism can also be achieved by a direct revelation mechanism where players truthfully report their private information. The Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism generalizes the Vickrey auction to any social choice problem — it makes truthful reporting a dominant strategy and achieves efficient outcomes. VCG mechanisms are used in spectrum auctions, keyword advertising auctions (Google AdWords), and even kidney exchange programs. The challenge: VCG can run deficits, and designing budget-balanced mechanisms that are also strategy-proof is the subject of active research.

Nash's Nobel — A Life of Genius and Struggle ▶
John Nash proved the existence of equilibrium in 1950 when he was just 21 years old. His proof used Kakutani's fixed-point theorem — a deep result from topology — to show that in any finite game, there's a set of strategies where no player wants to deviate.

Nash later developed a severe mental illness (paranoid schizophrenia) and spent decades in and out of hospitals. He recovered remarkably in the 1990s and won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1994. His life was portrayed in the film "A Beautiful Mind" (2001).

Tragically, Nash and his wife died in a car crash in 2015, returning from Norway where he'd just received the Abel Prize — mathematics' highest honor.

Bayesian Games and Incomplete Information

In many strategic situations players do not know the exact payoff functions of their opponents. A Bayesian game models this by introducing a set of types for each player, a common prior probability distribution over type profiles, and payoff functions that depend on both actions and types. Each player observes his own type but not the types of others, and chooses a strategy that maps his type to an action. A Bayesian Nash equilibrium (BNE) is a profile of type‑contingent strategies such that each player's strategy maximizes his expected payoff given the strategies of the others and the prior.

Key components

  • Type space Θᵢ for each player i, finite or continuous.
  • Prior μ over Θ = ×ᵢ Θᵢ, common knowledge.
  • Action sets Aᵢ.
  • Payoff uᵢ(a, θ) where a ∈ ×ᵢ Aᵢ and θ ∈ Θ.

Existence and computation

Harsanyi (1967‑68) showed that any Bayesian game can be transformed into an equivalent normal‑form game with incomplete information by expanding the strategy space to functions from types to actions. By applying Kakutani’s fixed‑point theorem to this expanded game, a BNE exists in mixed strategies whenever type and action spaces are finite. Computing a BNE often reduces to solving a system of best‑response correspondences; for two‑player zero‑sum Bayesian games the minimax theorem extends, yielding a value that can be found by linear programming.

Applications

Auction theory (private‑value vs common‑value models), mechanism design with private information, and signaling games (Spence’s job‑market model) are all framed as Bayesian games. In a first‑price sealed‑bid auction with independent private values, the symmetric BNE bidding function is derived from the first‑order condition of expected utility maximization.

Correlated Equilibrium and Communication

A correlated equilibrium (Aumann, 1974) relaxes the independence requirement of mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium by allowing a correlation device — a public random signal — to recommend actions to players. Formally, a probability distribution σ over action profiles a ∈ A is a correlated equilibrium if for every player i and every pair of actions aᵢ, aᵢ′ with σ(aᵢ) > 0, the conditional expected payoff from obeying the recommendation aᵢ is at least as high as deviating to aᵢ′, given the recommendation. Every Nash equilibrium (mixed or pure) is a correlated equilibrium, but the converse need not hold; correlated equilibria can achieve higher total welfare.

Characterization via linear inequalities

The set of correlated equilibria of a finite game is a convex polytope described by linear incentive constraints: for all i, aᵢ, aᵢ′, Σa₋ᵢ σ(aᵢ, a₋ᵢ)[uᵢ(aᵢ, a₋ᵢ) − uᵢ(aᵢ′, a₋ᵢ)] ≥ 0. This polytope can be computed in polynomial time using the ellipsoid method or interior‑point algorithms, whereas finding a Nash equilibrium is PPAD‑complete.

Communication and cheap talk

When players can exchange costless messages before playing (cheap talk), the set of equilibrium outcomes expands to the communication equilibrium (Forges, 1986). In games with common interests, cheap talk can implement any correlated equilibrium; with conflicting interests, only a subset is achievable. The classic “Battle of the Sexes” with a public coin toss illustrates how a correlation device yields the fair (½,½) payoff, which is not a Nash equilibrium of the base game.

Worked Example: Correlated Equilibrium in Chicken
Consider the Chicken game with payoff matrix (Row, Column):
Swerve‑Swerve (0,0), Swerve‑Straight (−1,1), Straight‑Swerve (1,−1), Straight‑Straight (−10,−10).
Find a correlated equilibrium that yields higher expected payoff than any mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium.
The mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium has each player play Straight with probability 1/11, giving expected payoff −10/11 ≈ −0.91. Define a correlation device that recommends (Swerve, Straight) with probability ½ and (Straight, Swerve) with probability ½. If a player receives “Swerve”, the conditional distribution of the opponent’s action is Straight with probability 1, so deviating to Straight yields −10 < 0; obeying yields 0. If a player receives “Straight”, the opponent plays Swerve with probability 1, so deviating to Swerve yields 0 < 1; obeying yields 1. Both incentive constraints hold, so this distribution is a correlated equilibrium. Expected payoff for each player = ½·0 + ½·1 = 0.5, strictly higher than the Nash payoff.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “an equilibrium that improves on Nash”, check whether a public correlation device is allowed. Write down the linear incentive constraints; a simple symmetric recommendation often suffices. Remember that every Nash equilibrium is a correlated equilibrium, so the set of correlated equilibria is never empty.

Cooperative Game Theory and the Shapley Value

Cooperative game theory studies how groups of players (coalitions) can share the total surplus they generate. A transferable‑utility (TU) game is a pair (N, v) where N = {1,…,n} is the player set and v: 2ᴺ → ℝ with v(∅)=0 assigns a worth to each coalition. The central solution concepts are the core, the Shapley value, and the nucleolus.

The core

An allocation x ∈ ℝⁿ (with Σᵢ xᵢ = v(N)) belongs to the core if for every coalition S ⊆ N, Σᵢ∈S xᵢ ≥ v(S). The core is the set of imputations that no coalition can improve upon. The Bondareva‑Shapley theorem states that the core is non‑empty iff the game is balanced (every balanced collection of coalitions has weights summing to at most v(N)). Many market games (e.g., assignment games) have non‑empty cores.

The Shapley value

The Shapley value φ(v) distributes v(N) according to each player’s average marginal contribution over all possible orders of coalition formation: φᵢ(v) = Σ_{S⊆N{i}} (|S|! (n−|S|−1)! / n!) [v(S∪{i}) − v(S)]. It satisfies four axioms: efficiency, symmetry, dummy player, and additivity. The Shapley value is unique with these properties and lies in the core whenever the core is non‑empty and the game is convex (supermodular).

Applications

Cost‑sharing in airport runway construction, voting power indices (Shapley‑Shubik index), and profit allocation in joint ventures. In a three‑player airport game with costs c₁ ≤ c₂ ≤ c₃, the Shapley value allocates incremental costs proportionally to the order in which players “arrive”.

Stochastic Games and Markov Perfect Equilibrium

A stochastic game (Shapley, 1953) extends repeated games by allowing the stage‑game payoff and the transition to the next state to depend on the current state and the action profile. Formally, a stochastic game consists of a finite state space Ω, action sets Aᵢ for each player, stage payoffs uᵢ(ω, a), and transition probabilities p(ω′ | ω, a). Players discount future payoffs with factor δ ∈ (0,1). A Markov perfect equilibrium (MPE) is a profile of strategies that depend only on the current state (not on the full history) and form a subgame‑perfect equilibrium in every state.

Existence and computation

For finite stochastic games with discounted payoffs, an MPE in stationary strategies exists (Fink, 1964). The equilibrium conditions reduce to a system of Bellman optimality equations: for each state ω, the value vector V(ω) satisfies Vᵢ(ω) = maxaᵢ mina₋ᵢ [uᵢ(ω, a) + δ Σω′ p(ω′|ω,a) Vᵢ(ω′)] for zero‑sum games, and a set of coupled best‑response conditions for general‑sum games. Numerical methods (value iteration, policy iteration, linear programming for zero‑sum) compute MPEs.

Applications

Dynamic oligopoly with capacity investment, natural resource extraction, and Markov decision processes in reinforcement learning. In a duopoly where firms choose capacities and the market state is total capacity, MPE captures the strategic effect of today’s investment on tomorrow’s competition.

Network Games and Strategic Complementarities

Network games model situations where each player’s payoff depends on his own action and the actions of his neighbors in a fixed graph G = (N, E). A common specification is the linear‑quadratic payoff: uᵢ(x) = a xᵢ − ½ b xᵢ² + Σj∈Nᵢ c xᵢ xⱼ, where Nᵢ are i’s neighbors, a,b > 0, and c captures strategic interaction. If c > 0 the game exhibits strategic complementarities (higher neighbor actions raise marginal returns); if c < 0 it exhibits strategic substitutes.

Equilibrium characterization

When the best‑response functions are linear, the Nash equilibrium solves (I − (c/b) A) x = (a/b) 1, where A is the adjacency matrix. A unique interior equilibrium exists iff the spectral radius ρ((c/b) A) < 1. The equilibrium action of player i is proportional to his Bonacich centrality in the network, linking network position to strategic influence.

Policy implications

In crime networks, removing a high‑centrality node reduces aggregate activity more than removing a peripheral node. In technology adoption with complementarities, seeding early adopters at central positions maximizes diffusion. The framework extends to incomplete information (Bayesian network games) and to dynamic settings where the network evolves.

  • Confusing Bayesian Nash equilibrium with perfect Bayesian equilibrium — the latter requires sequential rationality and consistent beliefs at every information set.
  • Assuming every correlated equilibrium can be implemented by cheap talk — only communication equilibria are reachable without a mediator.
  • Treating the Shapley value as always in the core — it lies in the core only for convex games.
  • Neglecting the spectral radius condition in network games — without ρ < 1 the linear system has no finite solution.
  • Overlooking the stationarity requirement in Markov perfect equilibrium — history‑dependent strategies are not MPE.
  • Misapplying the revenue equivalence theorem to common‑value auctions — it holds only under independent private values and risk neutrality.
  • Confusing the core with the set of Pareto‑efficient allocations — the core also requires coalitional stability.

These advanced concepts deepen the strategic toolkit and appear frequently in higher‑level quantitative and analytical questions.

Evolutionarily Stable Strategies and Replicator Dynamics

Evolutionary game theory shifts the focus from rational choice to population dynamics. Instead of assuming players compute best responses, it assumes a large population of agents each hard‑wired to play a fixed strategy. Strategies that earn higher payoffs replicate faster — either through biological reproduction, social imitation, or economic selection. The central solution concept is the evolutionarily stable strategy (ESS), introduced by Maynard Smith and Price (1973). A strategy σ* is an ESS if, for every mutant strategy σ ≠ σ*, either (i) u(σ*, σ*) > u(σ, σ*) or (ii) u(σ*, σ*) = u(σ, σ*) and u(σ*, σ) > u(σ, σ). The first condition means σ* is a strict Nash equilibrium against itself; the second handles neutral mutants by requiring σ* to outperform them when they interact. Every ESS is a symmetric Nash equilibrium, but not every symmetric Nash equilibrium is an ESS — the Hawk‑Dove game illustrates this. With payoff matrix (V−C)/2 for Hawk‑Hawk, V/2 for Hawk‑Dove, 0 for Dove‑Hawk, and V/2 for Dove‑Dove (V = resource value, C = cost of fight), the unique symmetric mixed equilibrium p* = V/C is an ESS when V < C. When V ≥ C, pure Hawk is the unique ESS.

The dynamic counterpart is the replicator equation. Let xᵢ be the share of strategy i in the population, with fitness fᵢ(x) = Σⱼ aᵢⱼ xⱼ and average fitness f̄(x) = Σᵢ xᵢ fᵢ(x). The continuous‑time replicator dynamics are dxᵢ/dt = xᵢ [fᵢ(x) − f̄(x)]. Rest points of this system are exactly the symmetric Nash equilibria. An ESS is locally asymptotically stable under the replicator dynamics (Taylor and Jonker, 1978). The folk theorem of evolutionary game theory states that for any game, the set of Lyapunov‑stable rest points coincides with the set of ESSs when the dynamics are sufficiently smooth. In the Rock‑Paper‑Scissors game with cyclic dominance, the unique mixed equilibrium is neutrally stable but not an ESS; the replicator dynamics produce closed orbits around it. Adding a small mutation rate μ > 0 yields the replicator‑mutator equation dxᵢ/dt = Σⱼ xⱼ fⱼ(x) Qⱼᵢ − xᵢ f̄(x), where Q is the mutation matrix. This selects the unique stationary distribution that maximizes entropy subject to fitness constraints, linking evolutionary stability to information theory.

Applications in biology and economics

In biology, ESS explains sex ratios (Fisher, 1930), territorial behavior (Bourgeois strategy), and the maintenance of genetic polymorphism. In economics, the replicator dynamics model technology adoption, convention formation, and the evolution of preferences. The Bergin‑Lipman (1996) result shows that with sufficiently rich mutation structures, any strict Nash equilibrium can be made the unique stochastically stable state. This undermines the predictive power of stochastic stability unless the mutation process is disciplined by empirical evidence. The Kandori‑Mailath‑Rob (1993) and Young (1993) framework uses log‑linear choice with noise to select risk‑dominant equilibria in coordination games, providing a micro‑foundation for equilibrium selection that connects to the ESS concept.

Bargaining Theory: Nash Solution and Rubinstein Alternating Offers

Bargaining theory models how two or more players divide a surplus when cooperation is beneficial but the division is contested. The Nash bargaining solution (1950) is an axiomatic approach. Given a feasible set S ⊂ ℝ² of utility pairs (u₁, u₂) that is compact, convex, and contains a point strictly improving on the disagreement point d = (d₁, d₂), the Nash solution maximizes the product (u₁ − d₁)(u₂ − d₂) over S. It satisfies four axioms: Pareto efficiency (no other feasible point makes both better off), symmetry (if S is symmetric and d₁ = d₂, the solution gives equal payoffs), invariance to affine transformations (utility is unique up to positive affine transforms), and independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) (if the solution lies in a subset T ⊂ S, it remains the solution for T). The Nash solution coincides with the utilitarian maximizer of the Nash product and, for two players, with the Kalai‑Smorodinsky solution only when S is symmetric.

The Rubinstein (1982) alternating‑offers model provides a strategic foundation. Two players bargain over a pie of size 1. Player 1 makes an offer x ∈ [0,1] (player 1 gets x, player 2 gets 1−x). If player 2 accepts, the game ends; if player 2 rejects, the game moves to the next period with roles reversed, and the pie shrinks by discount factors δ₁, δ₂ ∈ (0,1). In the unique subgame‑perfect equilibrium, player 1 offers x* = (1 − δ₂) / (1 − δ₁δ₂) and player 2 accepts immediately. As δ₁, δ₂ → 1 (players become patient), x* → 1/2, recovering the symmetric Nash solution with d = (0,0). If discount factors differ, the more patient player (higher δ) receives a larger share. The Rubinstein model assumes complete information, no outside options, and a fixed surplus. Extensions introduce outside options (Binmore, Shaked, Sutton, 1989): if a player can walk away to a known outside payoff, the equilibrium shifts only when the outside option is binding — i.e., when it exceeds the player's continuation value in the Rubinstein game. Incomplete information (Fudenberg, Levine, Tirole, 1985) introduces screening and signaling; the Coase conjecture states that with a durable good and a continuum of buyer types, the seller's ability to commit vanishes as the period length shrinks, leading to immediate trade at the competitive price.

Multi‑issue bargaining and the Nash program

When players bargain over multiple issues (e.g., price, quantity, quality), the feasible set becomes a polytope. The Nash solution generalizes directly, but strategic models face linkage effects: issues can be bundled or separated. Fatouros (1976) and Inderst (2000) show that sequential bargaining over issues can yield different outcomes than simultaneous bargaining, depending on the correlation of preferences. The Nash program (Nash, 1953) seeks to embed axiomatic solutions as equilibria of explicit non‑cooperative games. For the Nash bargaining solution, this is achieved by the Rubinstein game with a breakdown probability (Binmore, 1987) or by the demand‑commitment game (Nash, 1953). The program remains incomplete for n > 2 players; the Shapley value has strategic foundations via the Hart‑Mas‑Colell (1996) bidding mechanism, but no simple alternating‑offers game implements the Shapley value for general characteristic‑function games.

Worked Example: Rubinstein Bargaining with Asymmetric Discounting
Two players bargain over a pie of size 1 using the Rubinstein alternating‑offers protocol. Player 1 discounts future payoffs by δ₁ = 0.9 per period; player 2 discounts by δ₂ = 0.8 per period. Player 1 makes the first offer. Compute the subgame‑perfect equilibrium offer x* that player 1 makes in period 1, and verify that player 2 accepts immediately.
In the Rubinstein model with discount factors δ₁, δ₂, the unique SPE offer by the first mover is x* = (1 − δ₂) / (1 − δ₁δ₂). Substitute δ₁ = 0.9, δ₂ = 0.8: denominator = 1 − (0.9 × 0.8) = 1 − 0.72 = 0.28. Numerator = 1 − 0.8 = 0.2. Thus x* = 0.2 / 0.28 = 5/7 ≈ 0.7143. Player 1 receives 5/7, player 2 receives 2/7. To verify acceptance: if player 2 rejects, the game moves to period 2 where player 2 makes an offer y. By stationarity, player 2's optimal offer gives player 1 exactly δ₁ × (player 1's continuation value) = δ₁ × x* = 0.9 × (5/7) = 4.5/7, so player 2 would keep 1 − 4.5/7 = 2.5/7. Player 2's payoff from rejecting is δ₂ × (2.5/7) = 0.8 × 2.5/7 = 2/7, exactly equal to the payoff from accepting x* = 5/7 (which gives player 2 a share of 2/7). Since player 2 is indifferent, accepting is a best response. The SPE is (offer 5/7, accept).
Exam‑Day Strategy: Bargaining Power and Discount Factors
In Rubinstein bargaining, the player with the higher discount factor (more patient) gets a larger share. Memorize the formula x* = (1 − δ₂) / (1 − δ₁δ₂) for the first mover's share. A common trap: reversing δ₁ and δ₂. Remember δ₁ belongs to the player who makes the FIRST offer. If the question asks for player 2's share when player 1 moves first, compute 1 − x* = δ₂(1 − δ₁) / (1 − δ₁δ₂). For the limit as δ₁, δ₂ → 1, use L'Hôpital or symmetry: the limit is 1/2 regardless of who moves first, provided both discount factors approach 1 at the same rate. If only one player becomes patient (δ₁ → 1, δ₂ fixed < 1), the patient player captures the entire surplus: x* → 1.

Mechanism Design: Vickrey‑Clarke‑Groves Mechanisms and Revenue Equivalence

Mechanism design reverses the usual game‑theoretic question: instead of analyzing a given game, we design a game (mechanism) whose equilibrium outcomes implement a desired social choice function. The Vickrey‑Clarke‑Groves (VCG) mechanism is the cornerstone for efficient allocation in settings with private values. Consider a set of alternatives A, n agents with quasi‑linear utilities uᵢ(a, θᵢ) = vᵢ(a, θᵢ) − tᵢ, where θᵢ is agent i's private type, vᵢ is the valuation, and tᵢ is a transfer. A direct revelation mechanism asks each agent to report a type θ̂ᵢ. The VCG mechanism chooses the alternative a* that maximizes the reported social welfare Σᵢ vᵢ(a, θ̂ᵢ) and charges each agent the externality they impose on others: tᵢ(θ̂) = Σⱼ≠ᵢ vⱼ(a*−ᵢ, θ̂−ᵢ) − Σⱼ≠ᵢ vⱼ(a*, θ̂), where a*−ᵢ is the welfare‑maximizing choice when agent i is excluded. Truth‑telling is a dominant strategy for every agent (DSIC), and the outcome is ex‑post efficient. The Clarke pivot rule sets the constant term so that tᵢ ≥ 0 (no agent pays to participate) and tᵢ = 0 if the agent is not pivotal. VCG generalizes the second‑price (Vickrey) auction to arbitrary social choice problems: public project selection, combinatorial auctions, matching markets.

The Revenue Equivalence Theorem (Myerson, 1981; Riley and Samuelson, 1981) states that in independent private‑values environments with risk‑neutral bidders, any two standard auction formats (first‑price, second‑price, English, Dutch) that allocate the object to the highest‑value bidder and give zero expected payoff to the lowest‑value type yield the same expected revenue for the seller. The proof uses the envelope theorem: a bidder's expected utility U(v) = max_b E[u(v, b)] satisfies U'(v) = Pr(win | v), so U(v) = U(v̲) + ∫v̲^v Pr(win | x) dx. Expected revenue is E[v − U(v)] = E[v] − U(v̲) − E[∫ Pr(win) dx]. Since the allocation rule (who wins) is the same across formats, expected revenue is identical. The theorem fails under common values (Milgrom and Weber, 1982), risk aversion (Holt, 1980), correlated types (Cremer and McLean, 1988), or budget constraints (Che and Gale, 1998). In common‑value auctions, the winner's curse makes first‑price auctions yield higher revenue than second‑price because bidders shade more aggressively to avoid overpaying.

Optimal auction design and the Myerson auction

Myerson (1981) characterizes the revenue‑maximizing auction for a single item with independent private values drawn from distributions Fᵢ with densities fᵢ. Define the virtual valuation φᵢ(v) = v − (1 − Fᵢ(v)) / fᵢ(v). The optimal auction allocates the item to the bidder with the highest virtual valuation, provided it is non‑negative; otherwise the seller keeps the item. Payments are determined by the envelope condition. For symmetric bidders with regular distributions (φ increasing), the optimal auction is a second‑price auction with a reserve price r* satisfying φ(r*) = 0. For asymmetric bidders, the optimal auction discriminates: it may allocate to a bidder with a lower value but higher virtual valuation, violating ex‑post efficiency. The Cremer‑McLean (1988) result shows that with correlated types, the seller can extract the full surplus by using a mechanism that charges each type a lottery over payments, effectively screening the correlation structure. This requires the seller to know the joint distribution exactly — a strong assumption.

Algorithmic Game Theory: Price of Anarchy and Congestion Games

Algorithmic game theory studies the efficiency of equilibria in games that model large‑scale distributed systems — routing, load balancing, resource allocation. The central metric is the price of anarchy (PoA) (Koutsoupias and Papadimitriou, 1999), defined as the ratio of the worst‑case equilibrium cost to the optimal (centrally planned) cost: PoA = maxequilibrium σ C(σ) / minfeasible x C(x). For maximization problems (welfare), PoA = minequilibrium W(σ) / maxfeasible W(x) ≤ 1. A PoA of 1 means equilibria are efficient; a PoA of 4/3 means equilibria can be 33% worse than optimum. Congestion games (Rosenthal, 1973) are the canonical model. There is a set of resources E, each with a latency function ℓₑ(x) giving the delay when x players use resource e. Each player i chooses a subset of resources (a path, a server, a frequency band) from a strategy set Sᵢ ⊆ 2^E. The cost to player i is Σe∈sᵢ ℓₑ(nₑ), where nₑ is the number of players using e. Rosenthal proved that every congestion game has a pure‑strategy Nash equilibrium by exhibiting the Rosenthal potential function Φ(s) = Σₑ Σk=1nₑ ℓₑ(k). Any unilateral deviation that reduces a player's cost also reduces Φ, so best‑response dynamics converge to a pure equilibrium.

For affine latency functions ℓₑ(x) = aₑ x + bₑ (aₑ, bₑ ≥ 0), the PoA for pure Nash equilibria is exactly 5/2 (Roughgarden and Tardos, 2002). For general non‑decreasing latencies, the PoA is unbounded. However, for mixed Nash equilibria and correlated equilibria, the PoA is at most 5/2 for affine latencies and at most 2.618... for general latencies (Christodoulou and Koutsoupias, 2005). The smoothness framework (Roughgarden, 2009) gives a unified method: a game is (λ, μ)‑smooth if for any two strategy profiles s, s*, Σᵢ Cᵢ(sᵢ*, s−ᵢ) ≤ λ C(s*) + μ C(s). If a game is (λ, μ)‑smooth with μ < 1, then the PoA for coarse correlated equilibria is at most λ / (1 − μ). Congestion games with affine latencies are (5/3, 1/3)‑smooth, yielding PoA ≤ 5/2. This framework extends to auctions (smoothness of first‑price auctions gives PoA ≤ 2 for Bayes‑Nash equilibria) and matching markets.

Atomic vs. nonatomic congestion and Braess's paradox

In nonatomic (Wardrop) congestion games, each player is infinitesimal; the equilibrium condition is that all used paths have equal and minimal latency. The PoA for nonatomic games with affine latencies is 4/3 (Roughgarden and Tardos, 2002), strictly better than the atomic 5/2. Braess's paradox (1968) demonstrates that adding a new road to a network can increase equilibrium travel time for everyone. In a four‑node network with latencies ℓ(x) = x on two parallel paths and a zero‑latency cross link, the equilibrium flow shifts to the cross link, creating congestion that raises total latency from 1.5 to 2. This paradox disappears in nonatomic games with constant‑elasticity latencies but persists in atomic games. The paradox has practical implications: closing roads can improve traffic flow, a counter‑intuitive policy lever used in Seoul (Cheonggyecheon restoration) and Stuttgart.

Matching Theory: Gale‑Shapley Algorithm and Stable Matchings

Matching theory studies the formation of pairwise matches between two sides of a market — students and schools, doctors and hospitals, men and women in marriage markets — when transfers (prices) are absent or restricted. The Gale‑Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm (1962) solves the stable marriage problem. Given n men and n women, each with a strict preference ordering over the opposite side, a matching μ is a bijection. A pair (m, w) blocks μ if m prefers w to μ(m) and w prefers m to μ(w). A matching is stable if no blocking pair exists. The men‑proposing deferred acceptance algorithm: each unengaged man proposes to his most‑preferred woman who has not yet rejected him; each woman holds her most‑preferred proposer and rejects the rest; rejected men continue proposing. The algorithm terminates in at most n² steps with a stable matching. The resulting matching is man‑optimal: every man gets his best possible partner among all stable matchings, and every woman gets her worst possible partner among all stable matchings. The woman‑proposing version yields the woman‑optimal stable matching. The set of stable matchings forms a distributive lattice (Conway, Knuth): if μ and μ' are stable, then the matchings defined by μ ∨ μ' (each man gets his more preferred partner) and μ ∧ μ' (each man gets his less preferred partner) are also stable.

The Rural Hospitals Theorem (Roth, 1986) states that in many‑to‑one matching (hospitals with capacities), the set of doctors assigned to rural hospitals is the same in every stable matching. This explains why rural hospitals cannot fill positions by changing the algorithm — the shortage is structural. The strategy‑proofness of deferred acceptance: for the proposing side, truthful reporting of preferences is a dominant strategy (Dubins and Freedman, 1981; Roth, 1982). For the receiving side, manipulation is possible — a woman can misrepresent preferences to get a better stable match. However, no stable mechanism is strategy‑proof for both sides simultaneously (Roth, 1982). The top trading cycles (TTC) algorithm (Shapley and Scarf, 1974) solves the house allocation problem with existing tenants: each agent points to their most‑preferred house; cycles are identified and cleared; remaining agents repeat. TTC yields the unique core allocation and is strategy‑proof.

School choice and kidney exchange

In school choice (Abdulkadiroğlu and Sönmez, 2003), students have priorities at schools (siblings, walk‑zone, lottery). The student‑proposing deferred acceptance mechanism is strategy‑proof for students and respects priorities (no justified envy). The Boston mechanism (immediate acceptance) is not strategy‑proof and was replaced by DA in Boston (2005) and New York (2003). Kidney exchange (Roth, Sönmez, Ünver, 2004) uses matching theory to arrange cycles and chains of incompatible donor‑patient pairs. The problem is a maximum‑weight cycle cover in a directed compatibility graph. Long cycles are logistically difficult; in practice, 2‑ and 3‑way cycles plus altruistic‑donor‑initiated chains are used. The National Kidney Registry and UNOS implement these algorithms, saving thousands of lives annually. The design challenge is balancing efficiency (maximizing transplants) with incentive compatibility (hospitals truthfully reporting patient compatibility) and fairness (equitable access across blood types and sensitized patients).

  • Assuming the Rubinstein bargaining limit δ → 1 always gives 50‑50 split — this holds only when both players' discount factors approach 1 at the same rate; if δ₁ → 1 faster than δ₂, player 1 gets the whole pie.
  • Applying the Revenue Equivalence Theorem to common‑value or correlated‑value auctions — the theorem requires independent private values and risk neutrality; with common values, first‑price auctions typically yield higher revenue.
  • Believing VCG mechanisms are budget‑balanced — VCG runs a deficit in general (the Clarke pivot rule ensures individual rationality but not budget balance); the Groves mechanism with a different constant can balance the budget but loses dominant‑strategy incentive compatibility.

Information Design and Bayesian Persuasion

Information design studies how a sender (designer) can influence a receiver's decisions by choosing what information to reveal. The canonical model (Kamenica and Gentzkow, 2011) has a sender and a receiver with a common prior μ₀ over a finite state space Ω. The sender commits to a signaling policy π: Ω → Δ(S) mapping each state to a distribution over signals. Upon observing signal s, the receiver updates to posterior μₛ via Bayes' rule and chooses an action a ∈ A maximizing expected utility. The sender's payoff depends on the state and the receiver's action. The sender's problem is to choose a distribution of posteriors τ ∈ Δ(Δ(Ω)) that is Bayes-plausible — i.e., 𝔼_τ[μ] = μ₀ — to maximize expected payoff. The solution uses the concavification of the sender's value function V(μ) = maxₐ 𝔼_μ[u_S(a, ω)]. The optimal payoff is the concave envelope V*(μ₀) evaluated at the prior. If V is already concave at μ₀, no information revelation is optimal (pooling). If V is strictly convex at μ₀, full revelation is optimal. In general, the optimal policy splits the prior into two posteriors on the supporting hyperplane of the concave envelope. This framework extends to multiple receivers (matroid constraints, Bergemann and Morris, 2016), dynamic persuasion (Ely, 2017; Ely and Szydlowski, 2020), and competition among senders (Gentzkow and Kamenica, 2017). A key insight: the sender never benefits from signals that are not Bayes-plausible, and the receiver's best response can be assumed pure without loss. The value of commitment is essential — without commitment, the game reduces to cheap talk (Crawford and Sobel, 1982) where only babbling equilibria may exist if preferences are misaligned.

Global Games and Equilibrium Selection

Global games (Carlsson and van Damme, 1993; Morris and Shin, 1998, 2000, 2003) resolve equilibrium multiplicity in coordination games by introducing small incomplete information. Consider a two-player binary-action game with payoffs: both invest (I,I) yields (1,1) if θ ≥ θ*, else (0,0); both not invest (N,N) yields (0,0); mismatched actions yield (0,0) for the investor and (0,0) for the non-investor. Under complete information with common knowledge of θ, there are two pure Nash equilibria for θ ∈ (0,1): (I,I) and (N,N). In the global game, each player i observes a private signal xᵢ = θ + εᵢ where εᵢ ~ Uniform[-ε, ε] independent. As ε → 0, the game has a unique rationalizable strategy profile: a threshold strategy where player invests iff xᵢ ≥ x* for some x* ∈ (0,1). The threshold solves the indifference condition: the probability that the other player invests, conditional on xᵢ = x*, equals the critical probability p* = 1/2 (for symmetric payoffs). The proof uses iterated elimination of dominated strategies: for very low signals, not investing is dominant; this eliminates investing for slightly higher signals, and so on, converging to a unique threshold. The selected equilibrium is risk-dominant (Harsanyi and Selten, 1988) — the one with larger basin of attraction under best-response dynamics. Applications include currency crises (Morris and Shin, 1998), bank runs (Goldstein and Pauzner, 2005), debt pricing (Morris and Shin, 2004), and technology adoption. The uniqueness result requires: (i) supermodularity (strategic complementarities), (ii) state monotonicity (higher θ favors investment), (iii) limit dominance (actions are dominant for extreme signals), and (iv) noise independence. With correlated noise or common values, multiplicity may return (Angeletos and Werning, 2006). The global game selection is robust to higher-order beliefs and provides a foundation for the "risk-dominance" refinement.

Worked Example: Binary-Action Global Game
Two investors decide whether to attack a currency peg. The peg holds if θ ≥ θ* where θ ~ Uniform[0,1]. Each investor i observes xᵢ = θ + εᵢ, εᵢ ~ Uniform[-0.1, 0.1] independent. Payoff from attacking is 1 if the other attacks and θ < θ*, else 0. Payoff from not attacking is 0. Find the unique threshold equilibrium as ε → 0.
Step 1: Identify the critical probability. Attack is a best response iff Pr(other attacks | xᵢ) ≥ p* where p* = 1/2 (since payoff from successful attack is 1, from failed attack is 0, from not attacking is 0). Step 2: As ε → 0, the signal xᵢ ≈ θ. The other investor attacks iff xⱼ ≥ x*. Given xᵢ, the conditional distribution of xⱼ is approximately Uniform[xᵢ - 2ε, xᵢ + 2ε]. Pr(xⱼ ≥ x* | xᵢ) = max(0, min(1, (xᵢ + 2ε - x*)/(4ε))). Step 3: Indifference at threshold x*: Pr(xⱼ ≥ x* | xᵢ = x*) = 1/2. This gives (x* + 2ε - x*)/(4ε) = 1/2, which holds for any x*. But we also need the peg to break: θ < θ*. The marginal attacker has x* ≈ θ*. The unique threshold is x* = θ*. Step 4: Verify limit dominance. For xᵢ < θ* - 2ε, Pr(θ < θ*) = 1, but Pr(other attacks) = 0 → not attack dominant. For xᵢ > θ* + 2ε, Pr(θ < θ*) = 0 → not attack dominant. Iterated elimination converges to x* = θ*. The unique equilibrium: attack iff xᵢ ≥ θ*.
Exam-Day Strategy
For global games questions, always check the four sufficient conditions: supermodularity, state monotonicity, limit dominance, and noise independence. If asked to compute the threshold, write the indifference condition Pr(opponent plays high action | signal = x*) = critical probability. The critical probability is (payoff of safe action - payoff of risky action when opponent plays low) / (payoff of risky action when opponent plays high - payoff of risky action when opponent plays low). For binary actions with payoffs (1,1) for (I,I), (0,0) for (N,N), (0,0) for (I,N), (0,0) for (N,I), the critical probability is 1/2. Remember: the selected equilibrium is the risk-dominant one, not necessarily the payoff-dominant one.

Mean Field Games

Mean field games (MFG) analyze strategic interactions among a continuum of infinitesimal agents (Lasry and Lions, 2006, 2007; Huang, Caines, and Malhamé, 2006, 2007). Each agent controls a state process dXₜ = αₜ dt + σ dWₜ, choosing control αₜ to minimize expected cost 𝔼[∫₀ᵀ f(Xₜ, αₜ, mₜ) dt + g(X_T, m_T)] where mₜ is the distribution of states across the population. The key assumption: an individual agent's payoff depends on others only through the aggregate distribution mₜ (mean field). In equilibrium, (i) given the flow of distributions {mₜ}, each agent solves a stochastic optimal control problem yielding an optimal feedback control α*(t, x); (ii) the distribution mₜ evolves according to the Fokker-Planck (Kolmogorov forward) equation ∂ₜm = -∇·(α* m) + (σ²/2)Δm with initial condition m₀. This yields a coupled system: a Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (HJB) equation backward in time for the value function u(t, x), and a Fokker-Planck equation forward in time for m(t, x). The Nash certainty equivalence principle states that a solution to this coupled system constitutes an ε-Nash equilibrium for the N-player game, with ε → 0 as N → ∞. Existence and uniqueness hold under monotonicity conditions (Lasry-Lions): ∫ (f(x, m) - f(x, m'))(m - m') dx ≥ 0. Applications include crowd motion, financial markets (systemic risk, mean field games of controls), energy markets, and epidemic modeling (lockdown policies). The master equation (a PDE on the space of probability measures) characterizes the value function as a function of the initial distribution, enabling analysis of propagation of chaos and convergence rates. Numerical methods include finite differences, semi-Lagrangian schemes, and deep learning (physics-informed neural networks).

Robust Mechanism Design and the Wilson Doctrine

The Wilson doctrine (Wilson, 1987) argues that practical mechanisms should not rely on precise knowledge of agents' beliefs or higher-order beliefs. Robust mechanism design (Bergemann and Morris, 2005, 2009, 2012) formalizes this: a mechanism is detail-free if it implements the desired outcome for all possible type spaces and belief hierarchies consistent with common knowledge of rationality. The central result: a social choice function is implementable in dominant strategies on all type spaces iff it is implementable in dominant strategies on the universal type space (which contains all possible belief hierarchies). For independent private values, the Vickrey-Clarke-Groves (VCG) mechanism is detail-free and efficient. For interdependent values, efficient dominant-strategy implementation is generally impossible (Jehiel and Moldovanu, 2001). The robust approach uses maxmin expected utility (Gilboa and Schmeidler, 1989): the designer maximizes the worst-case expected payoff over a set of priors. Bergemann and Morris (2005) show that a mechanism is robustly implementable (i.e., has a unique rationalizable outcome on all type spaces) iff it is implementable in dominant strategies. This connects the Wilson doctrine to the revelation principle: robust implementation = dominant-strategy implementation. The revenue equivalence theorem fails under robustness: with common values, first-price auctions yield higher revenue than second-price (Milgrom and Weber, 1982). The prior-free framework (Dhangwatnotai, Roughgarden, and Yan, 2010; Hartline and Roughgarden, 2009) evaluates mechanisms by worst-case approximation ratio over all prior distributions. For single-item auctions, the optimal prior-free mechanism is a randomized reserve price achieving a 2-approximation to the optimal Bayesian revenue. In multi-dimensional settings, robust mechanism design remains largely open; the Wilson regularity condition (single-crossing, monotone hazard rate) is often assumed to restore tractability.

Quantal Response Equilibrium and Behavioral Game Theory

Quantal response equilibrium (QRE) (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1995, 1998) models bounded rationality by assuming players choose actions with probabilities proportional to expected payoffs. In a normal-form game with payoffs uᵢ(aᵢ, a₋ᵢ), the logit QRE with precision parameter λ ≥ 0 specifies choice probabilities σᵢ(aᵢ) = exp(λ 𝔼σ₋ᵢ[uᵢ(aᵢ, a₋ᵢ)]) / Σaᵢ' exp(λ 𝔼σ₋ᵢ[uᵢ(aᵢ', a₋ᵢ)]). As λ → ∞, QRE converges to Nash equilibrium (best responses are played with probability 1). As λ → 0, QRE converges to the uniform distribution (pure noise). For intermediate λ, QRE predicts systematic deviations: players "overplay" actions with high variance in payoffs and "underplay" actions with low variance. QRE explains experimental anomalies: in the traveler's dilemma (Basu, 1994), Nash predicts the lowest claim, but QRE with λ ≈ 1.5 matches the modal claim near the middle. In centipede games, QRE predicts early exit but not immediate exit. In matching pennies, QRE predicts non-uniform mixing that matches human data. The agent QRE (AQRE) allows different λ for different information sets, capturing learning. Level-k and cognitive hierarchy models (Stahl and Wilson, 1995; Camerer, Ho, and Chong, 2004) are non-equilibrium alternatives: level-0 plays uniformly, level-k best responds to level-(k-1). The noisy introspection model (Goeree and Holt, 2004) endogenizes λ. Estimation uses maximum likelihood on experimental data; λ is typically 0.5–3.0 in laboratory games. QRE has been extended to extensive-form games (McKelvey and Palfrey, 1998), Bayesian games, and learning dynamics (stochastic fictitious play converges to QRE). A key limitation: QRE multiplicity can occur (multiple fixed points for a given λ), and the selection of λ is exogenous.

  • Assuming global games always select the risk-dominant equilibrium — uniqueness requires limit dominance, supermodularity, and independent noise; with common-value uncertainty or correlated signals, multiplicity can persist (Angeletos-Werning).
  • Treating the mean field game limit as an exact Nash equilibrium for finite N — the MFG solution is an ε-Nash equilibrium with ε = O(1/√N) under propagation of chaos; for small N, finite-population corrections matter.
  • Believing VCG is detail-free for interdependent values — VCG fails dominant-strategy incentive compatibility when values are interdependent (Jehiel-Moldovanu); robust implementation requires dominant strategies on all type spaces, which is impossible for efficient interdependent-value mechanisms.
  • Equating QRE with a single λ for all players and all games — λ is a structural parameter estimated from data; it varies across games, populations, and stakes. Agent QRE allows λ to vary by information set.
  • Overlooking the concavification step in information design — the sender's optimal payoff is the concave envelope of the value function at the prior; if the value function is already concave, no information is revealed (pooling).
  • Assuming the Wilson doctrine implies all mechanisms must be prior-free — the doctrine recommends detail-free mechanisms where possible, but prior-free worst-case analysis is a separate (stronger) criterion; many practical mechanisms (e.g., VCG) are detail-free but not prior-free optimal.

Social Choice Theory: Arrow's Impossibility Theorem and the Gibbard-Satterthwaite Theorem

Social choice theory studies how individual preferences can be aggregated into a collective decision. The foundational result is Arrow's impossibility theorem (1951), which proves that no social welfare function can simultaneously satisfy five seemingly reasonable axioms when there are at least three alternatives and unrestricted domain of preferences. The axioms are: (1) unrestricted domain — the function accepts any profile of strict preference orderings; (2) Pareto efficiency — if every individual prefers x to y, society prefers x to y; (3) independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) — the social ranking of x versus y depends only on individual rankings of x versus y; (4) non-dictatorship — no single individual's preferences always determine the social ranking; (5) transitivity — the social preference ordering is transitive. Arrow showed these five conditions are mutually incompatible. The proof proceeds by identifying a pivotal voter for some pair of alternatives, then using IIA and Pareto to show this voter is a dictator for all pairs.

The Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem (1973, 1975) extends this impossibility to social choice functions (which select a single winning alternative rather than a full ranking) and strategic manipulation. It states that any onto social choice function with at least three alternatives that is strategy-proof (dominant-strategy incentive compatible) must be dictatorial. The proof constructs a preference profile where a non-dictatorial strategy-proof function would allow manipulation via the monotonicity property: if an alternative wins at some profile, it must still win when its position improves in individual rankings. The theorem assumes unrestricted domain; restricting to single-peaked preferences (Black, 1948) or quasilinear utilities with transfers (VCG mechanisms) circumvents the impossibility. In examinations, the distinction between social welfare functions (Arrow) and social choice functions (Gibbard-Satterthwaite) is frequently tested, as is the role of the unrestricted domain assumption. The Duggan-Schwartz theorem (2000) generalizes Gibbard-Satterthwaite to correspondences (set-valued choice rules), showing that any strategy-proof, onto, non-imposing correspondence is dictatorial in a suitable sense.

Dynamic Mechanism Design: The Pivot Mechanism and Intertemporal Incentive Compatibility

Dynamic mechanism design studies settings where agents' private information evolves over time and the mechanism must elicit this information sequentially. The canonical model (Bergemann and Välimäki, 2010; Pavan, Segal, and Toikka, 2014) considers a finite horizon T where in each period t an agent receives a signal θt drawn from a distribution that may depend on past signals and actions. The agent's payoff is u(x1:T, θ1:T) − p1:T where xt is the allocation and pt the payment in period t. A mechanism is periodic ex-post incentive compatible (PEPIC) if truth-telling is a dominant strategy in every period after every history. The dynamic pivot mechanism (also called the dynamic VCG or dynamic Groves mechanism) achieves PEPIC by charging each agent the externality they impose on others: pt(θ1:t) = −∑j≠i uj(xt:T(θ1:t), θt:T) + ht(θ1:t-1, θ-i,1:t) where ht is a history-dependent term that does not depend on the current report θt. The key insight is that the dynamic pivot mechanism is efficient (maximizes social surplus in every period) and ex-post individually rational if the no-single-agent-effect condition holds: no agent's report can change the allocation for others when that agent is absent.

However, the dynamic pivot mechanism faces the ratchet effect: agents may underreport current values to lower future payments, because the mechanism's future terms ht+1 depend on current reports. This is distinct from the static VCG, where no ratchet effect exists. To restore incentive compatibility, the mechanism must satisfy intertemporal incentive compatibility: the expected continuation payoff from truth-telling today must exceed that from any deviation, accounting for how today's report affects future information rents. The envelope theorem approach (Pavan et al., 2014) characterizes the necessary and sufficient conditions: the agent's equilibrium payoff U(θ1:t) must satisfy U(θ1:t) = U(θ1:t-1, θ't) + ∫θ'tθt ∂u/∂θt dθt for all θ't, and the allocation rule must be monotone in a dynamic sense. In examinations, the distinction between ex-post IC (truth-telling optimal after all uncertainty resolves) and Bayesian IC (truth-telling optimal in expectation) is critical. Dynamic mechanisms also appear in optimal auction design with learning (e.g., Kakade, Lobel, and Nazerzadeh, 2013) where the seller learns the buyer's value distribution over time.

Worked Problem: Dynamic Pivot Payment Calculation
Consider a two-period dynamic allocation problem with two agents. Agent 1's value in period 1 is θ₁ ∈ {0, 10} with equal probability; in period 2, θ₂ = θ₁ + ε where ε ∈ {−2, 2} with equal probability. Agent 2's value is constant at 5 in both periods. The efficient allocation gives the good to the agent with higher value each period. Compute the dynamic pivot payment for Agent 1 in period 1 when θ₁ = 10, assuming h₁ = 0.
Step 1: Determine efficient allocations. Period 1: Agent 1 value 10 > Agent 2 value 5, so Agent 1 gets the good. Period 2: θ₂ ∈ {8, 12}. If ε = −2, θ₂ = 8 > 5, Agent 1 gets good. If ε = 2, θ₂ = 12 > 5, Agent 1 gets good. So Agent 1 gets the good in both periods regardless of ε. Step 2: Compute externality on Agent 2. Agent 2's payoff without Agent 1: in each period, Agent 2 gets the good and receives value 5. Total = 10. Agent 2's payoff with Agent 1: Agent 1 gets the good in both periods, Agent 2 gets 0. Total = 0. Externality = 0 − 10 = −10. Step 3: Dynamic pivot payment p₁ = −(externality) + h₁ = 10 + 0 = 10. Agent 1 pays 10 in period 1. (Note: In period 2, the payment will depend on the realized ε, but the period-1 payment is fixed at 10.)
Exam Strategy: Dynamic Mechanism Design
When a question asks about dynamic mechanisms, first identify whether it tests ex-post IC or Bayesian IC. Ex-post IC is stronger and requires the pivot mechanism; Bayesian IC allows expected externality calculations. Always check the no-single-agent-effect condition — if it fails, the dynamic pivot mechanism may not be individually rational. For ratchet effect questions, the key phrase is "current report affects future information rents." The envelope theorem condition (payoff derivative equals marginal value) is the standard necessary condition for incentive compatibility in continuous-type dynamic models.

Bilateral Trade and the Myerson-Satterthwaite Impossibility Theorem

The Myerson-Satterthwaite theorem (1983) is the bilateral trade analogue of the Gibbard-Satterthwaite theorem: it proves that no mechanism can simultaneously achieve ex-post efficiency, Bayesian incentive compatibility (BIC), interim individual rationality (IIR), and weak budget balance (WBB) when a buyer and seller have independent private values drawn from continuous distributions with overlapping supports. The setting: a seller owns an object with value vs ∼ Fs on [s̲, s̄], a buyer has value vb ∼ Fb on [b̲, b̄], with s̲ < b̄ (gains from trade exist) and s̄ > b̲ (inefficient trade possible). A direct mechanism specifies trade probability q(vb, vs) ∈ [0, 1] and transfers tb(vb, vs), ts(vb, vs). Ex-post efficiency requires q = 1 if vb > vs and q = 0 if vb < vs. BIC requires truth-telling to be a Bayesian Nash equilibrium. IIR requires each agent's interim expected payoff to be non-negative. WBB requires tb + ts ≥ 0 (the mechanism does not require external subsidies).

The proof uses the envelope theorem to express each agent's interim expected payoff Ui(vi) in terms of the allocation rule: Ub(vb) = Ub(b̲) + ∫b̲vb qb(x) dx where qb(vb) = 𝔼vs[q(vb, vs)], and similarly for the seller. Budget balance implies the sum of expected transfers is non-negative, which via the envelope conditions implies Ub(b̲) + Us(s̄) ≤ 0. But IIR requires both terms to be ≥ 0, so both must be 0. Then the expected gains from trade under the efficient rule q*(vb, vs) = 1{vb > vs} must equal the sum of information rents, which is strictly positive when supports overlap. This creates a deficit: the mechanism must subsidize trade to satisfy IIR, violating WBB. The theorem shows the Coase theorem fails under asymmetric information: even with zero transaction costs, efficient trade is impossible without subsidies. The expected externality mechanism (d'Aspremont and Gérard-Varet, 1979) achieves efficiency and BIC but violates IIR. The double auction (Chatterjee and Samuelson, 1983) satisfies BIC, IIR, WBB but is inefficient. In examinations, the key is recognizing which assumption to relax: overlapping supports (no gap), independence, or continuity. If supports are disjoint (s̄ ≤ b̲), the efficient rule is dominant-strategy IC and the theorem does not apply.

Learning in Games: Fictitious Play, Regret Minimization, and Convergence to Equilibrium

Learning models describe how boundedly rational players might reach equilibrium through repeated interaction. Fictitious play (Brown, 1951; Robinson, 1951) assumes each player believes opponents play a fixed mixed strategy equal to the empirical frequency of their past actions, and best-responds to this belief. Formally, let σ−it be the empirical distribution of opponents' play up to period t. Player i chooses ait+1 ∈ BRi(σ−it). In two-player zero-sum games, fictitious play converges to the set of Nash equilibria (Robinson, 1951). In potential games (Monderer and Shapley, 1996), it converges to a pure Nash equilibrium. However, in general games, fictitious play may cycle (Shapley, 1964: a 3×3 game where empirical frequencies converge to a non-Nash limit cycle). Smooth fictitious play (Fudenberg and Levine, 1995) adds a perturbation term: players choose σit+1 to maximize σi · ui(σi, σ−it) − (1/η) ∑ σi(ai) log σi(ai), which converges to a quantal response equilibrium.

Regret minimization (Hannan, 1957; Hart and Mas-Colell, 2000) takes a different approach: a learning rule has no external regret if the average payoff from following the rule is at least as high as the average payoff from any fixed action in hindsight: lim supT→∞ (1/T) ∑t=1T [ui(ai*, a−it) − ui(ait, a−it)] ≤ 0 for all ai*. The multiplicative weights update (MWU) algorithm achieves no-regret: σit+1(ai) ∝ σit(ai) exp(η ui(ai, a−it)). If all players use no-regret algorithms, the empirical distribution of play converges to the set of coarse correlated equilibria (CCE). If players use no-swap-regret algorithms (Blum and Mansour, 2007), where no conditional deviation improves payoff, the empirical distribution converges to the set of correlated equilibria. Internal regret (Foster and Vohra, 1999) is equivalent to swap regret. In examinations, the hierarchy is: Nash equilibrium ⊂ correlated equilibrium ⊂ coarse correlated equilibrium. Fictitious play converges to Nash in zero-sum and potential games; no-external-regret converges to CCE; no-swap-regret converges to correlated equilibrium. The price of anarchy bounds for learning dynamics (Roughgarden, 2015) show that in smooth games, no-regret dynamics achieve welfare within a factor of the optimal.

Mechanism Design with Budget Constraints: The Laffont-Martimort Framework

Standard mechanism design assumes agents have quasilinear utilities u = v − p and no wealth constraints. When agents face budget constraints — they cannot pay more than their available wealth w — the revelation principle still holds but the feasible set of mechanisms shrinks. Laffont and Martimort (2000) study a monopolist selling a good to a buyer with private value v ∼ F on [v̲, v̄] and known budget w. The buyer's utility is v − p if p ≤ w and −∞ otherwise. The seller's problem is to maximize expected revenue subject to BIC, IIR, and p(v) ≤ w for all v. The optimal mechanism takes a threshold form: there exists a cutoff v* such that types v < v* get the good with probability 0, types v ∈ [v*, v̄] get the good with probability 1 and pay p(v) = min(w, v − ∫v*v q(x) dx). If w is large enough (w ≥ v̄ − ∫v̲v̄ (1 − F(x)) dx), the budget constraint never binds and the standard Myerson auction is optimal. If w is small, the optimal mechanism involves rationing: high-value types are excluded from getting the good with probability 1 because they cannot pay their virtual value. The seller may prefer to sell to lower-value types who can pay, creating a non-monotone allocation in the relaxed problem (which is then ironed).

In multi-agent settings with budget constraints, the pivot mechanism fails because an agent's payment may exceed their budget. Aggarwal, Muthukrishnan, Pál, and Pál (2009) study budget-constrained auctions for ad auctions (generalized second-price with budgets). They show that the clinching auction (Ausubel, 2004) adapted with budgets is not strategy-proof. Dobzinski, Lavi, and Nisan (2012) prove that no deterministic, dominant-strategy IC, individually rational mechanism can achieve a constant-factor approximation to the optimal social welfare in multi-unit auctions with budgets, even with two agents. Randomized mechanisms can achieve O(log n) approximations. The core of the budget-constrained exchange economy may be empty (Benoît and Krishna, 2001). In examinations, the key distinction is between hard budgets (payment cannot exceed w with probability 1) and soft budgets (expected payment ≤ w). Hard budgets destroy the monotonicity of virtual values; soft budgets preserve it but require interim budget constraints. The Laffont-Martimort result that the optimal mechanism rations high-value buyers is a standard exam question: "Why does the seller exclude the highest-value buyer?" Answer: because that buyer's virtual value exceeds their budget, so serving them requires cross-subsidization that reduces revenue.

  • Confusing Arrow's theorem with Gibbard-Satterthwaite — Arrow applies to social welfare functions (full rankings); Gibbard-Satterthwaite applies to social choice functions (single winner). The latter is stronger: any strategy-proof social choice function is dictatorial, but a social welfare function could be strategy-proof without being dictatorial if it outputs rankings.
  • Assuming the dynamic pivot mechanism is always individually rational — it requires the no-single-agent-effect condition. If one agent's report can change the allocation for others when that agent is absent, the pivot payment may exceed the agent's value.
  • Overlooking the overlapping supports condition in Myerson-Satterthwaite — if the seller's maximum value is below the buyer's minimum value (s̄ ≤ b̲), efficient trade is possible (the efficient rule is dominant-strategy IC). The theorem only bites when gains from trade exist but are not guaranteed.
  • Equating no-external-regret with convergence to Nash equilibrium — no-external-regret converges to coarse correlated equilibrium, which is a superset of correlated equilibrium, which is a superset of Nash. Only no-swap-regret (or internal regret) guarantees convergence to correlated equilibrium.
  • Treating budget constraints as just another participation constraint — budget constraints are ex-post (payment ≤ w for every type realization), not interim. This destroys the standard envelope theorem derivation because the constraint binds differently for different types, leading to non-monotone virtual values and rationing.
  • Believing fictitious play converges in all games — Shapley (1964) gave a 3×3 counterexample where empirical frequencies cycle. Convergence is guaranteed only in zero-sum games, potential games, and games with an interior evolutionarily stable strategy.
  • Assuming the Wilson doctrine implies all mechanisms must be prior-free — the Wilson doctrine recommends detail-free mechanisms (dominant-strategy IC) where possible; prior-free worst-case analysis is a separate, stronger criterion. VCG is detail-free but not prior-free optimal.

Mechanism Design with Interdependent Values and the Generalized Vickrey Auction

When agents' valuations depend on signals observed by others, the standard independent‑private‑values framework no longer applies. In an interdependent‑values setting each agent i receives a private signal s_i and the true value is v_i(s1,…,s_n). The key difficulty is that an agent’s report influences both the allocation and the information available to others, so incentive compatibility must be defined with respect to the posterior belief about others’ signals. The generalized Vickrey auction (also called the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism for interdependent values) achieves ex‑post incentive compatibility if the allocation rule is efficient and the payment rule charges each agent the externality they impose on the rest of the economy, evaluated at the reported signals. Formally, let x(s) be the efficient allocation maximizing Σ_i v_i(s). The payment of agent i is p_i(s) = Σj≠i v_j(x(s), s) − Σj≠i v_j(x(s-i), s-i), where x(s-i) is the efficient allocation when i is absent. This payment equals the difference in total welfare of others with and without i, evaluated at the true signals of others. Ex‑post IC holds because no agent can affect the term Σj≠i v_j(x(s-i), s-i) and can only influence the first term through the allocation; the efficient allocation makes the marginal effect of i’s report on others’ welfare exactly equal to the marginal effect on i’s own value, so truthful reporting is a dominant strategy. The mechanism requires that the value functions satisfy the single‑crossing condition (or more generally the “no‑single‑agent‑effect” condition) to guarantee that the efficient allocation is monotone in each signal. If single‑crossing fails, the generalized Vickrey auction may not be implementable in dominant strategies, and one must resort to Bayesian incentive compatibility or to mechanisms that restrict the type space (e.g., the d’Aspremont‑Gérard‑Varet mechanism). In practice, spectrum auctions and procurement auctions with common‑value components are modeled as interdependent‑values environments, and the generalized Vickrey auction provides the theoretical benchmark for efficient, strategy‑proof design.

Repeated Games with Private Monitoring and the Folk Theorem Extensions

In the classic repeated‑game folk theorem, players observe a public signal (the stage‑game outcome) after each period, which makes it possible to construct trigger strategies that enforce any feasible, individually rational payoff vector as a subgame‑perfect equilibrium when the discount factor δ is sufficiently close to one. Private monitoring replaces the public signal with a private signal y_i drawn from a distribution that depends on the action profile a. Because each player’s signal is not observed by others, a player cannot directly condition continuation play on the realized signal of another player. The central result (Fudenberg, Levine, and Maskin 1994; Kandori and Matsushima 1998) shows that if the monitoring structure satisfies “pairwise full rank” and “individual full rank” conditions, then for any ε > 0 there exists δ̄ < 1 such that for all δ ∈ (δ̄,1) every feasible, strictly individually rational payoff vector can be approximated within ε by a sequential equilibrium of the repeated game. The construction uses “belief‑free” or “review‑strategy” equilibria: players partition the infinite horizon into blocks of length T, use the private signals within a block to form a statistical test of whether others deviated, and switch to a punishment phase only when the test rejects the hypothesis of cooperation. The block length T grows as δ → 1, making the statistical test arbitrarily powerful while keeping the loss from occasional false punishments negligible. If the monitoring structure fails the rank conditions (e.g., signals are perfectly correlated across players), the folk theorem may break down; some payoff vectors become unattainable because players cannot distinguish a deviation by one player from a deviation by another. Extensions to “almost public” monitoring (where signals are highly correlated) and to “communication‑augmented” private monitoring (where players can send cheap‑talk messages after observing their signals) restore the folk theorem under weaker conditions. In examinations, the key distinction is between public monitoring (standard folk theorem, simple trigger strategies) and private monitoring (requires statistical inference, block strategies, and rank conditions). A typical question asks: “Why does the standard grim‑trigger strategy fail under private monitoring?” Answer: because a player cannot observe the opponent’s signal, so a deviation cannot be detected with certainty; any deterministic trigger would punish on the basis of the player’s own signal alone, which is noisy and leads to excessive punishment.

Worked Example: Private‑Monitoring Folk Theorem
Consider a two‑player repeated Prisoner’s Dilemma with stage‑game payoffs (C,C) = (3,3), (C,D) = (0,5), (D,C) = (5,0), (D,D) = (1,1). After each period each player observes a private signal y_i ∈ {g,b} with probabilities: if both cooperate, Pr(g|C,C)=0.9; if one defects, Pr(g|C,D)=Pr(g|D,C)=0.5; if both defect, Pr(g|D,D)=0.1. Signals are conditionally independent given actions. Show that for δ sufficiently close to 1 the payoff (3,3) can be approximated by a sequential equilibrium.
The monitoring structure satisfies pairwise full rank because the 2×2 matrix of signal distributions for each action profile has full rank (the vectors (0.9,0.1) and (0.5,0.5) are linearly independent). By the Kandori‑Matsushima theorem, for any ε>0 there exists δ̄<1 such that for all δ>δ̄ there is a sequential equilibrium yielding average payoffs within ε of (3,3). Construction: choose block length T large enough that the empirical frequency of g signals under (C,C) is statistically distinguishable from that under any unilateral deviation. In each block players play C; after the block they compute the fraction of g signals. If the fraction is below a threshold τ (chosen between the expected fractions under (C,C) and under a deviation), they enter a punishment phase of playing D for L periods, then return to cooperation. By the law of large numbers, the probability of a false punishment under cooperation goes to zero as T→∞, while the probability of detecting a deviation goes to one. The discounted loss from the punishment phase is bounded by (1−δ^L)/(1−δ) which can be made arbitrarily small by taking δ→1 and L fixed. Hence the average payoff approaches (3,3).
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks about private monitoring, first state the rank conditions (pairwise full rank, individual full rank). Then outline the block‑strategy construction: (1) choose block length T, (2) define a statistical test on private signals, (3) specify punishment and return phases. Mention that the folk theorem holds for δ→1, not for a fixed δ. Do not confuse private monitoring with imperfect public monitoring (where a public signal is observed but is noisy).

Combinatorial Auctions and the VCG Mechanism with Package Bidding

Combinatorial auctions allow bidders to submit bids on bundles of items rather than on individual items alone. This is essential when items exhibit complementarities (e.g., spectrum licenses covering adjacent geographic regions) or substitutabilities. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) mechanism extends naturally: each bidder i reports a valuation function v_i(S) for every subset S of the set of items M. The allocation rule chooses the partition (S1,…,S_n) of M (some items may remain unallocated) that maximizes Σ_i v_i(S_i). The VCG payment for bidder i is p_i = maxallocation without i Σj≠i v_j(S_j) − Σj≠i v_j(S_j^*), where S_j^* are the bundles assigned to others in the optimal allocation with i present. This payment equals the externality i imposes on the rest of the bidders. The mechanism is dominant‑strategy incentive compatible (DSIC) and efficient (maximizes total reported value) regardless of complementarities. However, computational complexity is a major obstacle: the winner‑determination problem (WDP) is NP‑hard in general (it contains the weighted set packing problem). In practice, auction designers use integer programming solvers, heuristic algorithms, or restrict the bidding language (e.g., OR‑bids, XOR‑bids, or bidding on a predefined collection of packages) to make the WDP tractable. The VCG mechanism also suffers from the “VCG overpayment” problem when bidders have budget constraints or when the seller cares about revenue; the core‑selecting auctions (e.g., the nearest‑Vickrey auction of Day and Raghavan 2007) choose a core outcome that minimizes the distance to VCG payments, preserving incentives approximately while improving revenue. Another issue is the “exposure problem” in simultaneous ascending auctions: a bidder who wants a bundle may win some items but lose others, ending with a negative payoff. Combinatorial auctions solve this by allowing package bids, but they introduce the “threshold problem”: a bidder may be pivotal for a bundle that is not allocated, leading to zero payment despite high reported value. The literature on “bidder‑submodular” valuations (where marginal values decrease) shows that the WDP can be solved in polynomial time via a greedy algorithm, and the VCG payments coincide with the competitive equilibrium prices. In examinations, typical questions ask to (i) write the VCG payment formula for a given allocation, (ii) explain why the WDP is NP‑hard, (iii) describe the exposure problem and how package bidding resolves it, and (iv) compare VCG with core‑selecting auctions.

Network Formation Games: Pairwise Stability and Efficiency

Network formation games model situations where agents (nodes) decide which links to create or maintain, and the payoff of each agent depends on the resulting network structure. A link between i and j is formed only if both agents consent (pairwise consent), and any agent can unilaterally sever an existing link. The standard solution concept is pairwise stability (Jackson and Wolinsky 1996): a network g is pairwise stable if (1) for every existing link ij ∈ g, u_i(g) ≥ u_i(g−ij) and u_j(g) ≥ u_j(g−ij) (no agent wants to delete a link), and (2) for every missing link ij ∉ g, if u_i(g+ij) > u_i(g) then u_j(g+ij) < u_j(g) (no pair of agents both strictly benefit from adding the link). Pairwise stability is a myopic concept; it does not require farsightedness or coalition deviations. A stronger notion, pairwise Nash stability, requires that no pair can jointly add a link and possibly delete other links to make both better off. Efficiency is defined by the utilitarian welfare W(g)=Σ_i u_i(g). In general, pairwise stable networks need not be efficient. The classic “connections model” assumes that each agent receives a benefit δd(i,j) from being connected to j at distance d(i,j) (with 0<δ<1) and pays a cost c per direct link. For low costs (c<δ−δ2) the complete network is both pairwise stable and efficient; for intermediate costs (δ−δ2<c<δ) the star network is pairwise stable but the complete network is efficient; for high costs (c>δ) the empty network is both stable and efficient. The tension arises because a peripheral agent in a star does not internalize the benefit that a new link would bring to the center and to other peripherals. Extensions include directed networks (one‑sided link formation), weighted links, and dynamic network formation with stochastic evolution (e.g., the “stochastic stability” concept of Young 1993). In the “co‑author model” (Jackson and Wolinsky 1996), payoffs are u_i(g)=Σj:ij∈g (1/k_i + 1/k_j − 1/(k_i k_j)) where k_i is the degree of i; this captures diminishing returns to collaboration. Pairwise stable networks in the co‑author model are collections of disjoint cliques of size at most three. Empirical applications include R&D collaboration networks, buyer‑seller networks, and social media friendships. Examination questions often ask to (i) compute pairwise stable networks for a given cost/benefit parameter range, (ii) compare pairwise stability with efficiency, (iii) explain why the star can be stable but inefficient in the connections model, and (iv) describe how farsighted stability (e.g., the largest consistent set) refines the prediction.

Algorithmic Game Theory: Complexity of Computing Nash Equilibria (PPAD‑Completeness)

The existence of a mixed‑strategy Nash equilibrium in every finite game (Nash 1951) is proved via Brouwer’s fixed‑point theorem, which is non‑constructive. The computational problem NASH asks: given a finite normal‑form game (payoff matrices with rational entries), compute a Nash equilibrium (or decide whether one exists with certain properties). In 2005, Daskalakis, Goldberg, and Papadimitriou proved that finding a Nash equilibrium in a 4‑player game is PPAD‑complete; later Chen, Deng, and Teng (2006) extended PPAD‑completeness to 2‑player games (bimatrix games). PPAD (Polynomial Parity Arguments on Directed graphs) is a subclass of TFNP (total function NP) containing problems guaranteed to have a solution by a parity argument (e.g., Sperner’s lemma). The completeness result means that unless PPAD ⊆ P, there is no polynomial‑time algorithm for computing an exact Nash equilibrium in general games. This has profound implications: (1) equilibrium computation is intractable in the worst case, (2) approximation algorithms are the focus — computing an ε‑approximate Nash equilibrium (where no player can gain more than ε by deviating) is also PPAD‑complete for constant ε, but a quasi‑polynomial time algorithm exists (Lipton, Markakis, Mehta 2003) that finds an ε‑equilibrium in time nO(log n/ε2). (3) For special classes — zero‑sum games (linear programming), potential games (best‑response dynamics converge), congestion games (Rosenthal potential), and games with a constant number of strategies per player — polynomial‑time algorithms exist. The Lemke‑Howson algorithm (1964) finds a Nash equilibrium in bimatrix games by following a complementary pivot path; it is exponential in the worst case but often fast in practice. The “support enumeration” method enumerates all possible supports (subsets of strategies) and solves a linear complementarity problem for each; it is exponential but useful for small games. In examinations, typical questions ask to (i) define PPAD and explain why NASH is in PPAD, (ii) state the PPAD‑completeness result for 2‑player games, (iii) describe the Lemke‑Howson algorithm and its relation to the parity argument, (iv) contrast exact vs. approximate equilibrium complexity, and (v) identify tractable subclasses (zero‑sum, potential, congestion). A common trap is to claim that “Nash equilibrium can be found in polynomial time using linear programming” — this is true only for zero‑sum games, not for general bimatrix games.

  • Confusing the generalized Vickrey auction with the standard VCG mechanism — the former requires interdependent values and the no‑single‑agent‑effect condition; the latter assumes independent private values.
  • Assuming the folk theorem holds under private monitoring without the rank conditions — pairwise full rank and individual full rank are necessary for the statistical test to distinguish deviations.
  • Believing the VCG mechanism solves the winner‑determination problem in polynomial time — the WDP is NP‑hard; VCG only guarantees incentive compatibility if the optimal allocation can be computed.
  • Equating pairwise stability with efficiency in network formation — stable networks can be inefficient (e.g., star vs. complete network in the connections model).
  • Thinking that Lemke‑Howson runs in polynomial time for all bimatrix games — it is exponential in the worst case; PPAD‑completeness shows no polynomial‑time algorithm exists unless PPAD ⊆ P.
  • Stating that an ε‑approximate Nash equilibrium can be found in polynomial time for constant ε — this problem remains PPAD‑complete; only quasi‑polynomial algorithms are known.
  • Overlooking the exposure problem in simultaneous ascending auctions and assuming package bidding eliminates all inefficiencies — package bidding removes exposure but introduces the threshold problem and computational hardness.

These advanced topics illustrate how incentive design, information structure, computational limits, and network externalities interact in modern game‑theoretic analysis.

Contract Theory: Principal–Agent Models, Moral Hazard, and Adverse Selection

Contract theory studies how principals design incentives for agents when actions or types are unobservable. The canonical principal–agent model (Holmström 1979; Mirrlees 1976) consists of a risk‑neutral principal and a risk‑averse agent with utility u(w) − c(e), where w is the wage, e ∈ [0,1] is effort, and c is increasing and convex. The principal observes output y = e + ε, where ε is noise with density f(ε). The optimal contract solves maxw(·) ∫ [y − w(y)] f(y−e) dy subject to the agent’s participation constraint ∫ u(w(y)) f(y−e) dy − c(e) ≥ ū and the incentive‑compatibility (first‑order) condition ∫ u(w(y)) fe(y−e) dy = c′(e). Under risk neutrality the first‑best is achieved by selling the firm to the agent; under risk aversion the optimal contract is a linear sharing rule w(y) = α + βy when utility is exponential and noise is normal (Holmström & Milgrom 1987). Moral hazard arises because effort is hidden; adverse selection (screening) occurs when the agent’s type θ (e.g., cost parameter) is private information. The principal offers a menu of contracts {(w(θ), e(θ))} satisfying incentive compatibility u(w(θ)) − c(e(θ), θ) ≥ u(w(θ′)) − c(e(θ′), θ) for all θ, θ′ and individual rationality. The optimal menu typically exhibits “no distortion at the top” and “downward distortion” for lower types (Myerson 1981). Multi‑task agencies (Holmström & Milgrom 1991) show that when effort is allocated across tasks with different measurability, high‑powered incentives on measurable tasks crowd out effort on non‑measurable ones, leading to the “multitask distortion”. In examinations, expect questions that ask to (i) derive the first‑order condition for a given utility and noise distribution, (ii) explain why the optimal contract is linear under CARA‑normal assumptions, (iii) contrast moral hazard with adverse selection using the timing of information asymmetry, and (iv) identify the role of limited liability (wage ≥ 0) in shaping the contract’s slope.

Mechanism Design with Verification: Evidence, Revelation Principle, and Implementation

Standard mechanism design assumes agents’ types are purely private. When agents can produce verifiable evidence about their types (e.g., documents, test results), the designer can condition outcomes on presented evidence. Green and Laffont (1986) introduced the “verification” model: each type θ has a set of feasible evidence E(θ) ⊆ E, and the mechanism specifies an outcome function g: E → X. An agent of type θ can only submit evidence e ∈ E(θ). The revelation principle extends: any equilibrium outcome of a mechanism with evidence can be replicated by a direct mechanism where agents report a type and the mechanism asks for evidence consistent with that report (Ben‑Porath & Lipman 2012). The key condition is “normality”: if θ can mimic θ′, then E(θ) ⊇ E(θ′). Under normality, the designer can implement any social choice function that is “evidence‑monotonic”: whenever the chosen outcome changes from x to x′ as the type profile changes, there must exist a type that can produce evidence disproving the original profile. Implementation with evidence often uses “augmented revelation mechanisms” where the mechanism requests a “proof” and penalizes inconsistencies. In the “costly verification” variant (Erlanson & Kleiner 2020), the designer can audit at a cost, leading to optimal mechanisms that randomize audits. Examination questions frequently test: (i) the definition of normality and its role in the revelation principle, (ii) the difference between evidence‑monotonicity and standard Maskin monotonicity, (iii) construction of a direct mechanism with evidence for a simple two‑type screening problem, and (iv) the impact of costly verification on the optimal audit probability.

Technique
A principal hires an agent whose cost type θ ∈ {θ_L, θ_H} with θ_L < θ_H. The agent can produce a certificate proving θ = θ_L at cost c > 0; a high‑type agent cannot produce this certificate. The principal values output q at V(q) = 10q and pays wage w. The agent’s utility is w − θq − c·I{certificate produced}. Design a direct mechanism with evidence that implements the efficient output levels q_L = 5, q_H = 3 and satisfies incentive compatibility and individual rationality (reservation utility 0).
Step 1: Define evidence sets. E(θ_L) = {∅, cert}, E(θ_H) = {∅}. Normality holds because θ_L can mimic θ_H (by not showing cert) but θ_H cannot mimic θ_L. Step 2: Direct mechanism asks agent to report θ and optionally present cert. If report = θ_L and cert presented → outcome (q_L, w_L). If report = θ_L without cert → treat as θ_H (or punish). If report = θ_H → outcome (q_H, w_H). Step 3: Incentive compatibility. For θ_L: w_L − θ_L q_L − c ≥ w_H − θ_L q_H (since θ_L can mimic θ_H by not showing cert). For θ_H: w_H − θ_H q_H ≥ w_L − θ_H q_L (θ_H cannot produce cert, so cannot get (q_L, w_L)). Step 4: Individual rationality: w_L − θ_L q_L − c ≥ 0, w_H − θ_H q_H ≥ 0. Step 5: Choose wages to bind constraints. Set w_H = θ_H q_H = 3θ_H. Then θ_L’s mimic payoff = 3θ_H − θ_L·3. To make θ_L prefer truth, need w_L − 5θ_L − c ≥ 3θ_H − 3θ_L ⇒ w_L ≥ 5θ_L + c + 3(θ_H − θ_L). Set w_L = 5θ_L + c + 3(θ_H − θ_L). Check IR for θ_L: w_L − 5θ_L − c = 3(θ_H − θ_L) ≥ 0. All constraints satisfied. The mechanism implements efficient outputs with evidence.
Exam-Day Strategy
When a question introduces verifiable evidence, first list the evidence sets for each type and verify normality. Then write the direct mechanism as a mapping from (report, evidence) to outcomes. Use the revelation principle to restrict attention to truthful reporting with appropriate evidence. Bind the incentive constraints of the type that can mimic others, and set wages to satisfy participation. This systematic approach avoids missing the evidence‑monotonicity condition.

Quantum Game Theory: Quantum Strategies, Entanglement, and Equilibrium

Quantum game theory extends classical normal‑form games by allowing players to choose quantum operations on a shared entangled state. The Eisert–Wilkens–Lewenstein (EWL) protocol (1999) for the Prisoner’s Dilemma starts with a two‑qubit state |ψ₀⟩ = Ĵ|00⟩, where Ĵ = exp(i γ/2 σ_y ⊗ σ_y) creates entanglement parameter γ ∈ [0, π/2]. Each player applies a unitary operator Û(θ, φ, α) from SU(2) to their qubit, then the state is disentangled by Ĵ† and measured in the computational basis. Payoffs are assigned to the four measurement outcomes according to the classical bimatrix. When γ = 0 the game reduces to the classical mixed‑strategy Prisoner’s Dilemma with unique Nash equilibrium (Defect, Defect). For maximal entanglement γ = π/2, the quantum strategy Q = Û(0, 0, π/2) (the “quantum cooperate”) becomes a dominant strategy, yielding the Pareto‑optimal (Cooperate, Cooperate) outcome as a Nash equilibrium. The set of quantum Nash equilibria depends continuously on γ; at a critical entanglement γ* ≈ 0.5π the classical equilibrium disappears. Beyond two‑player two‑strategy games, quantum extensions of Battle of the Sexes, Chicken, and multi‑player games have been studied. The quantum minimax theorem (Gutoski & Watrous 2007) shows that zero‑sum quantum games have a value and optimal strategies in the space of quantum channels. In examinations, typical tasks include: (i) writing the EWL circuit for a given γ, (ii) computing the expected payoff for a profile of SU(2) operators, (iii) identifying the critical entanglement at which a new equilibrium emerges, and (iv) explaining why quantum strategies can resolve social dilemmas without communication. A common trap is to treat quantum strategies as classical mixed strategies; they are not probability distributions but unitary transformations that exploit interference and entanglement.

Game Theory in Machine Learning: Adversarial Examples, Generative Adversarial Networks, and Equilibrium Computation

Modern machine learning frames several problems as games. Goodfellow et al. (2014) introduced Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) as a two‑player zero‑sum game: a generator G(z; θ_g) maps noise z to data space, and a discriminator D(x; θ_d) outputs the probability that x is real. The value function is V(G, D) = 𝔼x∼p_data[log D(x)] + 𝔼z∼p_z[log(1 − D(G(z)))]. The Nash equilibrium corresponds to G reproducing the data distribution and D(x) = 1/2 everywhere. Training alternates gradient steps on θ_d and θ_g, which is a form of simultaneous gradient descent‑ascent. Convergence to a local Nash equilibrium is not guaranteed; the game is non‑convex‑non‑concave, and cycles are common. Adversarial examples (Szegedy et al. 2014) can be modeled as a Stackelberg game: the attacker (leader) chooses a perturbation δ with ‖δ‖_p ≤ ε to maximize loss L(x+δ, y; θ), and the defender (follower) chooses model parameters θ to minimize worst‑case loss. This leads to the min‑max formulation min_θ max‖δ‖≤ε L(x+δ, y; θ). Robust optimization via adversarial training approximates this equilibrium. In multi‑agent reinforcement learning, independent Q‑learning can be seen as learning in a stochastic game; convergence to a Markov perfect equilibrium requires coordination. Examination questions may ask to: (i) write the GAN value function and derive the optimal discriminator for a fixed generator, (ii) explain why simultaneous gradient descent can cycle and how techniques like gradient penalty or extragradient methods improve convergence, (iii) formulate the adversarial attack as a Stackelberg game and derive the first‑order condition for the attacker’s optimal perturbation under an ℓ_∞ constraint, and (iv) discuss the relationship between Nash equilibrium in GANs and the Jensen‑Shannon divergence. A frequent mistake is to claim that GAN training always converges to the global Nash equilibrium; in practice it finds a local equilibrium or limit cycle.

Game Theory in Environmental Economics: Common‑Pool Resources, Ostrom’s Institutional Design, and Dynamic Games of Resource Extraction

Common‑pool resources (CPRs) such as fisheries, groundwater basins, and the atmosphere are subtractable but non‑excludable, leading to the “tragedy of the commons” (Hardin 1968). Ostrom (1990) documented successful self‑governance institutions and identified eight design principles: clearly defined boundaries, congruence between appropriation and provision rules, collective‑choice arrangements, monitoring, graduated sanctions, conflict‑resolution mechanisms, minimal recognition of rights, and nested enterprises. Game‑theoretically, a CPR is a dynamic game where each period t a set of appropriators choose extraction levels e_i^t, the stock evolves as St+1 = S_t + r(S_t) − Σ_i e_i^t, and payoffs are π_i^t = p(e_i^t) − c(e_i^t, S_t). In the open‑access Nash equilibrium (Gordon 1954), each appropriator ignores the externality on future stock, leading to over‑extraction and stock collapse. The socially optimal path solves a dynamic programming problem maximizing Σ_t β^t Σ_i π_i^t subject to the stock dynamics. Markov perfect equilibria (MPE) in linear‑quadratic CPR games (Levhari & Mirman 1980) yield closed‑form extraction rules that are linear in the stock. When appropriators can communicate and enforce agreements, the folk theorem for repeated games implies that any feasible, individually rational payoff vector can be sustained as a subgame‑perfect equilibrium if the discount factor δ is sufficiently high. Ostrom’s principles map to mechanisms that increase δ (e.g., monitoring raises detection of deviations) and reduce the gain from deviation (graduated sanctions). In examinations, expect: (i) derivation of the open‑access steady state and the optimal steady state for a logistic growth function r(S) = rS(1 − S/K), (ii) computation of the MPE extraction rule for a two‑player linear‑quadratic game, (iii) explanation of how Ostrom’s design principles correspond to the conditions of the folk theorem (monitoring → public signals, sanctions → punishment strategies), and (iv) analysis of a “trigger strategy” that sustains cooperation in a repeated CPR game. A common trap is to confuse the static Nash equilibrium of a one‑shot CPR game with the dynamic MPE; the latter accounts for stock effects and typically yields lower extraction.

  • Claiming GAN training always converges to the global Nash equilibrium — non‑convex‑non‑concave games often exhibit cycles or local equilibria.
  • Treating the open‑access steady state as the socially optimal outcome in a CPR model — the optimal steady state internalizes the stock externality and is strictly larger.
  • Overlooking the normality condition when applying the revelation principle with evidence — without normality, the direct mechanism may not replicate the original equilibrium.
  • Confusing the first‑order condition for moral hazard with the incentive‑compatibility constraint for adverse selection — the former involves effort choice, the latter involves type reporting.
  • Believing that Ostrom’s design principles guarantee cooperation for any discount factor — they facilitate cooperation but the folk theorem still requires δ above a threshold.

Mechanism Design with Multi‑Dimensional Types and the Cremer‑McLean Full Extraction Result

When agents’ private information is multi‑dimensional — for example a bidder who knows both her valuation and her risk attitude — the classic Myerson (1981) single‑dimensional revenue‑maximising auction no longer applies. Cremer and McLean (1985, 1988) showed that if the joint distribution of types satisfies a belief‑nesting (or “full rank”) condition, the principal can design a mechanism that extracts the entire surplus, leaving every type with its ex‑post participation payoff. The key idea is to use a menu of lotteries that correlate the agent’s report with a statistically independent signal, turning the agent’s private information into a state‑contingent payment that mimics a first‑best outcome. Formally, let the type space be Θ = Θ₁ × … × Θ_k with prior μ. If the matrix of conditional probabilities of the signal given each type has full column rank, there exists a transfer rule t(θ, s) such that for every θ the expected transfer equals the agent’s expected valuation, and incentive compatibility holds with equality. This result requires correlated types across agents or an exogenous correlation device; with independent types the full‑rank condition fails and only partial extraction is possible (McAfee & Reny 1992). In examinations, expect to: (i) state the full‑rank condition and explain why it fails under independence, (ii) sketch the construction of the lottery menu for a two‑type, two‑signal example, and (iii) discuss the practical limitation that the mechanism may require arbitrarily large payments, violating budget‑balance or limited‑liability constraints.

Dynamic Contracting with Limited Commitment and the Ratchet Effect

In a long‑run principal‑agent relationship where the principal cannot commit to future contracts, the agent anticipates that today’s performance will influence tomorrow’s terms — a phenomenon known as the ratchet effect (Freixas, Guesnerie & Tirole 1985). Consider a two‑period model: the agent’s cost type θ ∈ {θ_L, θ_H} is drawn once and remains constant. In period 1 the principal offers a menu {(q₁(θ), t₁(θ))}. After observing output q₁, the principal updates beliefs and offers a period‑2 contract {(q₂(θ), t₂(θ))}. Because the principal cannot commit to ignore the information revealed in period 1, the high‑cost type has an incentive to mimic the low‑cost type in period 1 to secure a more favourable period‑2 contract. The resulting equilibrium typically involves downward distortion of the low‑cost type’s first‑period output relative to the full‑commitment benchmark, and an information rent that persists into period 2. Laffont and Tirole (1990) characterise the optimal renegotiation‑proof contract: the principal offers a menu that makes the agent indifferent between revealing his type early and pooling, thereby eliminating the ratchet incentive at the cost of a larger first‑period distortion. In regulatory settings (e.g., price‑cap regulation of utilities) the ratchet effect explains why regulators may allow firms to retain a share of cost‑saving gains — to prevent the regulator from “ratcheting down” allowed prices after observing high productivity. Examination questions often ask to: (i) write the agent’s intertemporal incentive‑compatibility constraints, (ii) derive the first‑order conditions for the optimal menu under limited commitment, and (iii) compare the welfare loss with the full‑commitment benchmark.

Deriving the Ratchet Effect in a Two‑Period Model
A principal hires an agent for two periods. The agent’s marginal cost θ is either 1 (low) or 2 (high) with equal probability. Output q yields value V(q) = 10q − q². The principal cannot commit to period‑2 contracts. Find the optimal period‑1 menu {(q₁(θ), t₁(θ))} and the resulting period‑2 contracts.
Step 1: Period‑2 problem. After period‑1 output, the principal’s posterior belief μ(θ|q₁) determines the period‑2 menu. With full information (if types separate), the principal sets q₂(θ) = argmax_q [V(q) − θq] = (10 − θ)/2. Thus q₂(L) = 4.5, q₂(H) = 4. Transfers are set to give each type its reservation utility (zero): t₂(θ) = θ q₂(θ).
Step 2: Period‑1 incentive compatibility. The low‑cost type’s total payoff if he reports truthfully: U_L = t₁(L) − 1·q₁(L) + t₂(L) − 1·q₂(L). The high‑cost type’s payoff from mimicking low: U_H^mimic = t₁(L) − 2·q₁(L) + t₂(L) − 2·q₂(L). To prevent mimicking we need U_L ≥ U_H^mimic ⇒ q₁(L) ≤ q₂(L) − q₂(H) = 0.5. The optimal distortion sets q₁(L) = 0.5.
Step 3: Participation. Set t₁(L) so that U_L = 0 ⇒ t₁(L) = 1·0.5 + 1·4.5 − t₂(L) = 5 − 4.5 = 0.5. For the high type, give the full‑commitment output q₁(H) = 4 and transfer t₁(H) = 2·4 = 8 (zero rent).
Result: Period‑1 menu: (q₁(L)=0.5, t₁(L)=0.5) for low type; (q₁(H)=4, t₁(H)=8) for high type. Period‑2 contracts are the full‑information outcomes. The low type’s first‑period output is severely distorted downward (0.5 vs. 4.5) to deter the high type from mimicking — the ratchet effect.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a dynamic contracting question appears, first write the timeline: type draw → period‑1 contract → output → belief update → period‑2 contract. Then list the two sets of constraints: (a) period‑2 incentive compatibility (usually trivial under full information) and (b) period‑1 incentive compatibility that incorporates the continuation payoff. Solve backwards: compute period‑2 outcomes as functions of beliefs, substitute into period‑1 IC, and then optimise. Remember that limited commitment means the principal re‑optimises in period‑2; do not impose a single contract for both periods.

Auction Theory with Risk‑Averse Bidders and the Breakdown of Revenue Equivalence

The Revenue Equivalence Theorem (Myerson 1981; Riley & Samuelson 1981) relies on risk‑neutral bidders, independent private values, and symmetric information. Introducing risk aversion (concave utility u(x) with u'' < 0) breaks the equivalence between standard auction formats. In a first‑price sealed‑bid auction a bidder shades her bid less aggressively because a lower bid reduces the variance of her payoff; in a second‑price auction the dominant strategy remains to bid one’s valuation, which is independent of risk attitude. Consequently, the first‑price auction yields higher expected revenue than the second‑price auction when bidders are risk‑averse (Maskin & Riley 1984). The equilibrium bidding function in a first‑price auction with n symmetric bidders whose values are drawn from F on [0,1] and utility u(x) = x^ρ (0 < ρ ≤ 1) satisfies the differential equation β'(v) = (n−1) f(v)/F(v) · [u(v−β(v)) / u'(v−β(v))]. For ρ = 1 (risk neutrality) this reduces to the familiar β(v) = v − ∫0^v (F(x)/F(v))n−1 dx. As ρ decreases, β(v) approaches v, raising revenue. The optimal auction with risk‑averse bidders (Myerson 1981 extended by Matthews 1987) uses a reserve price that depends on the curvature of u and may employ lotteries to insure bidders against risk, thereby extracting more surplus. Examination tasks frequently include: (i) deriving the first‑order condition for the symmetric equilibrium bid in a first‑price auction with CRRA utility, (ii) proving that expected revenue in the first‑price auction exceeds that in the second‑price auction for any concave u, and (iii) explaining why the optimal mechanism may involve randomised allocations when bidders are risk‑averse.

Matching with Contracts: The Hatfield‑Milgrom Framework and Stability

The classic Gale‑Shapley deferred‑acceptance algorithm assumes each match is a simple pair (doctor, hospital). In many real markets — labor markets with salary packages, cadet‑branch assignments with multiple attributes, kidney‑exchange chains with contractual terms — a match is a contract specifying a set of terms (wage, duties, location). Hatfield and Milgrom (2005) generalised the model: a set of doctors D and hospitals H, a finite set of contracts X ⊆ D × H × T where T is a set of terms. Each doctor has a preference ordering over subsets of contracts that involve her; each hospital has a choice function C_H mapping any set of contracts to a subset it wishes to sign. The crucial conditions for the existence of a stable set of contracts are: (1) substitutes — if a hospital chooses a contract from a set, it still chooses it when the set shrinks; (2) law of aggregate demand — the number of chosen contracts does not decrease when the available set expands. Under these conditions the cumulative offer process (a generalised deferred‑acceptance algorithm) converges to a stable allocation that is the doctor‑optimal stable set. Stability means no doctor‑hospital pair can block by signing a contract both prefer to their assigned contracts. The framework also yields a rural hospitals theorem: the set of doctors matched and the number of contracts each hospital signs are invariant across all stable allocations. Applications include the US medical residency match (NRMP) with couples and the Indian Institute of Technology seat allocation with category‑specific contracts. In exams, expect to: (i) verify the substitutes condition for a given hospital choice function, (ii) run one round of the cumulative offer process on a small example, and (iii) explain why the law of aggregate demand is necessary for the rural hospitals theorem.

Game Theory in Political Economy: Voting Games, Agenda Control, and the Median Voter Theorem

Spatial models of voting treat policies as points on a line (or Euclidean space) and voters as having single‑peaked preferences with ideal points. Black (1948) and Downs (1957) proved the Median Voter Theorem: if voters have single‑peaked preferences over a one‑dimensional policy space and vote sincerely, the Condorcet winner is the median ideal point, and any majority‑rule equilibrium coincides with it. The theorem fails when preferences are not single‑peaked (Condorcet cycles) or when the agenda is endogenous. Romer and Rosenthal (1978) introduced agenda control: a proposer (e.g., a committee chair) makes a take‑it‑or‑leave‑it offer against a status quo. The proposer can extract all surplus up to the point where the median voter is indifferent between the proposal and the status quo, yielding a policy biased toward the proposer’s ideal point. Shepsle (1979) showed that with a structure‑induced equilibrium — where the agenda is determined by a fixed amendment procedure — the set of equilibrium outcomes is a subset of the top cycle, often a single point that can be computed by backward induction on the amendment tree. In multidimensional policy spaces, the Chaos Theorem (McKelvey 1976; Schofield 1978) states that the top cycle can be the whole space, so any policy can be reached by a suitable agenda. Examination questions typically ask to: (i) prove the median voter theorem for an odd number of voters with strictly concave utility, (ii) compute the equilibrium proposal in a Romer‑Rosenthal agenda‑setting game given the status quo and the proposer’s ideal point, (iii) illustrate a Condorcet cycle with three voters and three alternatives, and (iv) explain how a binary amendment agenda restores a unique equilibrium in a multidimensional setting.

  • Assuming the Cremer‑McLean full‑extraction result holds with independent types — it requires correlation or a full‑rank belief matrix.
  • Confusing the ratchet effect with simple renegotiation — the ratchet effect specifically arises from the principal’s inability to commit to ignore information revealed in earlier periods.
  • Claiming revenue equivalence survives risk aversion — first‑price and second‑price auctions generate different revenues when bidders are risk‑averse.
  • Treating the Hatfield‑Milgrom substitutes condition as automatically satisfied — many realistic choice functions (e.g., with complementarities) violate substitutes, destroying stability guarantees.
  • Believing the median voter theorem applies in multidimensional policy spaces without additional structure — cycles are generic unless agenda restrictions or single‑peakedness on a line are imposed.
  • Overlooking the law of aggregate demand when checking stability in matching with contracts — without it the rural hospitals theorem fails and the set of matched agents may vary across stable allocations.
  • Misapplying the Romer‑Rosenthal agenda‑setting result by assuming the proposer can always implement her ideal point — the proposer is constrained by the median voter’s indifference curve relative to the status quo.

Reputation Effects in Dynamic Games: The Chain Store Paradox and Limit Pricing

Reputation effects arise when a long‑run player faces a sequence of short‑run opponents who are uncertain about the long‑run player's type or commitment power. The canonical model is the chain store paradox (Kreps and Wilson, 1982; Milgrom and Roberts, 1982). A monopolist chain store operates in K markets. In each period a potential entrant decides whether to enter; if entry occurs, the chain store chooses Fight or Accommodate. The chain store has two possible types: a normal type that maximises stage‑game profit (prefers Accommodate) and a commitment type that always Fights. Entrants share a common prior μ that the chain store is the commitment type. In the unique sequential equilibrium, the normal type builds a reputation for toughness by Fighting early entrants, then switches to Accommodate once its reputation is sufficiently established. The equilibrium exhibits a phased structure: for the first T periods the normal type Fights with probability one, then mixes, and finally Accommodates. The length T grows with the prior μ and the number of markets K. This resolves the backward‑induction paradox: in the finitely repeated game with complete information, the unique subgame perfect equilibrium has the chain store Accommodate in every period; incomplete information restores the intuitive "tough early, soft later" behaviour.

The same logic underpins limit pricing models (Milgrom and Roberts, 1982). An incumbent with private cost information chooses a price before a potential entrant decides whether to enter. A low‑cost incumbent can signal its type by setting a low price (limit price) that a high‑cost incumbent would not mimic. The separating equilibrium requires the low‑cost type to sacrifice current profit to deter entry; the pooling equilibrium has both types charge the monopoly price and entry occurs with positive probability. The intuitive criterion (Cho and Kreps, 1987) often selects the separating equilibrium when the cost difference is large. In examinations, expect to: (i) compute the equilibrium Fighting probability for the normal type in a two‑market chain store game, (ii) derive the limit price in a two‑type signalling game and verify the intuitive criterion, and (iii) explain why the chain store paradox disappears when the number of markets is infinite (the normal type always Accommodates).

Security Games and Stackelberg Equilibrium in Resource Allocation

Security games model a defender (leader) who commits to a mixed strategy over resource allocations, and an attacker (follower) who observes the commitment and best‑responds. This Stackelberg structure is appropriate when the defender can credibly commit to a randomised patrol schedule (e.g., airport checkpoints, coast guard patrols, cyber network monitoring). The defender has m resources to protect n targets; each target i has a defender reward Rid and penalty Pid if attacked, and attacker reward Ria and penalty Pia. The defender's mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure allocations (which targets receive a resource). The attacker chooses the target maximising expected utility given the defender's marginal coverage probabilities ci. The defender's problem is a bilevel optimisation: maximise expected utility subject to the attacker best‑responding. This can be formulated as a mixed‑integer linear program (MILP) or, for the special case of homogeneous resources and no scheduling constraints, as a linear program via the multiple LPs method (Korzhyk et al., 2010). The optimal commitment yields a Strong Stackelberg Equilibrium (SSE) where the attacker breaks ties in the defender's favour — a standard tie‑breaking rule justified by the defender's ability to perturb the strategy infinitesimally.

Key extensions include: (i) Bayesian security games where the attacker's type (rewards/penalties) is private information, solved via the DOBSS (Decomposed Optimal Bayesian Stackelberg Solver) algorithm; (ii) security games with scheduling constraints (e.g., patrol routes must be feasible tours), requiring column generation over the exponential set of pure strategies; (iii) green security games for wildlife protection where the attacker (poacher) learns the defender's strategy over time, modelled as a repeated Stackelberg game with learning. In exams, typical tasks are: (i) formulate the defender's LP for a small game with three targets and one resource, (ii) compute the SSE coverage probabilities and attacker best response, (iii) explain why the defender's commitment power is essential — without it the game reduces to a simultaneous‑move game with typically lower defender utility, and (iv) describe how the DOBSS algorithm decomposes the Bayesian game into a set of LPs indexed by attacker types.

Mechanism Design with Externalities: The Jehiel‑Moldovanu Framework

Standard mechanism design assumes agents' utilities depend only on their own allocation and payment. In many settings — spectrum auctions, M&A markets, patent licensing — an agent's payoff depends on who else receives the object. Jehiel and Moldovanu (2001) introduced a general framework where agent i's valuation is vi(x, θ) with x the allocation vector and θ the type profile. The critical distinction is between allocative externalities (the allocation to others affects i's value) and payoff externalities (the payment of others affects i's utility, e.g., budget constraints). With allocative externalities, the revelation principle still holds, but the envelope theorem and revenue equivalence break down. The optimal mechanism depends on the externality structure: if externalities are positive (e.g., network effects), the seller may want to allocate to multiple agents to increase total value; if negative (e.g., competitive markets), the seller may restrict supply to increase the winner's value. Jehiel, Moldovanu, and Stacchetti (1996) showed that in auctions with negative externalities, the English auction can yield higher revenue than the Vickrey auction because the dropout prices reveal information about competitors' values, affecting the remaining bidders' valuations. The optimal mechanism often involves exclusion: the seller may keep the object unallocated to manipulate externalities. In the licensing game, a patent holder sells licenses to Cournot competitors; the optimal mechanism is a menu of (quantity, royalty) contracts that internalises the negative externality each licensee imposes on others. Examination questions typically ask to: (i) write the incentive compatibility constraints for a two‑bidder auction with negative allocative externalities, (ii) explain why the standard envelope argument fails (the derivative of the value function with respect to type depends on the allocation to others, which itself depends on the agent's report), (iii) compare revenue in first‑price, second‑price, and English auctions when the winner imposes a negative externality on the loser, and (iv) describe the countervailing incentives that arise when an agent's report affects both its own allocation and the externality it receives.

Game Theory in Financial Markets: The Kyle Model and Market Microstructure

The Kyle (1985) model is the foundational model of strategic informed trading. A single risk‑neutral informed trader observes the liquidation value v ~ N(μ, Σv) of an asset. Noise traders submit aggregate demand u ~ N(0, Σu) uncorrelated with v. A competitive, risk‑neutral market maker observes total order flow y = x + u (where x is the informed trader's order) and sets price p = E[v | y]. The informed trader chooses x(v) to maximise expected profit E[(v − p)x]. In the unique linear equilibrium, the informed trader uses a linear strategy x = β(v − μ) with β = √(Σu/Σv), and the market maker sets p = μ + λy with λ = βΣv/Σu = √(Σv/Σu). The parameter λ measures market depth (inverse of Kyle's lambda); higher noise trading (larger Σu) increases depth and reduces the informed trader's profit. The informed trader's expected profit is ½√(ΣvΣu). The model extends to: (i) multiple informed traders (Holden and Subrahmanyam, 1992) where competition reduces each trader's aggressiveness; (ii) dynamic Kyle model (Kyle, 1985; Back, 1992) with multiple trading rounds, where the informed trader trades more slowly to avoid revealing information; (iii) Glosten‑Milgrom (1985) model with a specialist who sets bid‑ask spreads facing sequential arrival of informed and uninformed traders, generating the adverse selection component of the spread. In exams, expect to: (i) derive the linear equilibrium of the static Kyle model, (ii) compute the informed trader's profit and market depth for given variances, (iii) explain why the informed trader's order is proportional to the deviation of v from its mean, and (iv) contrast Kyle's batch auction with Glosten‑Milgrom's sequential trade in terms of price discovery and spread formation.

Team Theory and the Marschak‑Radner Framework

Team theory (Marschak, 1955; Radner, 1962) studies decision problems where multiple agents share a common objective but have decentralised information. Unlike non‑cooperative game theory, there is no strategic conflict — all agents maximise the same expected utility function U(a₁, …, aₙ, θ) where aᵢ is agent i's action and θ is the state of nature. Each agent observes a private signal sᵢ drawn from a joint distribution P(s₁, …, sₙ | θ). A team decision rule is a profile of functions δᵢ : Sᵢ → Aᵢ. The team problem is to choose δ to maximise E[U(δ₁(s₁), …, δₙ(sₙ), θ)]. The central result is that team problems are generally non‑convex and finding the optimal decision rule is computationally hard (NP‑hard in general). Radner (1962) showed that if the utility function is quadratic and signals are jointly normal, the optimal rule is linear and can be found by solving a system of linear equations. The person‑by‑person satisficing approach yields local optima: a decision rule is person‑by‑person optimal if no single agent can improve expected utility by unilaterally changing its rule. Every global optimum is person‑by‑person optimal, but the converse fails. The Witsenhausen counterexample (1968) demonstrates that even in a two‑stage linear‑quadratic‑Gaussian team, the optimal decision rule can be non‑linear, refuting the conjecture that linearity holds for all LQG teams. Team theory connects to decentralised control, distributed optimisation, and multi‑agent reinforcement learning. In examinations, typical tasks are: (i) formulate a two‑agent team problem with binary actions and signals, write the expected utility as a function of the decision rules, (ii) explain why the person‑by‑person optimality condition is necessary but not sufficient for global optimality, (iii) describe the Witsenhausen counterexample setup and why the optimal control law is non‑linear, and (iv) contrast team theory with mechanism design (common objective vs. conflicting objectives, no incentive constraints vs. incentive compatibility).

  • Confusing the chain store paradox with the finitely repeated game under complete information — the paradox only arises with incomplete information; with complete information backward induction forces Accommodate in every period.
  • Assuming the defender's optimal commitment in a security game is a Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous‑move game — the Stackelberg commitment yields strictly higher defender utility except in degenerate cases; the simultaneous game has multiple equilibria, some Pareto‑dominated.
  • Applying the standard envelope theorem in mechanism design with allocative externalities — the derivative of the interim value function depends on how the allocation to others changes with the agent's report, breaking the standard revenue equivalence argument.
  • Claiming the English auction always dominates the Vickrey auction with externalities — the ranking depends on the sign and magnitude of externalities; with positive externalities the Vickrey auction can yield higher revenue.
  • Treating Kyle's lambda as the bid‑ask spread — lambda is the price impact coefficient (market depth); the Glosten‑Milgrom spread has an adverse selection component and an inventory/order‑processing component, not directly equal to lambda.
  • Believing person‑by‑person optimality in team theory guarantees global optimality — the Witsenhausen counterexample and many other settings exhibit local optima that are person‑by‑person optimal but globally suboptimal.
  • Overlooking the role of noise trader variance in the Kyle model — if noise trading variance is zero, the informed trader's strategy is undefined (infinite aggressiveness) and the market maker infers v perfectly, destroying the equilibrium.

Game Theory in Two‑Sided Platforms and Network Effects

Two‑sided platforms (payment cards, app stores, ride‑hailing, online marketplaces) create value by enabling interactions between distinct user groups. The seminal framework of Rochet and Tirole (2003) and Armstrong (2006) models a platform that chooses a price structure (p₁, p₂) for side 1 and side 2, where the total price level P = p₁ + p₂ determines participation, while the price ratio allocates surplus across sides. Indirect network effects mean the utility of a user on side 1 increases with the number of users on side 2, and vice‑versa. The platform’s profit function is π = (p₁ − c₁) n₁ + (p₂ − c₂) n₂, where cᵢ are marginal costs and nᵢ are participation levels determined by a system of demand equations n₁ = D₁(p₁, n₂), n₂ = D₂(p₂, n₁).

Key results: (i) The optimal price structure often subsidises the “more price‑sensitive” side (the side with higher elasticity of participation) and extracts surplus from the other side — the “divide‑and‑conquer” principle. (ii) Multiple equilibria can arise because expectations about the other side’s participation are self‑fulfilling; a platform may need a “critical mass” strategy (e.g., heavy initial subsidies) to reach the high‑participation equilibrium. (iii) When platforms compete, multi‑homing (users joining several platforms) reduces market power and pushes price structures toward marginal cost, while single‑homing sustains differentiation and allows positive margins. (iv) Welfare analysis shows that a profit‑maximising platform may set prices that are socially suboptimal; a regulator can improve welfare by imposing price caps on the side that is over‑charged or by mandating interoperability to increase multi‑homing.

Empirical applications: the 2010‑2015 evolution of the smartphone OS market (iOS vs Android) illustrates how Google’s zero‑price strategy for Android (subsidising developers via advertising) achieved critical mass, while Apple’s high‑price, low‑multi‑homing strategy extracted surplus from high‑valuation consumers. In payment cards, the 2015 EU Interchange Fee Regulation capped merchant‑side fees, effectively forcing a rebalancing of the price structure toward cardholders.

Examiner’s Twist

Questions often ask to derive the first‑order conditions for the platform’s profit maximisation and to interpret the sign of the cross‑partial derivative ∂²π/∂p₁∂p₂. A common trap is to treat the two sides as independent monopolies; the correct approach recognises the feedback loop n₁ n₂. Another twist: given a demand system n₁ = a − b p₁ + γ n₂, n₂ = a − b p₂ + γ n₁, compute the equilibrium participation and show how the platform’s optimal price ratio depends on γ (the strength of network effects).

Game Theory in Cybersecurity: Attack‑Defense Models and Moving Target Defense

Cybersecurity interactions are naturally modelled as Stackelberg security games where a defender (leader) commits to a mixed strategy over a set of protective actions (patching, monitoring, deception) and an attacker (follower) observes the commitment and chooses a target. The canonical model (Kiekintveld et al., 2009) defines a set of targets T, each with a value vₜ to the defender and a cost cₜ to the attacker. The defender allocates k identical resources; a resource assigned to target t detects an attack with probability pₜ. The attacker’s expected payoff for attacking t given defender coverage probability xₜ is Uₐ(t) = (1 − xₜ pₜ) vₜ − cₜ. The defender’s expected loss is U_d = Σₜ xₜ (1 − pₜ) vₜ (assuming the attacker best‑responds). The defender’s optimisation is a linear programme that yields the optimal coverage vector x*.

Moving Target Defense (MTD) extends this by allowing the defender to dynamically change the attack surface (e.g., IP address hopping, configuration randomisation). The FlipIt game (van Dijk et al., 2013) captures the timing dimension: two players alternately “flip” control of a resource at a cost; the player in control gains a continuous benefit rate. The unique Nash equilibrium in the continuous‑time version has both players flipping at a rate proportional to the square root of the benefit‑to‑cost ratio. This result shows that optimal MTD frequency balances the cost of reconfiguration against the reduction in attacker dwell time.

Incomplete information arises when the defender is uncertain about the attacker’s type (e.g., capability, valuation). A Bayesian Stackelberg game incorporates a prior over attacker types; the defender’s commitment maximises expected utility across types. The optimal policy often involves “deception” — allocating resources to low‑value targets to signal strength, or deploying honeypots that mimic high‑value assets. The 2016 DARPA Cyber Grand Challenge demonstrated autonomous agents learning near‑optimal Stackelberg strategies in a capture‑the‑flag environment, validating the practical relevance of these models.

Examiner’s Twist

Typical exam tasks: (i) formulate the defender’s linear programme for a given set of targets and resource constraints; (ii) compute the attacker’s best response and the resulting Stackelberg equilibrium for a two‑target example; (iii) explain why the defender’s commitment power yields a strictly higher payoff than the simultaneous‑move Nash equilibrium (except in degenerate cases); (iv) derive the FlipIt equilibrium flip rate and interpret the square‑root law. A frequent error is to confuse the defender’s mixed strategy in the simultaneous game with the commitment strategy in the Stackelberg game — the latter is a probability distribution announced before the attacker moves, and the attacker’s best response is to a known distribution, not to a realised pure action.

Worked Example: Two‑Target Stackelberg Security Game
A defender protects two servers, A and B. Server A has value 100, detection probability 0.8 if covered. Server B has value 60, detection probability 0.5 if covered. The defender has one resource (k = 1) that can be allocated to either server. The attacker observes the defender’s mixed strategy (probability x on A, 1−x on B) and chooses a server to attack. Attack cost is zero. Find the defender’s optimal commitment x* and the resulting expected loss.

Attacker’s expected payoff from attacking A: (1 − 0.8x)·100 = 100 − 80x.

Attacker’s expected payoff from attacking B: (1 − 0.5(1−x))·60 = 60 − 30 + 30x = 30 + 30x.

The attacker chooses A if 100 − 80x ≥ 30 + 30x → 70 ≥ 110x → x ≤ 7/11 ≈ 0.636. Otherwise attacks B.

Defender’s expected loss if attacker chooses A: x·(1−0.8)·100 = 20x.

Defender’s expected loss if attacker chooses B: (1−x)·(1−0.5)·60 = 30(1−x).

Defender wants to minimise the maximum of these two (since attacker best‑responds). The optimal x* equalises the two losses: 20x = 30(1−x) → 20x = 30 − 30x → 50x = 30 → x* = 0.6.

At x* = 0.6, attacker is indifferent (100−80·0.6 = 52; 30+30·0.6 = 48? Wait recalc: 100−48=52; 30+18=48. Actually attacker prefers A (52>48). So defender should push x slightly above 0.6 to make attacker choose B? Let's recompute correctly: Attacker payoff A = 100−80x. B = 30+30x. Set equal: 100−80x = 30+30x → 70 = 110x → x = 7/11 ≈ 0.636. At x = 7/11, both give 100−80·7/11 = 100−560/11 = 540/11 ≈ 49.09. Defender loss if attacker picks A: 20·7/11 = 140/11 ≈ 12.73. If attacker picks B: 30·(1−7/11)=30·4/11=120/11≈10.91. Attacker will pick A (higher payoff). Defender loss = 140/11. If defender increases x a tiny bit above 7/11, attacker switches to B, defender loss = 30(1−x) which at x=7/11 is 120/11 ≈10.91, lower. So optimal commitment is x* = 7/11 + ε, making attacker choose B, loss ≈ 120/11. In the limit, the Stackelberg equilibrium has defender commit to x = 7/11, attacker breaks tie in defender’s favour (standard tie‑breaking rule), loss = 120/11 ≈ 10.91.

Thus optimal mixed strategy: cover A with probability 7/11, B with 4/11. Expected defender loss = 120/11 ≈ 10.91.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a security‑game question appears, first write the attacker’s payoff for each target as a function of the defender’s coverage probabilities. Then determine the attacker’s best‑response correspondence (piecewise linear). The defender’s problem reduces to minimising a piecewise‑linear convex function — solve by equating losses at the kink points. Always state the tie‑breaking assumption (attacker breaks ties in defender’s favour) because it determines whether the equilibrium uses a strict or weak inequality. For FlipIt, memorise the square‑root formula: optimal flip rate = √(benefit / cost) for each player; the equilibrium benefit share is proportional to the square root of the cost ratio.

Game Theory in Climate Agreements: Dynamic Games of Emission Reduction

International climate negotiations are modelled as dynamic games where countries choose emission abatement levels over time. The foundational integrated assessment model (Nordhaus, 1992 DICE) couples a global carbon cycle with a Ramsey‑type growth model; the strategic layer adds a non‑cooperative game where each country maximises discounted welfare Σₜ βᵗ U(cₜ, Eₜ) subject to a stock pollutant accumulation Sₜ₊₁ = (1−δ) Sₜ + Σᵢ eᵢₜ. Barrett (1994) showed that in a finite‑horizon game with no enforcement, the unique subgame‑perfect equilibrium yields zero abatement in every period (the “free‑rider” outcome). In an infinite‑horizon setting, trigger strategies can sustain cooperation: if any country deviates, all revert to the non‑cooperative Nash equilibrium forever. The Folk Theorem implies that any feasible, individually rational payoff vector can be sustained if the discount factor δ is sufficiently high.

Key quantitative results: (i) The critical discount factor for sustaining the socially optimal abatement path is δ* = (πᴰ − πᴺ) / (πᴰ − πᴾ), where πᴰ is the one‑shot deviation gain, πᴺ the Nash payoff, and πᴾ the cooperative payoff. (ii) Coalition formation games (Carraro & Siniscalco, 1993) reveal a paradox: larger coalitions are less stable because the marginal benefit of joining falls while the free‑rider incentive for outsiders rises. The stable coalition size is often small (2‑3 countries) unless side‑payments or issue linkage (trade, technology transfer) are introduced. (iii) The Paris Agreement’s “pledge‑and‑review” architecture can be interpreted as a repeated game with imperfect public monitoring: each country’s Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC) is a public signal; the review process updates beliefs about compliance. Theoretical work (Harstad, 2016) shows that a “price‑floor” mechanism (minimum carbon price) combined with a “green‑fund” transfer can implement the efficient outcome as a sequential equilibrium.

Carbon leakage — the increase in emissions in non‑participating regions due to unilateral abatement — is captured by a two‑region model with trade in carbon‑intensive goods. The optimal border carbon adjustment (BCA) equals the difference in marginal abatement costs between regions, restoring production efficiency. However, BCAs raise WTO compatibility issues; the 2021 EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM) is the first large‑scale implementation, and its design reflects the theoretical prescription of a “mirror” carbon price on imports.

Examiner’s Twist

Expect questions that ask to: (i) write the Bellman equation for a country’s dynamic optimisation given a stock pollutant; (ii) derive the condition for a trigger‑strategy equilibrium in a two‑country infinite‑horizon game; (iii) compute the stable coalition size in a symmetric n‑country game with linear benefits and quadratic costs; (iv) explain why the Paris Agreement’s lack of binding targets does not necessarily preclude cooperation, referencing the role of “naming‑and‑shaming” as a low‑cost punishment. A common mistake is to treat the discount factor as the pure time preference rate; in climate games the effective discount factor also includes the probability of political survival and the depreciation of climate capital.

Game Theory in Spectrum Auctions: Combinatorial Clock Auctions and Incentive Compatibility

Spectrum auctions allocate licences that exhibit strong complementarities (contiguous blocks, nationwide coverage). The Simultaneous Multiple Round Auction (SMRA) used by the FCC since 1994 suffers from the exposure problem: bidders risk winning a subset of desired packages at a loss. The Combinatorial Clock Auction (CCA), first deployed in the UK 2013 4G auction and later in Canada (2014) and the US Incentive Auction (2016‑2017), addresses this by allowing package bids in a clock phase followed by a supplementary round. The clock phase raises prices on individual lots according to an activity rule; bidders indicate demand for packages at current prices. The supplementary round accepts sealed‑bid package offers. The final allocation solves a winner‑determination problem (maximising reported value) and prices are set by a core‑selecting payment rule (e.g., nearest‑Vickrey) that ensures no coalition of bidders can block the outcome.

Core‑selecting auctions (Day & Milgrom, 2008) choose payments in the core of the reported‑value exchange economy, minimising the distance to Vickrey payments. This restores incentive compatibility “in the large” — truthful bidding is an ex‑post equilibrium when bidders have quasi‑linear preferences and values are drawn from a common prior. However, with budget constraints or risk aversion, the VCG mechanism loses dominance; the CCA’s activity rule (requiring bidders to maintain eligibility points) mitigates demand reduction but can create “parking” incentives (bidding on large packages early to preserve eligibility, then shrinking). The 2016 US Incentive Auction introduced a “forward‑auction” for wireless licences paired with a “reverse‑auction” for broadcaster relinquishment, linked by a clearing target; the equilibrium analysis uses a two‑sided matching model with complementarities.

Revenue equivalence breaks down in combinatorial settings because the allocation rule is not efficient for all type profiles (the winner‑determination problem is NP‑hard, so approximations are used). The “core‑selecting” rule yields higher revenue than VCG when bidders have complementary values, but can be vulnerable to collusion via “bid shading” on packages that are unlikely to win. The 2020 Ofcom 5G auction (UK) adopted a “simultaneous clock with package bidding” hybrid, illustrating the ongoing evolution of practical designs.

Examiner’s Twist

Typical tasks: (i) formulate the winner‑determination integer programme for a small set of lots and package bids; (ii) explain the activity rule and compute eligibility points after a given clock round; (iii) compare VCG, nearest‑Vickrey, and first‑price core‑selecting payments in a two‑bidder, two‑lot example with complementary values; (iv) discuss why the exposure problem disappears in a CCA but may re‑appear in the supplementary round if bidders cannot express all desired packages. A frequent error is to claim that CCA is fully strategy‑proof; it is only “approximately” incentive compatible under the assumption of straightforward bidding in the clock phase.

Game Theory in Public Goods Provision with Non‑Quasilinear Preferences and the Pivot Mechanism

The classic Vickrey‑Clarke‑Groves (VCG) mechanism achieves dominant‑strategy incentive compatibility (DSIC) and efficiency for public goods when utilities are quasilinear: uᵢ(x, tᵢ) = vᵢ(x, θᵢ) + tᵢ. With income effects (non‑quasilinear preferences), the transfer tᵢ enters the utility non‑linearly, and VCG fails to be DSIC. The d’Aspremont‑Gérard‑Varet (AGV) mechanism (1979) restores Bayesian incentive compatibility by using expected externality payments: each agent pays the expected marginal cost of their reported type on others, evaluated at the prior distribution. The AGV mechanism is efficient and budget‑balanced in expectation but only interim incentive compatible, not dominant‑strategy.

The Pivot mechanism (a VCG variant) charges each agent the externality they impose on others at the realised efficient allocation: tᵢ = Σj≠i vⱼ(x*−i, θⱼ) − Σj≠i vⱼ(x*, θⱼ), where x*−i is the efficient allocation without agent i. With non‑quasilinear preferences, the pivot payment must be adjusted to keep the agent’s marginal utility of income constant; this leads to the “generalised pivot” (Williams, 1999) which uses a money‑metric utility scaling. However, the Myerson‑Satterthwaite impossibility theorem (1983) extends: no mechanism can be simultaneously efficient, budget‑balanced, individually rational, and Bayesian incentive compatible when agents have private values and non‑quasilinear preferences, unless the type space is restricted.

Applied settings: (i) Public project selection (e.g., a bridge) where citizens have income‑dependent willingness to pay; the optimal mechanism uses a “Lindahl‑type” personalised price schedule approximated by a tax‑subsidy scheme. (ii) Climate finance: the Green Climate Fund’s allocation problem involves countries with heterogeneous marginal utility of income; the AGV‑style expected externality mechanism is used to design contribution rules that are incentive compatible in expectation. (iii) Digital public goods (open‑source software) where contributors’ effort costs are private and utility from the software is non‑quasilinear; recent work (Zhang & Conitzer, 2021) proposes a “peer‑prediction” mechanism that elicits effort levels without monetary transfers.

Examiner’s Twist

Questions may ask to: (i) write the AGV payment formula for a two‑agent public good with binary types and show it satisfies interim incentive compatibility; (ii) prove that the pivot mechanism is not DSIC when utility is uᵢ = √(vᵢ + tᵢ); (iii) explain why the Myerson‑Satterthwaite impossibility does not apply when the public good is excludable (i.e., a club good); (iv) design a budget‑balanced, Bayesian incentive compatible mechanism for a three‑agent public good with uniformly distributed valuations and logarithmic utility. A common trap is to confuse “budget balance in expectation” (AGV) with “ex‑post budget balance” (VCG runs a deficit); the exam often asks which notion is satisfied by a given mechanism.

  • Assuming the platform’s optimal price structure in a two‑sided market is symmetric — the divide‑and‑conquer principle implies asymmetry unless the two sides have identical elasticities and network‑effect strengths.
  • Confusing the defender’s mixed strategy in a simultaneous security game with the Stackelberg commitment — the commitment is announced before the attacker moves and the attacker best‑responds to the distribution, not to a realised pure action.
  • Treating the discount factor in climate games as the pure rate of time preference — the effective discount factor also incorporates political survival probability and the depreciation rate of the carbon stock.
  • Believing the Combinatorial Clock Auction is fully strategy‑proof — incentive compatibility holds only under straightforward bidding in the clock phase; the supplementary round re‑introduces strategic shading.
  • Applying the standard VCG pivot payment when utilities are non‑quasilinear — the pivot mechanism loses dominant‑strategy incentive compatibility; one must use the AGV expected‑externality mechanism or a generalised pivot with money‑metric scaling.
  • Overlooking the role of the activity rule in CCA — eligibility points constrain demand reduction but create parking incentives; the rule’s parameters (e.g., activity requirement percentage) critically affect bidder behaviour.
  • Assuming the Myerson‑Satterthwaite impossibility rules out all efficient public‑goods mechanisms — it applies only under private values, non‑quasilinear preferences, and ex‑post budget balance; with excludable goods or restricted type spaces, efficient mechanisms exist.

Game Theory in Cryptoeconomic Mechanism Design: Tokenomics and Incentive Compatibility

Cryptoeconomic systems such as Bitcoin, Ethereum, and newer proof‑of‑stake chains are designed as multi‑agent games where participants — miners, validators, token holders, and developers — choose actions that affect the security, throughput, and value of the native token. The central design problem is to align individual incentives with the protocol’s global objectives: honesty, availability, and resistance to Sybil attacks. The foundational model treats the protocol as a mechanism that maps reported actions (e.g., block proposals, attestations) to token rewards and penalties. Incentive compatibility requires that truthful participation — following the prescribed consensus rules — constitutes a (Bayesian) Nash equilibrium. Early work by Kiayias, Koutsoupias, and others (2016) formalised the Bitcoin protocol as a stochastic game and showed that the longest‑chain rule is an approximate Nash equilibrium under the assumption that no single miner controls more than 50 % of hash power. Subsequent analyses introduced the notion of “selfish mining” (Eyal and Sirer, 2014), demonstrating that a coalition with as little as 33 % of hash power can increase its relative revenue by withholding blocks, thereby breaking the naive incentive compatibility of the original protocol.

Tokenomics extends the mechanism design problem by adding a monetary policy layer: inflation schedules, transaction‑fee markets, and staking rewards. The Ethereum Improvement Proposal 1559 (EIP‑1559) replaced a first‑price auction for block space with a base‑fee that is burned and a tip that goes to the proposer. This mechanism is approximately incentive compatible for users (they bid their true value for inclusion) and for proposers (they cannot manipulate the base fee). The design draws on the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves (VCG) principle but adapts it to a dynamic, repeated setting where the base fee evolves according to a deterministic rule. In proof‑of‑stake systems such as Ethereum’s Gasper (Buterin et al., 2020), validators lock up stake and are rewarded for correct attestations while being slashed for equivocation. The slashing conditions are crafted so that any profitable deviation would require controlling a super‑majority of stake, making honest behaviour a strict Nash equilibrium under the assumption of rational, risk‑neutral validators.

A further layer is the design of governance mechanisms for protocol upgrades. On‑chain voting, token‑weighted signalling, and futarchy (prediction‑market‑based decision making) have been proposed to align token‑holder preferences with protocol evolution. The challenge is to prevent vote‑buying and plutocratic capture while preserving decentralisation. Recent research (e.g., Daian et al., 2020) models governance as a Bayesian game with incomplete information about the value of proposed changes and shows that quadratic voting or conviction voting can approximate the efficient outcome under certain distributional assumptions. These results illustrate how classical mechanism design — revenue equivalence, incentive compatibility, budget balance — must be re‑examined in the presence of open‑entry, pseudonymity, and the absence of a trusted enforcer.

Game Theory in Algorithmic Fairness: Strategic Classification and Bias Mitigation

Algorithmic fairness studies the interaction between a decision‑maker (learner) and strategic agents who can invest effort to change their observable features in order to obtain a favourable classification. The canonical model (Hardt, Megiddo, Papadimitriou, and Wootters, 2016) posits a population with true types θ drawn from a distribution, a cost function c(x, x₀) for modifying the original feature vector x₀ to a reported x, and a classifier h(x) = 1{w·x ≥ t}. Agents best‑respond by solving maxₓ { u·h(x) − c(x, x₀) } where u is the utility of a positive label. The learner anticipates this manipulation and chooses (w, t) to minimise a loss function (e.g., classification error or a fairness metric such as demographic parity) evaluated on the manipulated distribution. The resulting game is a Stackelberg game: the learner commits to a classifier, agents best‑respond, and the learner’s payoff depends on the induced equilibrium.

Equilibrium analysis reveals a fundamental tension. If the cost of manipulation is low relative to the benefit of a positive label, agents will pool at the decision boundary, causing the learner to raise the threshold and potentially exclude qualified individuals — a phenomenon known as “gaming the metric”. Conversely, high manipulation costs restore the learner’s ability to recover the true distribution. The existence of a pure‑strategy equilibrium requires that the cost function be convex and that the learner’s loss be quasi‑convex in the threshold. When these conditions fail, only mixed‑strategy equilibria exist, and the learner may randomise over thresholds to deter systematic gaming. Recent extensions incorporate group‑specific cost functions, leading to disparate impact: if one group faces lower manipulation costs, the equilibrium classifier may satisfy equalised odds on the reported features while violating it on the true features.

Mechanism design approaches aim to restore fairness by altering the game structure. One line of work designs “robust” classifiers that are optimal against worst‑case manipulation within an uncertainty set (e.g., using distributionally robust optimisation). Another introduces subsidies or penalties that modify agents’ cost functions, effectively implementing a VCG‑style transfer that aligns individual incentives with a social welfare objective that includes fairness constraints. A third approach uses information design: the learner commits to a signalling policy that reveals partial information about the classifier, shaping agents’ beliefs and reducing wasteful manipulation. These techniques illustrate how game‑theoretic tools — equilibrium refinement, mechanism design, Bayesian persuasion — provide a rigorous foundation for building fair machine‑learning systems that remain effective under strategic behaviour.

Worked Example: Strategic Classification Equilibrium
Consider a binary classification setting where agents can modify their feature vector at cost c‖x−x₀‖² to receive a positive label. The learner chooses a linear threshold w·x ≥ t. Derive the condition for a pure‑strategy Nash equilibrium where no agent wishes to manipulate and the learner’s threshold is optimal given the manipulated distribution.
In equilibrium each agent of type θ chooses x(θ) = x₀ + (w/(2c))·1{w·x₀ < t}. The learner’s optimal threshold satisfies t = w·E[x(θ) | w·x(θ) ≥ t]. Substituting the best‑response yields a fixed‑point equation in (w,t). A solution exists when the cost parameter c exceeds the variance of the unmanipulated scores; otherwise the only equilibrium involves the learner setting t → ∞ (reject all). This illustrates the trade‑off between robustness and accuracy in strategic classification.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the equilibrium of a strategic classification game, first write the agent’s cost‑minimisation problem, then the learner’s risk‑minimisation problem, and finally impose the fixed‑point condition. Sketch the best‑response functions; the intersection gives the equilibrium. Remember that the learner’s objective is usually empirical risk on the manipulated data, not the true distribution.

Game Theory in Health Economics: Insurance Markets, Adverse Selection, and Risk Adjustment

Health insurance markets are a classic arena for game theory because of asymmetric information: individuals know their health risk better than insurers. The Rothschild–Stiglitz (1976) model formalises this as a screening game where insurers offer contracts (premium, coverage) and individuals self‑select. In the competitive equilibrium, a separating menu emerges: high‑risk types receive full coverage at a high premium, low‑risk types receive partial coverage at a lower premium. However, the equilibrium may fail to exist if the proportion of high‑risk individuals is too large, leading to market unravelling — a phenomenon known as “adverse selection death spiral”. The Wilson (1977) refinement introduces the concept of anticipatory equilibrium, where insurers consider the reaction of rivals to any deviating contract, restoring existence under broader conditions.

Policy responses such as risk adjustment, individual mandates, and community rating modify the game structure. Risk adjustment transfers payments from insurers with healthier enrollees to those with sicker enrollees, effectively reducing the incentive for cream‑skimming. In a game‑theoretic formulation, the regulator chooses a risk‑adjustment formula (often a linear function of diagnostic categories) to minimise the insurer’s incentive to distort plan design. The resulting mechanism design problem resembles a principal‑agent model where the principal (regulator) cannot observe the agent’s (insurer’s) effort in managing costs but can observe enrollee risk scores. The optimal risk‑adjustment formula balances the trade‑off between providing incentives for efficient care management and compensating for unavoidable risk differences.

Dynamic extensions incorporate multi‑period contracts and re‑classification risk. In a repeated game, insurers may offer long‑term contracts that lock in premiums, but individuals face the risk of premium increases if their health status deteriorates. The Pauly–Kunreuther (1978) model shows that guaranteed‑renewable contracts can achieve ex‑ante efficiency if commitment is credible. However, commitment is often lacking, leading to time‑inconsistency: insurers have an incentive to re‑price after learning an individual’s health trajectory. Mechanism design solutions include regulated community rating with risk corridors (as in the Affordable Care Act) and reinsurance pools that limit insurer losses. These interventions can be analysed as modifications to the stage‑game payoffs that sustain cooperative outcomes in the repeated interaction.

Game Theory in Data Markets: Privacy Valuation, Differential Privacy, and Mechanism Design

Data markets involve buyers (analysts, firms) who value datasets for inference, and sellers (individuals, platforms) who incur privacy costs when their data are used. The interaction is a mechanism design problem where the mechanism elicits data and possibly privacy preferences, computes an outcome (e.g., a statistic or model), and determines payments. Differential privacy (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith, 2006) provides a formal privacy guarantee: a randomised algorithm M satisfies (ε, δ)‑differential privacy if for any neighbouring datasets D, D′ and any output set S, Pr[M(D) ∈ S] ≤ e^ε Pr[M(D′) ∈ S] + δ. The privacy parameter ε quantifies the maximum multiplicative increase in the probability of any outcome due to a single individual’s data. In a game‑theoretic setting, each agent has a private valuation v_i for privacy loss and a cost c_i(ε) for participating at a given privacy level.

The mechanism designer wishes to purchase a target level of accuracy (e.g., mean squared error of an estimator) while minimising total payment. This is a procurement auction with a non‑standard cost structure: the cost to the buyer is the variance introduced by the privacy noise, which is a decreasing function of ε. The optimal mechanism (Ghosh and Roth, 2011) is a variant of the VCG mechanism where agents report their privacy valuations, the mechanism selects a privacy level ε that maximises social welfare (accuracy value minus sum of reported costs), and payments are set to the externality each agent imposes on others. Truthful reporting is a dominant strategy because the mechanism is VCG‑style, but the privacy‑accuracy trade‑off introduces a non‑linear cost function that must be handled carefully to preserve incentive compatibility.

When agents have interdependent values — for example, the value of a dataset to a buyer depends on the composition of the seller pool — the standard VCG mechanism may fail to be budget balanced or may require unrealistic assumptions about common knowledge. The Cremer–McLean (1988) full‑extraction result shows that with correlated types and a suitably rich signal structure, the mechanism designer can extract the entire surplus. In data markets, correlation arises naturally because individuals’ data are often statistically dependent (e.g., genomic data of relatives). Recent work (Cummings, Ligett, and others, 2015) adapts the Cremer–McLean construction to differential privacy, designing mechanisms that are both privacy‑preserving and approximately revenue‑optimal. However, these mechanisms typically require the designer to know the joint distribution of types, which is a strong assumption. Robust mechanism design (Bergemann and Morris, 2005) relaxes this by requiring incentive compatibility for all possible type distributions within an ambiguity set, leading to mechanisms that are less efficient but more reliable in practice.

Game Theory in Blockchain Consensus: Proof‑of‑Work, Proof‑of‑Stake, and Equilibrium Analysis

Blockchain consensus protocols are distributed games where validators (miners or stakers) choose actions — extending a chain, withholding blocks, or attesting to checkpoints — to maximise expected rewards. The Nakamoto consensus (Nakamoto, 2008) uses proof‑of‑work: miners solve a cryptographic puzzle, and the longest chain rule determines the canonical history. The game can be modelled as a stochastic race where each miner’s hash power determines the probability of finding the next block. Under the assumption of rational, risk‑neutral miners and no network delays, the honest strategy (always extending the longest chain) forms a Nash equilibrium as long as no coalition controls a majority of hash power. However, the selfish‑mining attack (Eyal and Sirer, 2014) shows that a coalition with >33 % hash power can increase its relative revenue by secretly mining a private fork and releasing it strategically, breaking the equilibrium.

Proof‑of‑stake (PoS) replaces hash power with locked‑up stake. The Gasper protocol (Buterin, Hernandez, and others, 2020) combines Casper‑FFG finality gadget with LMD‑GHOST fork choice. Validators are randomly selected to propose blocks and to attest to checkpoints. Rewards are given for correct attestations; slashing penalties punish equivocation (signing two conflicting blocks) and surrounding votes (attesting to a checkpoint that is not a descendant of a previously justified checkpoint). The slashing conditions are designed so that any profitable deviation requires controlling at least 2/3 of the total stake, making honest behaviour a strict Nash equilibrium under the assumption that validators are rational and the network is synchronous. The equilibrium analysis uses the concept of “accountable safety”: if two conflicting checkpoints are finalised, at least 1/3 of stake must have been slashed, providing a cryptoeconomic proof of misbehaviour.

Dynamic aspects such as validator entry/exit, stake delegation, and reward compounding introduce repeated‑game considerations. In a long‑run setting, validators may form cartels to manipulate the random beacon that selects proposers, or they may engage in “stake grinding” by biasing the randomness. Mechanism design solutions include verifiable delay functions (VDFs) for unbiasable randomness, and committee‑based sampling that limits the influence of any single validator. The analysis of these protocols often employs the framework of “resource‑constrained games” where the total stake is a common‑pool resource and the reward function is designed to be a potential game, guaranteeing convergence to a pure‑strategy Nash equilibrium under best‑response dynamics. These results demonstrate how classical game‑theoretic concepts — Nash equilibrium, subgame perfection, potential games, mechanism design — are essential for reasoning about the security and economic sustainability of decentralized consensus.

Game Theory in Supply Chain Contracts: Coordination, Double Marginalization, and Revenue Sharing

Supply chains are natural sequential games where a manufacturer (upstream) sets a wholesale price and a retailer (downstream) chooses a retail price. The classic double‑marginalization problem arises because each firm adds its own markup, leading to a final price higher than the integrated monopoly price and a total profit lower than the joint optimum. In a Stackelberg setting the manufacturer moves first; the subgame‑perfect equilibrium yields a wholesale price w* that maximizes the manufacturer’s profit given the retailer’s best‑response function p(w). The resulting equilibrium quantities are q* = (a - w*) / (2b) for linear demand p = a - bq and constant marginal costs c_m, c_r. Coordination contracts — quantity discounts, two‑part tariffs, buy‑back, and revenue‑sharing — can align incentives. A two‑part tariff (wholesale price = marginal cost plus a fixed fee) achieves the integrated optimum if the fixed fee extracts the retailer’s entire surplus. Revenue‑sharing contracts, where the retailer pays a fraction φ of revenue to the manufacturer and keeps the rest, also achieve coordination when φ = (c_m) / (p) under linear demand. The key condition is that the contract parameters make the retailer’s marginal revenue equal the supply chain’s marginal cost. Empirical work (Cachon & Lariviere, 2005) shows that simple wholesale‑price contracts dominate in practice because of bargaining power asymmetries and information frictions. Extensions incorporate demand uncertainty, where the retailer’s order quantity becomes a newsvendor decision; the optimal buy‑back rate b satisfies b = w - c_m to induce the first‑best order quantity. When multiple retailers compete, the manufacturer’s problem becomes a mechanism design exercise: allocate wholesale prices and quantities to maximize total profit subject to incentive compatibility and individual rationality. The optimal mechanism often resembles a menu of quantity‑discount schedules that screen retailer types. Recent literature adds behavioral considerations — loss aversion and reference dependence — which shift the optimal contract away from the risk‑neutral benchmark.

Game Theory in Electricity Markets: Strategic Bidding, Capacity Mechanisms, and Market Power

Wholesale electricity markets are modeled as uniform‑price auctions where generators submit supply functions (price‑quantity pairs) and the system operator clears the market at the marginal offer that meets demand. The strategic interaction is a supply‑function equilibrium (SFE) game (Klemperer & Meyer, 1989). In a symmetric Cournot‑type SFE with linear demand D(p) = a - bp and n identical generators with constant marginal cost c, each generator’s optimal supply function is linear: S_i(p) = (a - c) / (b(n+1)) + (p - c) / (b(n+1)). The equilibrium price exceeds marginal cost by a markup that shrinks as n grows. Market power is amplified by transmission constraints, which create locational marginal prices (LMPs) and enable generators in congested zones to act as local monopolists. Capacity mechanisms — capacity payments, reliability options, and capacity auctions — are introduced to ensure resource adequacy. In a capacity auction, generators bid capacity prices; the equilibrium can be analyzed as a two‑stage game: first stage capacity investment, second stage energy market bidding. The “missing money” problem arises when energy‑only markets do not provide sufficient scarcity rents to cover fixed costs, leading to under‑investment. The optimal capacity payment equals the expected scarcity rent net of energy‑market profits. Strategic withholding of capacity (physical or economic) can be modeled as a reduction in offered quantity at high prices; the SFE framework captures this through the slope of the supply function. Regulatory mitigation includes offer caps, market power screens (e.g., pivotal supplier test), and demand‑response participation, which flattens the residual demand curve and reduces markups. Recent work integrates renewable intermittency: stochastic supply from wind/solar creates a residual demand distribution, and generators’ optimal supply functions become state‑contingent. The equilibrium analysis uses the concept of “ex‑post” SFE where each realization of renewable output yields a different marginal cost curve.

Worked Example: Supply‑Function Equilibrium with Two Generators
Consider a wholesale electricity market with linear demand D(p) = 100 - p and two symmetric generators with constant marginal cost c = 20. Each generator submits a linear supply function S_i(p) = α + β(p - c). Find the symmetric SFE parameters α and β and the equilibrium price.
In a symmetric SFE with n = 2, the residual demand faced by each generator is D(p) - S_j(p). The first‑order condition for generator i yields β = 1 / (b (n+1)) where b = 1 (slope of demand). Thus β = 1/3. The intercept α satisfies market clearing at the equilibrium price p*: total supply 2(α + β(p* - c)) = 100 - p*. The equilibrium price in a symmetric linear SFE is p* = (a + n c) / (n + 1) with a = 100, c = 20, n = 2 → p* = (100 + 40) / 3 = 140/3 ≈ 46.67. Plugging p* into the clearing condition gives α = (a - c) / (b (n+1)2) = (80) / (9) ≈ 8.89. Each generator’s supply function is S_i(p) = 8.89 + (1/3)(p - 20). The equilibrium price is 46.67 and each generator produces q_i = S_i(p*) ≈ 17.78.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the equilibrium price in a uniform‑price auction with linear supply functions, first identify the number of symmetric firms n, the demand intercept a and slope b, and marginal cost c. Use the closed‑form p* = (a + n c) / (n + 1) for linear demand and constant marginal cost. Then compute quantities from the supply function. Memorize this formula; it appears in multiple guises (capacity auctions, renewable integration).

Game Theory in International Trade Policy: Tariff Games, Retaliation, and the WTO Dispute Settlement

Trade policy between two large countries is a classic prisoner’s dilemma: each country’s optimal tariff given the other’s tariff is positive, but mutual free trade yields higher joint welfare. The Nash equilibrium of the one‑shot tariff game is the pair of optimal tariffs t1^N, t2^N that satisfy each country’s first‑order condition ∂W_i/∂t_i = 0, where welfare W_i includes consumer surplus, producer surplus, and tariff revenue. For linear demand and supply, the optimal tariff for a large country is t* = (p_w - c) / 2 where p_w is the world price and c the foreign marginal cost. In an infinitely repeated game, the folk theorem permits cooperation (zero tariffs) as a subgame‑perfect equilibrium if the discount factor δ exceeds a threshold δ* = (π^D - π^C) / (π^D - π^N), where π^D is the one‑shot deviation payoff, π^C the cooperative payoff, and π^N the Nash payoff. The WTO dispute settlement mechanism acts as a multilateral enforcement device: a ruling authorizes retaliation proportional to the nullification or impairment of benefits. The “optimal retaliation” problem is a mechanism design question — choose a tariff vector on the offending country’s exports that maximizes the complainant’s welfare subject to WTO proportionality constraints. Bagwell and Staiger (1999) show that the WTO’s reciprocity and non‑discrimination principles implement the efficient frontier of the repeated game when countries are symmetric. Asymmetries (size, market power) shift the equilibrium; the larger country may prefer a “tariff war” equilibrium because its optimal tariff extracts more terms‑of‑trade gain. Recent quantitative trade models (e.g., Caliendo & Parro, 2015) embed the tariff game in a general‑equilibrium framework with input‑output linkages, allowing computation of the full welfare effects of tariff escalation and the value of dispute settlement. The “trade war” of 2018‑2019 between the United States and China illustrates the dynamics: initial tariffs, retaliation, and a phase‑one agreement that partially restored cooperation but left many tariffs in place, consistent with a grim‑trigger strategy with imperfect monitoring.

Game Theory in Vaccine Allocation Mechanisms: Priority Design, Incentive Compatibility, and Equity

Allocating scarce vaccines during a pandemic is a mechanism design problem with multi‑dimensional types: individuals differ in health risk, transmission potential, and socioeconomic vulnerability. The planner’s objective combines utilitarian health outcomes (e.g., minimized deaths) and equity concerns (e.g., prioritized access for disadvantaged groups). A direct revelation mechanism asks agents to report their type θ = (risk, exposure, vulnerability) and assigns a priority score π(θ). Incentive compatibility requires that truthful reporting maximizes each agent’s expected utility, which depends on the probability of receiving a dose and the timing. When the allocation rule is monotone in a single‑dimensional risk index, the Myerson‑type payment (or priority) identity yields a simple characterization: the priority function must be non‑decreasing in the reported risk. With multiple dimensions, the problem becomes a multi‑dimensional screening problem; the optimal mechanism generally involves bunching at the boundaries of the type space. The “priority‑score” approach used by many countries (age‑based, comorbidity‑based) can be interpreted as a coarse approximation to the optimal mechanism. Recent work (Akbarpour et al., 2020) shows that a simple “reserve‑price” auction where agents bid for earlier slots using a synthetic currency (e.g., “vaccine dollars” allocated equally) can achieve near‑optimal utilitarian outcomes while preserving equity if the initial endowment is progressive. The design must also address strategic misreporting of health conditions; verification costs and penalties for fraud enter the mechanism as additional constraints. Dynamic allocation — where supply arrives in batches — introduces a sequential screening problem: early batches can be used to learn the distribution of types, improving later allocation. The optimal dynamic mechanism solves a Bellman equation with the state being the posterior belief over the type distribution. Empirical simulations using COVID‑19 data suggest that a hybrid rule — age‑based priority for the first 30 % of doses, followed by a lottery among remaining high‑risk groups — performs within 5 % of the fully optimal mechanism while being transparent and politically feasible.

Game Theory in Decentralized Finance: Automated Market Makers, Impermanent Loss, and Liquidity Mining

Automated market makers (AMMs) such as Uniswap implement a constant‑product bonding curve x·y = k where x and y are reserves of two tokens. Liquidity providers (LPs) deposit tokens in proportion to the current price and receive LP shares representing a claim on the pool. The interaction between traders and LPs is a game: traders choose trade sizes to maximize profit given price impact; LPs choose how much liquidity to supply and whether to withdraw, facing impermanent loss — the difference between the value of their pool share and the value of simply holding the deposited tokens. In a static setting with a single trade of size Δx, the price moves from p = y/x to p' = (y - Δy)/(x + Δx) with Δy = k/(x+Δx) - y. The LP’s payoff change is ΔV = (x+Δx)p' + (y-Δy) - (xp + y). Impermanent loss is zero only if the price returns to its initial level; otherwise it is strictly negative for any price divergence. Liquidity mining programs reward LPs with governance tokens, effectively subsidizing liquidity provision. The equilibrium analysis models LPs as choosing a liquidity level L to maximize expected returns: trading fee revenue γ·Volume plus token rewards minus impermanent loss. The fee rate γ (e.g., 0.3 %) determines the trade‑off; higher fees attract more liquidity but deter volume. In a competitive market with multiple AMMs, LPs allocate capital across pools to equalize risk‑adjusted returns, leading to a Nash equilibrium in liquidity allocation. Recent work (Angeris et al., 2021) shows that the constant‑product AMM is a special case of a broader class of constant‑function market makers (CFMMs) where the trading function φ(R) = k defines the feasible reserve set. The curvature of φ controls price impact and impermanent loss; a flatter curve reduces loss but increases slippage for large trades. Mechanism design for AMMs asks: choose φ and fee schedule to maximize social welfare (trader surplus + LP surplus) subject to incentive compatibility for LPs (no profitable deviation to another pool) and budget balance. The optimal CFMM for a given volatility profile is a “concentrated liquidity” curve (as in Uniswap v3) where LPs specify price ranges, effectively providing a piecewise‑constant product. This design reduces impermanent loss for LPs who correctly anticipate the trading range, but introduces a new strategic dimension: LPs must forecast price paths, turning liquidity provision into a prediction market.

Game Theory in Miner Extractable Value and Transaction Ordering in Blockchain

Miner Extractable Value (MEV) arises when block producers can reorder, insert, or censor transactions within a block to capture profit beyond the standard block reward and gas fees. The strategic interaction between users, searchers, and validators forms a sequential game with imperfect information: users submit transactions with gas bids, searchers observe the mempool and construct bundles, and validators choose the ordering that maximizes their own payoff. The payoff for a validator equals the block reward plus the sum of gas fees from included transactions plus any MEV extracted from the ordering. Searchers compete in a first‑price auction for the right to have their bundle placed at a favorable position; the equilibrium bidding strategy follows the revenue‑equivalence principle when bidders are risk‑neutral and values are independently drawn. However, the presence of a dominant searcher who can front‑run any profitable opportunity creates a Stackelberg leader‑follower structure: the leader commits to a bundle, and followers best‑respond by either abstaining or submitting complementary bundles. The subgame‑perfect equilibrium of this game often involves the leader extracting the full surplus of the arbitrage opportunity, leaving zero rent for followers. In proof‑of‑stake chains with proposer‑builder separation (PBS), the builder runs an auction for block construction, turning the ordering problem into a mechanism design task. The optimal mechanism for the builder is a Vickrey‑Clarke‑Groves (VCG) auction over transaction bundles, which yields efficient allocation and incentive compatibility, but the builder’s own profit motive may lead to a deviation toward a first‑price format that increases revenue at the cost of efficiency. Empirical analyses of Ethereum mainnet show that MEV accounts for a non‑trivial fraction of validator revenue, and that the introduction of Flashbots’ MEV‑Boost relay has shifted the game toward a more competitive builder market, reducing the variance of extracted value across validators.

Game Theory in Quadratic Voting and Quadratic Funding for Public Goods

Quadratic voting (QV) and quadratic funding (QF) are mechanism‑design proposals that use a quadratic cost function to align individual contributions with collective welfare. In QV, each voter purchases votes for a proposal at a cost equal to the square of the number of votes bought; the marginal cost of the k‑th vote is 2k‑1. A voter with valuation v for the outcome chooses the number of votes k to maximize v·k − k², yielding the optimal k* = v/2. The resulting vote count is proportional to the voter’s intensity of preference, and the sum of votes across the population approximates the utilitarian optimum when preferences are independent and the population is large. Quadratic funding extends this idea to the allocation of a matching pool across multiple public‑goods projects: each contributor donates an amount d_i to project i, and the matching pool distributes funds proportionally to (∑√d_i)². The mechanism is strategy‑proof for contributors under the assumption of quasilinear utilities and a fixed matching pool, because the marginal return on an additional dollar of donation equals the square root of the total donations to that project divided by the square root of the donor’s own contribution. The equilibrium of the contribution game is a Nash equilibrium where each donor’s contribution satisfies the first‑order condition d_i = (M·√d_i / ∑√d_j)², with M the matching pool size. In practice, QF has been deployed in Gitcoin grants rounds; empirical work shows that the mechanism reduces the influence of wealthy donors and increases the diversity of funded projects. However, the mechanism is vulnerable to Sybil attacks — a single entity splitting identity to inflate √d_i — and to collusion among contributors who coordinate donations to capture a larger share of the matching pool. Defense mechanisms include identity verification, contribution caps, and the use of a “pairwise” matching formula that penalizes correlated donation patterns.

Identifying the equilibrium transaction ordering strategy in a MEV game
Consider a simplified MEV game with one validator (leader) and two searchers (followers). The validator proposes a block and can order three transactions: a user swap (U), a searcher arbitrage bundle (A₁), and a second searcher arbitrage bundle (A₂). The validator’s payoff is the sum of gas fees: 2 from U, 5 from A₁, and 4 from A₂. Searcher 1 values the arbitrage at 10 if placed before U, 0 otherwise. Searcher 2 values the arbitrage at 8 if placed before U, 0 otherwise. Both searchers submit bids b₁, b₂ to the validator for inclusion before U. The validator chooses the ordering that maximizes its own payoff (gas fees plus accepted bids). Find the subgame‑perfect equilibrium bids and the resulting ordering.
The validator’s payoff for each ordering: (U, A₁, A₂) yields 2+5+4 = 11; (A₁, U, A₂) yields 5+2+4 = 11 plus b₁ if accepted; (A₂, U, A₁) yields 4+2+5 = 11 plus b₂; (A₁, A₂, U) yields 5+4+2 = 11 plus b₁+b₂; (A₂, A₁, U) yields 4+5+2 = 11 plus b₁+b₂. The validator will accept any bid that does not reduce total gas fees, so it accepts both bids if they are non‑negative. Searcher 1’s net payoff if placed before U is 10 − b₁; if not placed before U, payoff is 0. Searcher 2’s net payoff if placed before U is 8 − b₂. In a subgame‑perfect equilibrium, each searcher bids just enough to be included, but not more than its valuation. Since the validator can include both bundles before U without sacrificing gas fees, both searchers will be included. The equilibrium bids are b₁* = 10, b₂* = 8 (or any pair with b₁ ≤ 10, b₂ ≤ 8 that the validator accepts). The resulting ordering is (A₁, A₂, U) or (A₂, A₁, U); the validator is indifferent, so any tie‑breaking rule yields an SPE. The validator’s total payoff = 11 + 10 + 8 = 29.

Identify the leader‑follower structure (Stackelberg) or the simultaneous‑move structure (Nash). Solve the follower’s best‑response, then substitute into the leader’s problem. For quadratic funding, remember the square‑root matching formula and the first‑order condition d_i = (M·√d_i / ∑√d_j)²; use it to compute equilibrium contributions quickly.

Game Theory in Algorithmic Collusion Detection in Repeated Auctions

Repeated auctions provide a natural setting for tacit collusion: bidders can sustain supra‑competitive prices by threatening to revert to a competitive phase if any participant deviates. The classic folk theorem for repeated games states that any feasible, individually rational payoff vector can be sustained as a subgame‑perfect equilibrium if the discount factor δ is sufficiently high. In the context of first‑price or second‑price auctions, the collusive agreement typically involves a designated “winner” who bids a low price while others bid high (or abstain), rotating the winning role to distribute surplus. The incentive‑compatibility constraint for a bidder assigned to lose in a given round is that the one‑shot deviation gain (winning the item at the collusive price) must be less than the discounted future loss from triggering the punishment phase. Formally, if v is the bidder’s valuation, p_c the collusive price, and V_pun the continuation value in the punishment phase, the condition is v − p_c ≤ δ/(1−δ) (V_coll − V_pun), where V_coll is the per‑period collusive payoff. Algorithmic collusion detection exploits the statistical regularities of bidding data: under competition, bids are independent draws from a distribution; under collusion, bids exhibit low variance, systematic rotation, and a sharp drop in the winning bid relative to the second‑highest bid. A standard detection test computes the likelihood ratio between a competitive bidding model (e.g., independent private values with a known distribution) and a collusive model with a rotating low‑bid pattern. The test statistic follows a chi‑square distribution under the null of competition, allowing a regulator to set a significance level. However, sophisticated colluders can mimic competitive randomness by adding noise to their bids, reducing the power of the test. The equilibrium of the detection‑evasion game is a mixed strategy where the colluder randomizes between a pure collusive pattern and a noisy competitive pattern, and the detector randomizes the threshold. The resulting detection probability is strictly less than one, and the optimal collusive profit is reduced but not eliminated. Recent work shows that incorporating side information — such as communication logs or identity links — can restore detection power by expanding the signal space.

Game Theory in Decentralized Autonomous Organization Governance and Proposal Voting

Decentralized Autonomous Organizations (DAOs) govern protocol parameters, treasury allocations, and upgrade decisions through on‑chain voting. The typical governance game involves token holders who stake governance tokens to acquire voting power, submit proposals, and vote during a fixed voting window. The payoff for a token holder is the expected change in token value resulting from the proposal’s execution, minus the opportunity cost of locking tokens. A proposal passes if the total voting power in favor exceeds a quorum threshold Q and the support ratio exceeds a supermajority threshold S (e.g., 60 %). This creates a participation game: each holder decides whether to vote, and if so, how to vote. When voting is costless, the dominant strategy is to vote truthfully if the holder’s preference aligns with the proposal’s expected impact on token value. However, when voting requires gas fees or delegation costs, a free‑rider problem emerges: a holder with small stake may abstain, relying on others to reach quorum. The equilibrium participation rate can be characterized by a threshold stake size s* such that holders with stake ≥ s* vote and those below abstain. The threshold solves the indifference condition: the marginal benefit of voting (the probability of being pivotal times the value swing) equals the marginal cost. In large DAOs, the pivotal probability is approximately the probability that the total vote count without the holder falls in the interval [Q−s, Q] or [S·Total−s, S·Total], which can be approximated by a normal distribution via the Central Limit Theorem. Delegation introduces a principal‑agent layer: token holders delegate voting power to delegates who may have different preferences. The delegate’s optimal strategy is to vote in line with the median delegator’s preference if the delegate’s reputation payoff is concave in the distance from the median. Empirical studies of major DAOs (e.g., MakerDAO, Uniswap) show that voter turnout is typically below 10 %, and that a small number of delegates control a majority of voting power, creating a de facto oligarchy. Mechanism‑design improvements include quadratic voting for governance (to reduce plutocratic influence), conviction voting (where voting power grows with the time tokens are locked), and holographic consensus (where a prediction market filters proposals before on‑chain voting).

Game Theory in Cross‑Chain Arbitrage and Atomic Swaps

Cross‑chain arbitrage exploits price discrepancies of the same asset on different blockchains. An arbitrageur simultaneously buys the asset on the cheaper chain and sells on the more expensive chain, locking in a risk‑free profit if the two legs execute atomically. Atomic swaps implement this using Hashed Time‑Lock Contracts (HTLCs): the arbitrageur generates a secret preimage x, computes its hash h = H(x), and creates two HTLCs — one on chain A paying the seller upon revelation of x, and one on chain B paying the buyer upon revelation of x. Both contracts have a timeout T after which funds are refunded if x is not revealed. The game is a two‑stage sequential game with perfect information: in stage 1, the arbitrageur posts the HTLCs; in stage 2, the counterparties decide whether to claim by revealing x before timeout. The arbitrageur’s payoff is the price spread minus gas fees on both chains, provided both claims succeed. A counterparty’s payoff from claiming is the agreed price minus the market price on that chain; if the market moves against the counterparty after the HTLC is posted, the counterparty may prefer to let the contract timeout and receive a refund, causing the arbitrageur to lose the gas fees on the other chain. This creates a hold‑up problem: the arbitrageur must set the HTLC prices such that each counterparty’s incentive to claim dominates the option to wait. The subgame‑perfect equilibrium requires the arbitrageur to offer a price on each chain that is at least the counterparty’s expected market price at timeout minus a risk premium. If the price spread is smaller than the sum of gas fees and risk premiums, no profitable atomic swap exists. In practice, relayers and cross‑chain bridges (e.g., Wormhole, LayerZero) act as intermediaries that assume the counterparty risk, turning the game into a principal‑agent problem where the relayer charges a fee for guaranteeing execution. The equilibrium fee equals the relayer’s expected loss from failed swaps plus a competitive markup. Recent protocol designs (e.g., intent‑based architectures) allow users to express a desired outcome (swap asset X on chain A for asset Y on chain B) and let a network of solvers compete to fulfill the intent, effectively transforming the atomic‑swap game into a sealed‑bid auction among solvers. The solver’s bid is the maximum spread they are willing to accept; the user selects the solver offering the best rate. The resulting equilibrium is a first‑price auction where solvers shade their bids below the true spread, and the user’s expected surplus is the difference between the true spread and the winning bid.

Game Theory in Online Advertising Auctions: Generalized Second‑Price and VCG Variants

The dominant auction format for sponsored search is the Generalized Second‑Price (GSP) auction. Advertisers submit bids bi for a keyword; slots are allocated in decreasing order of bids, and the winner of slot k pays the bid of the next lower advertiser. Unlike the Vickrey‑Clarke‑Groves (VCG) mechanism, GSP is not dominant‑strategy incentive compatible. In a complete‑information setting the set of pure‑strategy Nash equilibria of GSP coincides with the set of envy‑free outcomes of the corresponding VCG auction (Edelman, Ostrovsky & Schwarz 2007; Varian 2007). An envy‑free equilibrium requires that no advertiser prefers the slot and payment of another advertiser given the click‑through rates (CTR) of the slots.

With incomplete information, the symmetric Bayes‑Nash equilibrium of GSP can be characterized by a system of differential equations linking the bidding function β(v) to the distribution of values. The equilibrium bidding function is strictly increasing and lies below the VCG truthful bid. Revenue equivalence does not hold across GSP and VCG because the allocation rule differs: GSP allocates based on bids, VCG allocates based on reported values and charges the externality imposed on others. Empirical work on Google and Yahoo! auction data shows that observed bids are close to the envy‑free equilibrium of the complete‑information game, suggesting that advertisers learn to coordinate on the “locally envy‑free” outcome.

Variants such as the “Generalized English Auction” (a dynamic ascending version) and the “Core‑Selecting Auction” (which chooses the VCG outcome in the core of the reported preferences) have been proposed to improve incentive properties. Core‑selecting auctions guarantee that no coalition of advertisers can block the outcome, but they sacrifice dominant‑strategy truthfulness. In practice, platforms retain GSP because of its simplicity, transparency, and the fact that advertisers can use automated bidding agents that approximate the Bayes‑Nash equilibrium.

Game Theory in Federated Learning: Incentive Compatibility and Data Valuation

Federated learning (FL) trains a global model across many devices while keeping raw data local. The central server must incentivize participants to contribute high‑quality updates and to report their local model improvements truthfully. This is a mechanism‑design problem: each client i holds a private data set Di with marginal contribution φi to the global loss. The server designs a payment rule pi(report) that makes truthful reporting a dominant strategy (or at least a Bayes‑Nash equilibrium) while satisfying budget balance and individual rationality.

The Shapley value provides a theoretically appealing allocation of the total model gain among clients because it satisfies efficiency, symmetry, dummy, and additivity. However, computing exact Shapley values requires evaluating the model on all 2n subsets, which is infeasible for large n. Approximation methods — Monte‑Carlo sampling, stratified sampling, and the “Shapley‑value‑based data valuation” (Ghorbani & Zou 2019) — reduce complexity but introduce variance that can be exploited by strategic clients. Recent work shows that a “verifiable” FL protocol using zero‑knowledge proofs can enforce that the reported update equals the locally computed gradient, turning the game into one of pure contribution rather than misreporting.

When clients have heterogeneous data quality, the optimal contract may involve a menu of (effort, payment) pairs that screen types (Laffont & Martimort 2002). The principal (server) offers a contract that satisfies incentive compatibility (IC) and participation (IR) constraints. The resulting allocation typically gives higher payments to clients with larger, more diverse data, but the server must also guard against “free‑riding” where a client submits a trivial update to collect a baseline payment. Dynamic contracts that condition future payments on cumulative contribution mitigate this, at the cost of increased communication overhead.

Identifying the equilibrium bidding strategy in a Generalized Second‑Price auction with incomplete information
Suppose three advertisers compete for two slots with click‑through rates 0.8 and 0.5. Their private values per click are v1=10, v2=8, v3=6. Assuming symmetric Bayes‑Nash equilibrium of the GSP auction, compute the expected payment of the highest‑value bidder.
In the symmetric Bayes‑Nash equilibrium each bidder uses a strictly increasing bidding function β(v). With two slots the highest bidder wins slot 1 (CTR 0.8) and pays the second‑highest bid β(v(2)). The expected payment of the highest‑value bidder (v=10) is E[β(V(2)) | V(1)=10]. For a uniform distribution on [0,10] the order statistic density of the second‑highest of three draws conditional on the maximum being 10 is f(v(2)|v(1)=10)=2·(v/10) for v∈[0,10]. The equilibrium bidding function for GSP with two slots and CTRs α1=0.8, α2=0.5 solves β(v)=v−∫0v(α2/α1)·(F(t)/F(v))2dt. Substituting F(t)=t/10 yields β(v)=v−(0.5/0.8)·∫0v(t/v)2dt = v−(5/8)·(v/3)= (19/24)v. Hence β(v(2)) = (19/24)v(2). The conditional expectation is (19/24)·E[V(2)|V(1)=10] = (19/24)·(2/3)·10 = (19/24)·(20/3) ≈ 5.28. The highest‑value bidder’s expected payment is approximately 5.28 currency units.
Exam‑day strategy
When a question asks for the revenue equivalence between GSP and VCG, remember that GSP does not satisfy the dominant‑strategy property; use the envy‑free equilibrium concept for complete information and the symmetric Bayes‑Nash equilibrium for incomplete information. Write down the indifference condition that defines the bidding function before attempting any integration.

Game Theory in Autonomous Vehicle Coordination: Intersection Management and Platooning

Autonomous vehicles (AVs) approaching an unsignalized intersection face a coordination game that can be modeled as a multi‑player game of chicken. Each vehicle chooses a crossing time; if two vehicles select overlapping intervals a collision occurs (large negative payoff), while crossing early yields a small time gain. The set of pure‑strategy Nash equilibria corresponds to all possible priority orderings that avoid overlap. A socially optimal outcome minimizes total delay, which is a centralized scheduling problem. Decentralized protocols — such as reservation‑based intersection management (Dresner & Stone 2008) — implement a Stackelberg game where the intersection manager (leader) announces a schedule and vehicles (followers) best‑respond by adjusting speed. The subgame‑perfect equilibrium of this Stackelberg game coincides with the centralized optimum if the manager can commit to the schedule and vehicles have perfect information.

Platooning — vehicles traveling in close formation to reduce drag — is a repeated game. In each period a vehicle decides whether to join the platoon (cooperate) or break away (defect). The stage‑game payoff gives a fuel saving s to each follower and a small cost c to the leader for maintaining communication. The folk theorem implies that for a sufficiently high discount factor δ, the cooperative outcome (all stay in platoon) can be sustained as a subgame‑perfect equilibrium using trigger strategies: any deviation triggers permanent reversion to the non‑cooperative Nash equilibrium (no platooning). The critical discount factor is δ* = (gain from deviation) / (gain from deviation + loss from punishment). Empirical studies on highway data estimate δ* ≈ 0.7, suggesting that platooning is viable for long‑haul trucks but less so for short urban trips where the horizon is short.

Communication delays and packet loss introduce imperfect monitoring. The resulting game is a repeated game with private monitoring, where equilibrium construction requires belief‑free strategies (e.g., the “review strategy” of Horner & Olszewski 2009). Robust platooning protocols therefore incorporate a tolerance window: a vehicle is punished only after a statistically significant number of missed beacons, reducing false‑positive defections.

Game Theory in Carbon Markets: Cap‑and‑Trade, Banking, and Market Power

A cap‑and‑trade system creates a market for emission allowances. The regulator sets a total cap Q and distributes allowances either by grandfathering or auction. Firms choose abatement levels ai and trade allowances to equate marginal abatement cost (MAC) across firms. In a competitive market the equilibrium price p* equals the common MAC at the aggregate abatement target. The Coase theorem guarantees cost‑effectiveness regardless of the initial allocation, provided transaction costs are negligible.

Banking (saving allowances for future periods) and borrowing (using future allowances today) introduce intertemporal linking. The Hotelling rule for a non‑renewable resource implies that the allowance price should rise at the rate of interest in the absence of policy shocks. However, regulatory uncertainty (e.g., future cap tightening) creates a “policy risk premium” that can cause price volatility. The EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) introduced a Market Stability Reserve (MSR) that automatically adjusts the supply of allowances based on the total number of allowances in circulation, acting as a feedback controller to dampen price spikes.

Market power arises when a few large emitters (e.g., electricity generators) hold a significant share of allowances. A dominant firm can withhold allowances to raise the price, increasing its profit from both allowance sales and higher electricity prices. The equilibrium in a Cournot‑type allowance market with a dominant firm and a competitive fringe yields a price above the competitive level. The regulator can mitigate this by auctioning a larger share of allowances, imposing holding limits, or using a price floor (as in the California‑Quebec linked market). Empirical analysis of the EU ETS Phase III shows that the largest 10 firms held roughly 30 % of allowances, and structural models estimate a markup of 5‑10 % over the competitive price.

Game Theory in Crowdsourcing and Peer Prediction: Proper Scoring Rules and Bayesian Truth Serum

Crowdsourcing platforms must elicit accurate information from self‑interested agents who observe a private signal about an unknown state. When ground truth is unavailable, peer‑prediction mechanisms reward agents based on the correlation between their report and the reports of others. The Bayesian Truth Serum (BTS) (Prelec 2004) asks each agent to report both their signal and a prediction of the distribution of others’ signals. The payment combines a proper scoring rule for the signal report (e.g., logarithmic score) with a scoring rule for the prediction report. Under the assumption that agents share a common prior and that signals are conditionally independent given the state, truthful reporting is a strict Bayes‑Nash equilibrium.

Proper scoring rules — logarithmic, quadratic, spherical — guarantee that an agent maximizes expected score by reporting their true belief. In the peer‑prediction setting the “peer” report serves as a proxy for the state. The Robust Bayesian Truth Serum (RBTS) and the Peer‑Prediction Mechanism with a “minimal” prior (Witkowski & Parkes 2012) relax the common‑prior assumption by using a hierarchical Bayesian model. These mechanisms remain incentive compatible provided the population size is large enough for the law of large numbers to make the empirical distribution converge to the true distribution.

When agents have heterogeneous expertise, a “weighted” peer‑prediction mechanism can assign higher weight to agents with a track record of accurate predictions. This creates a dynamic game where agents invest effort to improve their reputation, which in turn increases their future influence. The equilibrium analysis shows a trade‑off: excessive weight on past performance can discourage new entrants, while too little weight reduces the incentive to acquire expertise. Platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk and prediction markets (e.g., Augur) implement variants of these mechanisms, often combining them with monetary bonuses for consistent high‑quality contributions.

  • Ignoring the effect of banking and borrowing on the Hotelling price path in cap‑and‑trade; the price does not necessarily rise at the interest rate when policy interventions exist.
