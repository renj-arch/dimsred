Electromagnetism — Electric & Magnetic Fields, Maxwell's Equations, Circuits & EM Waves

Electromagnetism is the study of electric and magnetic fields, their sources (charges and currents), and their interactions. The electric field E is defined by the force on a test charge: F = qE. Coulomb's law F = kq₁q₂/r² governs the force between static charges. Gauss's law ∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀ relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the enclosed charge. The magnetic field B exerts a Lorentz force F = qv × B on moving charges. Ampere's law ∮ B·dl = μ₀I_enc gives the magnetic field due to a current; the Biot-Savart law provides the differential form. Faraday's law of induction ∮ E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt states that a changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force — the principle behind generators and transformers. Maxwell's equations unify electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single theory: ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t. The displacement current ∂E/∂t term is Maxwell's crowning achievement — it predicts electromagnetic waves traveling at speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀), and reveals that light is an electromagnetic wave. The Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀) E × B describes the energy flux carried by EM waves.

"One of the greatest achievements in the history of science — the union of electricity, magnetism, and optics."
— James Clerk Maxwell
Part 1

⚡ Electric Fields, Potential, and Capacitance

Electrostatics
Coulomb's Law, the Electric Field, Gauss's Law, Electric Potential, and Capacitors
Two charges q₁ and q₂ separated by distance r experience a force F = k q₁ q₂ / r² r̂ (Coulomb's law), where k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². The electric field E = F/q₀ is the force per unit test charge. For a point charge q: E = kq/r² r̂. The principle of superposition: the total field is the vector sum of fields from all charges. Gauss's law: ∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀, which is particularly useful for symmetric charge distributions (spherical, cylindrical, planar). The electric potential V is the line integral of E: V_b − V_a = −∫_a^b E·dl. For a point charge: V = kq/r. The potential difference between two points is the work per unit charge to move a test charge between them. Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to E. The relationship E = −∇V. Conductors: charges reside on the surface, E inside a conductor is zero, the surface is an equipotential. Capacitance: C = Q/V, measured in farads (F). For a parallel plate capacitor: C = ε₀A/d. Dielectrics increase capacitance by a factor of κ (the dielectric constant): C = κC₀. Energy stored in a capacitor: U = ½QV = ½CV². Capacitors in series combine as 1/C_eq = Σ 1/C_i; in parallel as C_eq = Σ C_i.
Electrostatics is governed by Coulomb's law and Gauss's law; the electric potential provides a scalar alternative to the vector field, and capacitors store energy in the electric field between conductors.
Part 2

🧲 Magnetic Fields, Lorentz Force, and Electromagnetic Induction

Magnetism
The Lorentz Force, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, Faraday's Law, and Inductance
The Lorentz force on a charge q moving with velocity v in electric and magnetic fields: F = q(E + v × B). The magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity — it does no work, only changes direction. The Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field due to a current element: dB = (μ₀/4π) Idl × r̂/r². For a long straight wire: B = μ₀I/(2πr). For a solenoid: B = μ₀nI (where n is turns per unit length). Ampere's law: ∮ B·dl = μ₀I_enc (for steady currents). The magnetic vector potential A satisfies B = ∇×A and ∇·A = 0 (Coulomb gauge). Faraday's law of induction: the induced EMF around a closed loop equals the negative rate of change of magnetic flux: ε = −dΦ_B/dt. Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it — this is the minus sign in Faraday's law. Motional EMF: when a conductor moves through a magnetic field, the Lorentz force on charges generates an EMF: ε = Blv (for a rod of length l moving at speed v perpendicular to B). Inductance: the self-inductance L relates the flux through a coil to its own current: Φ = LI. The induced EMF is ε = −L dI/dt. Energy stored in an inductor: U = ½LI². Mutual inductance M links two coils: ε₂ = −M dI₁/dt. RL circuits: I(t) = (ε/R)(1 − e^{−Rt/L}) (rise) and I(t) = I₀ e^{−Rt/L} (decay). Transformers: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p (ideal transformer), with P_in = P_out.
Magnetic fields are produced by moving charges and exert forces on moving charges; Faraday's law of induction is the principle behind electric generators, transformers, and inductors.
Part 3

📡 Maxwell's Equations and Electromagnetic Waves

Unification
Maxwell's Equations, the Displacement Current, the Wave Equation, and the Electromagnetic Spectrum
Maxwell's equations in differential form: (1) ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss's law for electricity); (2) ∇·B = 0 (Gauss's law for magnetism — no magnetic monopoles); (3) ∇×E = −∂B/∂t (Faraday's law); (4) ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t (Ampere's law with Maxwell's addition). The displacement current density J_d = ε₀∂E/∂t is Maxwell's crucial addition — it ensures that Ampere's law is consistent with charge conservation and makes the equations symmetric. In vacuum (ρ = 0, J = 0), Maxwell's equations become: ∇·E = 0, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×B = μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t. Taking the curl of Faraday's law gives the wave equation for E and B: ∇²E = μ₀ε₀∂²E/∂t², ∇²B = μ₀ε₀∂²B/∂t². The wave speed is c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s — the speed of light. EM waves are transverse: E, B, and the propagation direction k are mutually perpendicular. The ratio E/B = c. The Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀)E × B gives the energy flux (W/m²). The intensity I = ⟨S⟩ = (1/2)cε₀E₀² (for a sinusoidal wave). The electromagnetic spectrum: radio waves (λ > 1 m), microwaves (1 m to 1 mm), infrared, visible light (400-700 nm), ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays (λ < 0.01 nm). All travel at speed c in vacuum. Polarization: the direction of E defines the polarization state — linear, circular, or elliptical. Radiation pressure: P = I/c (for absorption) and P = 2I/c (for reflection).
Maxwell's equations are the crowning achievement of classical electromagnetism — they predict electromagnetic waves, unify electricity and magnetism with optics, and reveal that light is an EM wave.
Part 4

🔌 Advanced Topics: Circuits, AC Theory, and Electrodynamics

Applications
AC Circuits, Phasors, Impedance, RLC Circuits, Transmission Lines, and Relativistic Electrodynamics
AC circuits: alternating current V(t) = V₀ sin(ωt). The phasor representation represents sinusoidal quantities as complex exponentials: Ṽ = V₀ e^{jωt}. Impedance Z = Ṽ/Ĩ generalizes resistance to AC: Z_R = R, Z_L = jωL, Z_C = 1/(jωC). The phase angle φ between voltage and current: tan φ = (X_L − X_C)/R, where X_L = ωL is inductive reactance and X_C = 1/ωC is capacitive reactance. Resonance in RLC circuits: ω₀ = 1/√(LC), quality factor Q = ω₀L/R. Power in AC circuits: P_avg = V_rms I_rms cos φ, where cos φ is the power factor. Maxwell's equations in matter: ∇·D = ρ_f, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×H = J_f + ∂D/∂t, where D = εE, B = μH. The Poynting theorem: ∇·S + ∂u/∂t = −J·E (energy conservation), where u = ½(ε₀E² + B²/μ₀) is the electromagnetic energy density. The Maxwell stress tensor T_ij = ε₀(E_iE_j − ½δ_ijE²) + (1/μ₀)(B_iB_j − ½δ_ijB²) describes the momentum flux. Relativistic electrodynamics: the EM field tensor F^{μν} = ∂^{μ}A^{ν} − ∂^{ν}A^{μ} unifies E and B into a single Lorentz-covariant object. Under Lorentz transformations, E and B mix — what appears as an electric field in one frame appears partly as a magnetic field in another. The Liénard-Wiechert potentials give the fields of a moving point charge, and the Larmor formula P = (μ₀q²a²)/(6πc) gives the power radiated by an accelerating charge.
AC circuit theory is the practical application of electromagnetism to power distribution and electronics; relativistic electrodynamics reveals the deep unity of electric and magnetic fields as components of a single tensor.
