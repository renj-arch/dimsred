Electromagnetism — Electric & Magnetic Fields, Maxwell's Equations, Circuits & EM Waves

Electromagnetism is the study of electric and magnetic fields, their sources (charges and currents), and their interactions. The electric field E is defined by the force on a test charge: F = qE. Coulomb's law F = kq₁q₂/r² governs the force between static charges. Gauss's law ∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀ relates the electric flux through a closed surface to the enclosed charge. The magnetic field B exerts a Lorentz force F = qv × B on moving charges. Ampere's law ∮ B·dl = μ₀I_enc gives the magnetic field due to a current; the Biot-Savart law provides the differential form. Faraday's law of induction ∮ E·dl = −dΦ_B/dt states that a changing magnetic flux induces an electromotive force — the principle behind generators and transformers. Maxwell's equations unify electricity, magnetism, and optics into a single theory: ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t. The displacement current ∂E/∂t term is Maxwell's crowning achievement — it predicts electromagnetic waves traveling at speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀), and reveals that light is an electromagnetic wave. The Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀) E × B describes the energy flux carried by EM waves.

"One of the greatest achievements in the history of science — the union of electricity, magnetism, and optics."
— James Clerk Maxwell
Part 1

⚡ Electric Fields, Potential, and Capacitance

Electrostatics
Coulomb's Law, the Electric Field, Gauss's Law, Electric Potential, and Capacitors
Two charges q₁ and q₂ separated by distance r experience a force F = k q₁ q₂ / r² r̂ (Coulomb's law), where k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². The electric field E = F/q₀ is the force per unit test charge. For a point charge q: E = kq/r² r̂. The principle of superposition: the total field is the vector sum of fields from all charges. Gauss's law: ∮ E·dA = Q_enc/ε₀, which is particularly useful for symmetric charge distributions (spherical, cylindrical, planar). The electric potential V is the line integral of E: V_b − V_a = −∫_a^b E·dl. For a point charge: V = kq/r. The potential difference between two points is the work per unit charge to move a test charge between them. Equipotential surfaces are perpendicular to E. The relationship E = −∇V. Conductors: charges reside on the surface, E inside a conductor is zero, the surface is an equipotential. Capacitance: C = Q/V, measured in farads (F). For a parallel plate capacitor: C = ε₀A/d. Dielectrics increase capacitance by a factor of κ (the dielectric constant): C = κC₀. Energy stored in a capacitor: U = ½QV = ½CV². Capacitors in series combine as 1/C_eq = Σ 1/C_i; in parallel as C_eq = Σ C_i.
Electrostatics is governed by Coulomb's law and Gauss's law; the electric potential provides a scalar alternative to the vector field, and capacitors store energy in the electric field between conductors.
Part 2

🧲 Magnetic Fields, Lorentz Force, and Electromagnetic Induction

Magnetism
The Lorentz Force, Biot-Savart Law, Ampere's Law, Faraday's Law, and Inductance
The Lorentz force on a charge q moving with velocity v in electric and magnetic fields: F = q(E + v × B). The magnetic force is always perpendicular to velocity — it does no work, only changes direction. The Biot-Savart law gives the magnetic field due to a current element: dB = (μ₀/4π) Idl × r̂/r². For a long straight wire: B = μ₀I/(2πr). For a solenoid: B = μ₀nI (where n is turns per unit length). Ampere's law: ∮ B·dl = μ₀I_enc (for steady currents). The magnetic vector potential A satisfies B = ∇×A and ∇·A = 0 (Coulomb gauge). Faraday's law of induction: the induced EMF around a closed loop equals the negative rate of change of magnetic flux: ε = −dΦ_B/dt. Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in flux that produced it — this is the minus sign in Faraday's law. Motional EMF: when a conductor moves through a magnetic field, the Lorentz force on charges generates an EMF: ε = Blv (for a rod of length l moving at speed v perpendicular to B). Inductance: the self-inductance L relates the flux through a coil to its own current: Φ = LI. The induced EMF is ε = −L dI/dt. Energy stored in an inductor: U = ½LI². Mutual inductance M links two coils: ε₂ = −M dI₁/dt. RL circuits: I(t) = (ε/R)(1 − e^{−Rt/L}) (rise) and I(t) = I₀ e^{−Rt/L} (decay). Transformers: V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p (ideal transformer), with P_in = P_out.
Magnetic fields are produced by moving charges and exert forces on moving charges; Faraday's law of induction is the principle behind electric generators, transformers, and inductors.
Part 3

📡 Maxwell's Equations and Electromagnetic Waves

Unification
Maxwell's Equations, the Displacement Current, the Wave Equation, and the Electromagnetic Spectrum
Maxwell's equations in differential form: (1) ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ (Gauss's law for electricity); (2) ∇·B = 0 (Gauss's law for magnetism — no magnetic monopoles); (3) ∇×E = −∂B/∂t (Faraday's law); (4) ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t (Ampere's law with Maxwell's addition). The displacement current density J_d = ε₀∂E/∂t is Maxwell's crucial addition — it ensures that Ampere's law is consistent with charge conservation and makes the equations symmetric. In vacuum (ρ = 0, J = 0), Maxwell's equations become: ∇·E = 0, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×B = μ₀ε₀∂E/∂t. Taking the curl of Faraday's law gives the wave equation for E and B: ∇²E = μ₀ε₀∂²E/∂t², ∇²B = μ₀ε₀∂²B/∂t². The wave speed is c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3 × 10⁸ m/s — the speed of light. EM waves are transverse: E, B, and the propagation direction k are mutually perpendicular. The ratio E/B = c. The Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀)E × B gives the energy flux (W/m²). The intensity I = ⟨S⟩ = (1/2)cε₀E₀² (for a sinusoidal wave). The electromagnetic spectrum: radio waves (λ > 1 m), microwaves (1 m to 1 mm), infrared, visible light (400-700 nm), ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays (λ < 0.01 nm). All travel at speed c in vacuum. Polarization: the direction of E defines the polarization state — linear, circular, or elliptical. Radiation pressure: P = I/c (for absorption) and P = 2I/c (for reflection).
Maxwell's equations are the crowning achievement of classical electromagnetism — they predict electromagnetic waves, unify electricity and magnetism with optics, and reveal that light is an EM wave.

🔌 Advanced Topics: Circuits, AC Theory, and Electrodynamics

Applications
AC Circuits, Phasors, Impedance, RLC Circuits, Transmission Lines, and Relativistic Electrodynamics
AC circuits: alternating current V(t) = V₀ sin(ωt). The phasor representation represents sinusoidal quantities as complex exponentials: Ṽ = V₀ e^{jωt}. Impedance Z = Ṽ/Ĩ generalizes resistance to AC: Z_R = R, Z_L = jωL, Z_C = 1/(jωC). The phase angle φ between voltage and current: tan φ = (X_L − X_C)/R, where X_L = ωL is inductive reactance and X_C = 1/ωC is capacitive reactance. Resonance in RLC circuits: ω₀ = 1/√(LC), quality factor Q = ω₀L/R. Power in AC circuits: P_avg = V_rms I_rms cos φ, where cos φ is the power factor. Maxwell's equations in matter: ∇·D = ρ_f, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×H = J_f + ∂D/∂t, where D = εE, B = μH. The Poynting theorem: ∇·S + ∂u/∂t = −J·E (energy conservation), where u = ½(ε₀E² + B²/μ₀) is the electromagnetic energy density. The Maxwell stress tensor T_ij = ε₀(E_iE_j − ½δ_ijE²) + (1/μ₀)(B_iB_j − ½δ_ijB²) describes the momentum flux. Relativistic electrodynamics: the EM field tensor F^{μν} = ∂^{μ}A^{ν} − ∂^{ν}A^{μ} unifies E and B into a single Lorentz-covariant object. Under Lorentz transformations, E and B mix — what appears as an electric field in one frame appears partly as a magnetic field in another. The Liénard-Wiechert potentials give the fields of a moving point charge, and the Larmor formula P = (μ₀q²a²)/(6πc) gives the power radiated by an accelerating charge.
AC circuit theory is the practical application of electromagnetism to power distribution and electronics; relativistic electrodynamics reveals the deep unity of electric and magnetic fields as components of a single tensor.

Radiation Reaction and the Abraham‑Lorentz Force

When a charged particle accelerates it emits electromagnetic radiation. Conservation of momentum demands that the particle experience a recoil force, known as the radiation‑reaction or Abraham‑Lorentz force. The classical expression for a non‑relativistic point charge q is

F_rad = (μ₀ q² / 6π c) ˙a = (q² / 6π ε₀ c³) ˙a,

where ˙a is the time derivative of the acceleration (the “jerk”). This term appears in the equation of motion

m ˙v = q(E + v × B) + F_rad.

Key concepts:

  • Origin of the term: It follows from evaluating the self‑field of an accelerating charge and integrating the Poynting vector over a spherical surface surrounding the particle.
  • Runaway solutions: The third‑order differential equation admits solutions where the velocity grows exponentially even in the absence of external fields. Physically unacceptable, these solutions are eliminated by imposing the “asymptotic condition” that the acceleration vanishes as t → ∞.
  • Pre‑acceleration: The equation predicts that a particle begins to move before the external force is applied, a paradox resolved by recognizing the limits of classical electrodynamics; quantum electrodynamics provides a more consistent description.
  • Relativistic generalisation: In covariant form the radiation‑reaction four‑force is

F^μ_rad = (μ₀ q² / 6π m) (d²u^μ / dτ² + u^μ u_ν d²u^ν / dτ²),

with u^μ the four‑velocity and τ the proper time. This form respects Lorentz invariance and reduces to the non‑relativistic expression for low speeds.

Exam‑relevant applications include: (i) estimating the energy loss of electrons in synchrotrons, (ii) analysing the damping of oscillations in a radiating dipole antenna, and (iii) discussing the limits of classical theory in high‑field laser–matter interactions. Remember to state assumptions (point charge, non‑relativistic limit) before writing the formula, as examiners often penalise unqualified statements.

Skin Effect, Proximity Effect and Their Impact on AC Resistance

In alternating current (AC) conductors the current density is not uniform across the cross‑section. Two related phenomena dominate:

  1. Skin effect: At angular frequency ω the current tends to flow within a surface layer of thickness δ (the skin depth), given by

δ = √(2 / (ω μ σ)),

where μ is the magnetic permeability and σ the electrical conductivity of the material. For copper at 60 Hz, δ ≈ 8.5 mm; at 1 MHz it drops to ≈ 66 µm. The effective resistance R_ac increases roughly as R_ac ≈ R_dc (A / A_eff), where A_eff is the cross‑section occupied by the skin layer.

  1. Proximity effect: In multi‑turn windings or parallel conductors, the magnetic field of one conductor induces eddy currents in its neighbours, further concentrating current toward the outer surfaces. This effect is strongest when the spacing between conductors is comparable to δ.

Consequences for design:

  • High‑frequency transformers and inductors use Litz wire—bundles of insulated fine strands—to increase the total surface area and mitigate both effects.
  • Printed circuit board (PCB) traces at GHz frequencies are deliberately made wide and thin to reduce R_ac.
  • In power‑line cables, the skin effect raises losses above 1 kHz; thus aluminium conductors are often used with larger cross‑sections to keep R_ac acceptable.

Quantitative example: A solid copper rod of radius 5 mm carries 60 Hz AC. Using δ ≈ 8.5 mm, the current penetrates the whole cross‑section, so R_ac ≈ R_dc. At 10 kHz, δ ≈ 0.66 mm; the effective conducting area becomes A_eff ≈ 2πrδ ≈ 2π·5·0.66 mm² ≈ 21 mm² versus the full area πr² ≈ 79 mm², giving R_ac ≈ 3.8 R_dc.

Exam‑takers should be able to derive δ, discuss its frequency dependence, and explain why Litz wire is advantageous. A common mistake is to treat skin depth as a fixed material property; always relate it to ω, μ and σ.

Worked Example – AC Resistance of a Multi‑Strand Conductor
A Litz wire consists of 100 copper strands, each of radius 0.1 mm, insulated from each other. The wire carries a sinusoidal current of frequency 500 kHz. Calculate the ratio of the AC resistance of the Litz wire to that of a solid copper wire of equivalent total cross‑section.

1. Total cross‑section of Litz wire: A_total = 100·π·(0.1 mm)² = 100·π·0.01 mm² = π mm² ≈ 3.14 mm².

2. Equivalent solid wire radius r_eq satisfies π·r_eq² = 3.14 mm² ⇒ r_eq = 1 mm.

3. Skin depth at 500 kHz for copper (μ≈μ₀, σ≈5.8×10⁷ S m⁻¹): δ = √[2/(ωμσ)] = √[2/(2π·5×10⁵·4π×10⁻⁷·5.8×10⁷)] ≈ 0.093 mm.

4. For the solid wire (r_eq = 1 mm > δ), effective area A_eff,solid ≈ 2π·r_eq·δ = 2π·1·0.093 mm² ≈ 0.585 mm².

5. AC resistance ratio R_ac,solid / R_dc = A_total / A_eff,solid ≈ 3.14 / 0.585 ≈ 5.37.

6. For each strand (r = 0.1 mm < δ), the current penetrates the whole strand, so each strand’s AC resistance ≈ its DC resistance. Hence the Litz wire’s AC resistance ≈ its DC resistance.

7. Ratio of Litz R_ac to solid R_ac ≈ 1 / 5.37 ≈ 0.19. Thus the Litz construction reduces the AC resistance to about 19 % of that of an equivalent solid conductor.

Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for “the effect of frequency on resistance”, first write the skin‑depth formula, then state the qualitative trend (R_ac ↑ with f) before plugging numbers. If the problem involves a multi‑turn coil, explicitly mention the proximity effect; ignoring it is a frequent source of lost marks.

Waveguides, Cut‑off Frequencies and Mode Classification

Guiding electromagnetic energy in confined structures is essential for microwave engineering and accelerator technology. A rectangular waveguide of dimensions a (wide side) and b (narrow side) supports transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes, labelled TE_mn or TM_mn where m and n are the number of half‑wave variations across a and b respectively.

The cut‑off frequency for a given mode is

f_c(m,n) = (c / 2) √[(m/a)² + (n/b)²],

with c the speed of light. Below f_c the mode is evanescent and cannot propagate; above f_c the propagation constant is

β = √[ (2πf / c)² – (πm/a)² – (πn/b)² ].

Key observations:

  • The dominant mode in a rectangular waveguide is TE₁₀ because it has the lowest cut‑off (m=1, n=0). Its cut‑off frequency f_c10 = c / (2a). Designers therefore choose a such that the operating band lies well above f_c10 but below the next higher mode (usually TE₂₀ or TE₀₁) to avoid mode‑mixing.
  • For a circular waveguide, the dominant mode is TE₁₁ with cut‑off f_c = 1.841c / (2πR), where R is the radius. The Bessel‑function zeros determine the exact values.
  • Group velocity v_g = c √[1 – (f_c/f)²] and phase velocity v_p = c / √[1 – (f_c/f)²] satisfy v_g·v_p = c², a relationship often tested in exams.
  • Attenuation arises from finite conductivity of the walls; the attenuation constant α ≈ (R_s / (2Z₀)) (1 / a) (1 + (b/a)²) for TE₁₀, where R_s is the surface resistance and Z₀ the wave impedance.

Practical examples:

  1. In a WR‑90 waveguide (a = 22.86 mm, b = 10.16 mm) the TE₁₀ cut‑off is f_c ≈ 6.56 GHz. The X‑band (8–12 GHz) operates comfortably above this, while TE₂₀ (f_c ≈ 13.1 GHz) remains suppressed.
  2. Particle accelerators use cylindrical waveguides operating in TM₀₁ mode because the axial electric field provides acceleration. The cut‑off condition dictates the cavity dimensions for a given beam energy.

Exam‑writers often ask to compare TE and TM modes, calculate cut‑off for a given geometry, or explain why a particular mode is preferred in a device. Remember to state boundary conditions (E_tangential = 0 on conducting walls) before deriving mode equations.

Electromagnetic Momentum, Radiation Pressure and the Abraham–Minkowski Debate

Beyond energy, electromagnetic fields carry momentum. The momentum density g is given by the Poynting vector divided by c²:

g = S / c² = (E × B) / μ₀ c².

Integrating g over a volume yields the total field momentum. When light is absorbed or reflected by a surface, this momentum is transferred, producing radiation pressure.

For a perfectly absorbing surface, the pressure is

P_abs = I / c,

where I is the intensity. For a perfectly reflecting surface the pressure doubles:

P_ref = 2I / c.

These relations are routinely used in solar‑sail calculations and in laser‑trapping (optical tweezers) where forces of order piconewtons are measured.

The Abraham–Minkowski controversy concerns the correct form of electromagnetic momentum inside a dielectric of refractive index n. Two expressions compete:

  • Abraham momentum: p_A = ħk / n = (E / c) / n, implying the momentum density g_A = S / c².
  • Minkowski momentum: p_M = nħk = nE / c, leading to g_M = n² S / c².

Both are derivable from different stress‑tensor formulations. Modern consensus treats them as complementary: Abraham’s form corresponds to the kinetic momentum transferred to the medium, while Minkowski’s represents the canonical momentum associated with the field. Experiments with light in moving media (e.g., the Jones–Richards experiment) have measured forces consistent with the Abraham prediction, whereas photon‑drag experiments align with Minkowski.

Key exam points:

  1. State the expression for radiation pressure and indicate the factor of 2 for reflection.
  2. Explain the physical meaning of the two momenta and why the debate persists.
  3. Apply the appropriate form when a problem involves light entering a dielectric slab: the change in momentum flux equals (n−1)I/c for normal incidence.

Typical trap: students write “radiation pressure = I/c” for all cases, forgetting the factor of 2 for mirrors. Also, they sometimes mix up the symbols p_A and p_M, leading to sign errors in momentum‑balance problems.

Electromagnetic Compatibility (EMC), Shielding Effectiveness and the Role of Grounding

In modern electronic systems, unwanted coupling between circuits can degrade performance or cause malfunction. Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) addresses both emission (preventing a device from radiating excessive interference) and susceptibility (ensuring it tolerates external fields).

Shielding effectiveness (SE) quantifies how well a material attenuates incident fields. In decibels,

SE (dB) = 20 log₁₀ (E_incident / E_transmitted) = R + A + B,

where R is reflection loss, A absorption loss, and B multiple‑reflection correction (often negligible for thick shields). For a planar conductor of thickness t, conductivity σ and permeability μ, the absorption loss is

A = 8.68 t / δ,

with δ the skin depth at the frequency of interest. High‑conductivity metals (copper, aluminium) provide large A at RF, while high‑μ materials (mu‑metal) are effective for low-frequency magnetic field shielding.

Grounding is the complementary strategy that provides a low‑impedance return path for unwanted currents. A good ground network exhibits:

  • Low inductance (short, wide conductors, star‑ground topology).
  • Uniform potential (avoid ground loops that can pick up 50/60 Hz hum).
  • Separation of analog and digital grounds where noise isolation is critical.

Practical design steps:

  1. Identify emission sources (switching transients, clock edges) and route high‑frequency return currents close to the forward path (e.g., using differential pairs).
  2. Enclose sensitive circuitry in a Faraday cage; ensure seams are overlapped and conductive gaskets maintain continuity.
  3. Apply filtering at power‑entry points: common‑mode chokes and π‑filters attenuate conducted emissions.
  4. Perform a pre‑compliance test with a spectrum analyzer and near‑field probe; adjust shielding thickness or add absorptive material (e.g., ferrite tiles) as needed.

Exam‑oriented questions often ask to calculate SE for a given shield at a specified frequency, or to explain why a particular grounding scheme reduces EMI. Remember to convert thickness to skin depths before applying the absorption formula, and to mention both reflection and absorption contributions.

  • Confusing the Abraham and Minkowski momenta; always label which form you are using.
  • Omitting the factor of 2 in radiation‑pressure calculations for reflecting surfaces.
  • Treating skin depth as a constant; always relate it to frequency, μ and σ.
  • Neglecting the proximity effect when evaluating AC resistance of bundled conductors.
  • Using the cut‑off formula for waveguides without specifying mode indices (m,n).
  • Assuming shielding effectiveness equals only absorption loss; include reflection loss for thin or high‑conductivity shields.
  • For grounding, forgetting to mention the danger of ground loops and their effect on low‑frequency noise.

Relativistic Electrodynamics and Field Transformations

When charges move at speeds comparable to c, the electric and magnetic fields measured in one inertial frame mix under a Lorentz transformation. The electromagnetic field tensor Fμν encapsulates both fields: F0i = Ei/c and Fij = -εijkBk. A boost with velocity v along the x‑axis yields the transformation rules E′∥ = E∥, B′∥ = B∥, E′⊥ = γ(E⊥ + v × B⊥), B′⊥ = γ(B⊥ - v × E⊥/c²), where γ = 1/√(1 - v²/c²). These relations show that a pure electric field in one frame appears as a combination of electric and magnetic fields in another, and vice‑versa. The invariants E² - c²B² and E·B remain unchanged, providing a quick check for the existence of a frame where either field vanishes. The Lorentz force law dpμ/dτ = q Fμν uν (with proper time τ and four‑velocity uμ) is manifestly covariant. In practical problems, transforming to the rest frame of a moving charge simplifies the calculation of radiation fields: the Larmor formula P = (q² a²)/(6π ε₀ c³) holds in the instantaneous rest frame, and the radiated power in the lab frame follows from the invariance of the emitted four‑momentum. Examiners often test the ability to identify the correct invariant, to compute the field of a uniformly moving charge (the Liénard‑Wiechert fields reduce to a contracted Coulomb field), and to apply the transformation of the Poynting vector S = (1/μ₀) E × B which mixes energy density and momentum density under boosts.

Multipole Radiation and Antenna Fundamentals

Far‑field radiation from an arbitrary localized source is systematically expanded in electric and magnetic multipoles. The vector potential in the Lorenz gauge is A(r, t) = μ₀ ∫ J(r′, tr) / (4π |r - r′|) d³r′ with retarded time tr = t - |r - r′|/c. Expanding 1/|r - r′| in spherical harmonics yields the electric dipole term p(t) = ∫ r′ ρ(r′, t) d³r′, magnetic dipole m(t) = ½ ∫ r′ × J(r′, t) d³r′, and electric quadrupole Qij = ∫ (3xixj - r² δij) ρ d³r′. The radiated power per solid angle for an oscillating electric dipole p(t) = p₀ cos ωt is dP/dΩ = (μ₀ p₀² ω⁴ / 32π² c) sin²θ, giving the familiar sin²θ pattern and total power P = μ₀ p₀² ω⁴ / (12π c). Magnetic dipole radiation has the same angular dependence but is suppressed by (v/c)² relative to the electric dipole. Higher multipoles contribute at order (ka)2ℓ where k = ω/c and a is the source size. In antenna theory, a thin linear antenna of length L carrying a sinusoidal current I(z) = I₀ sin[k(L/2 - |z|)] produces a far field Eθ ≈ j η I₀ e-jkr / (2π r) [cos(kL cosθ/2) - cos(kL/2)] / sinθ. The half‑wave dipole (L = λ/2) yields a directivity of 1.64 (2.15 dBi) and a radiation resistance of 73 Ω. Array factor multiplication allows beam steering: for N identical elements spaced by d with progressive phase shift β, the array factor is AF(θ) = Σn=0N-1 ej n (kd cosθ + β). Grating lobes appear when d > λ/2. Exam questions frequently ask for the radiation resistance of a short dipole (Rrad = 80π² (L/λ)² Ω), the condition for end‑fire versus broadside arrays, and the relationship between effective aperture Ae = G λ² / (4π) and gain G.

Worked Example: Radiation Resistance of a Short Dipole
A centre‑fed dipole of length L = 0.05 λ carries a triangular current distribution I(z) = I₀ (1 - 2|z|/L). Compute its radiation resistance.
The far‑field of a short dipole with arbitrary current is Eθ = j η k e-jkr / (4π r) sinθ ∫-L/2L/2 I(z) ejkz cosθ dz. For L ≪ λ, expand the exponential to first order: ∫ I(z) dz = I₀ L/2 (area of triangle). The radiated power is P = (η k² / 32π²) |∫ I(z) dz|² ∫ sin³θ dΩ = (η k² / 32π²) (I₀ L/2)² (8π/3). Using η = √(μ₀/ε₀) ≈ 377 Ω and k = 2π/λ gives P = (I₀² / 2) × 80π² (L/λ)². Hence Rrad = 2P / I₀² = 80π² (L/λ)² Ω. For L = 0.05 λ, Rrad ≈ 80π² × 0.0025 ≈ 1.97 Ω.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the scaling laws: electric dipole ∝ ω⁴, magnetic dipole ∝ ω⁴ (but weaker by (v/c)²), electric quadrupole ∝ ω⁶. For array problems, write the array factor first; the element pattern multiplies it. Always check whether the question asks for directivity (dimensionless) or gain (includes efficiency). Convert dBi to linear scale via G = 10dBi/10 before using Ae = G λ²/(4π).

Superconductivity, the Meissner Effect and London Equations

Below the critical temperature Tc a superconductor exhibits zero dc resistivity and expels magnetic flux — the Meissner effect. The phenomenological London equations describe the electrodynamics: ∂Js/∂t = (ns e² / m) E (first London equation) and ∇ × Js = - (ns e² / m) B (second London equation), where ns is the density of superconducting electrons. Combining the second equation with Maxwell‑Ampère ∇ × B = μ₀ Js yields ∇² B = B / λL² with the London penetration depth λL = √(m / μ₀ ns e²). Magnetic fields decay exponentially inside the superconductor over λL (typically 50–500 nm). The first equation implies a dissipationless current response: for a static E field, Js grows linearly, but in practice the superconductor screens E by developing surface charges. The thermodynamic critical field Hc satisfies ½ μ₀ Hc² = Fn - Fs, the free‑energy difference between normal and superconducting states. Type‑I superconductors exhibit a complete Meissner state up to Hc then a first‑order transition to the normal state. Type‑II materials have two critical fields Hc1 < Hc2; between them magnetic flux penetrates as quantised vortices each carrying a flux quantum Φ₀ = h/2e ≈ 2.07×10⁻¹⁵ Wb. The Ginzburg‑Landau parameter κ = λL/ξ (ξ = coherence length) determines the type: κ < 1/√2 → Type‑I, κ > 1/√2 → Type‑II. The critical current density Jc is limited by depairing (Jd ≈ c Hc / λL) and by flux‑flow resistivity when vortices move. In applications, the surface resistance Rs = μ₀² ω² λL³ σn / 2 (σn normal‑state conductivity) governs microwave losses in superconducting cavities. Exam problems often require deriving λL from the London equations, calculating the magnetic field profile B(x) = B₀ e-x/λL, or estimating the maximum trapped flux in a Type‑II cylinder using Bean’s critical‑state model.

Metamaterials, Negative Refractive Index and Cloaking

Engineered structures with sub‑wavelength resonant inclusions can exhibit effective permittivity εeff(ω) and permeability μeff(ω) that become simultaneously negative over a bandwidth, yielding a negative refractive index n = -√(εeff μeff). The classic split‑ring resonator (SRR) provides a magnetic resonance described by a Lorentzian μeff(ω) = 1 - F ω² / (ω² - ω₀² + iΓω), while a wire array gives a Drude‑like εeff(ω) = 1 - ωp² / (ω² + iγω). Overlap of the two resonances produces a passband where both real parts are negative. In this regime the wavevector k = n ω/c points opposite to the Poynting vector S, leading to backward waves, reversed Doppler shift, and reversed Cherenkov radiation. Snell’s law still holds with n₂ negative, so a ray bends to the same side of the normal as the incident ray. A planar slab of negative‑index material of thickness d acts as a lens: for n = -1 it focuses a point source at distance d on the far side (Pendry’s perfect lens), restoring evanescent components and theoretically achieving sub‑diffraction imaging. Transformation optics provides a systematic route to cloaking: a coordinate transformation x′ = f(x) maps Maxwell’s equations onto an equivalent anisotropic, inhomogeneous medium with ε′ = μ′ = J JT / det J, where J is the Jacobian. A cylindrical cloak compresses the interior region r < R₁ into a shell R₁ < r < R₂, yielding radial and azimuthal parameters εr = μr = (r - R₁)/r, εθ = μθ = r/(r - R₁). Practical cloaks operate at a single frequency with lossy, dispersive metamaterials; bandwidth is limited by causality (Kramers‑Kronig). Exam questions test the sign of the wave impedance η = √(μ/ε) (remains positive for passive media), the condition for impedance matching to free space (ε = μ = -1), and the physical meaning of the group velocity vg = dω/dk being opposite to phase velocity.

Electromagnetic Scattering: Mie Theory and Radar Cross Section

Exact scattering of a plane wave by a homogeneous sphere of radius a and complex refractive index m = n - iκ is solved by expanding the incident and scattered fields in vector spherical harmonics. The Mie coefficients for electric (an) and magnetic (bn) multipoles are an = [m ψn(mx) ψn′(x) - ψn(x) ψn′(mx)] / [m ψn(mx) ξn′(x) - ξn(x) ψn′(mx)], bn = [ψn(mx) ψn′(x) - m ψn(x) ψn′(mx)] / [ψn(mx) ξn′(x) - m ξn(x) ψn′(mx)], where x = ka = 2πa/λ is the size parameter, ψn and ξn are Riccati‑Bessel functions. The far‑field scattering amplitude functions S₁(θ), S₂(θ) give the differential scattering cross section dσ/dΩ = (|S₁|² + |S₂|²)/2k². The total scattering cross section σsca = (2π/k²) Σ (2n+1)(|an|² + |bn|²). The absorption cross section σabs = (2π/k²) Σ (2n+1) Re(an + bn - |an|² - |bn|²). The extinction cross section σext = σsca + σabs obeys the optical theorem σext = (4π/k) Im[S(0)]. For radar, the monostatic radar cross section (RCS) is σ = limr→∞ 4π r² |Esca|² / |Einc|². In the Rayleigh limit (x ≪ 1) only the dipole terms survive: a₁ ≈ i 2/3 (m² - 1)/(m² + 2) x³, b₁ ≈ i 1/3 (m² - 1) x³, giving σsca ∝ x⁴ (Rayleigh scattering). In the geometric optics limit (x ≫ 1) the RCS approaches the physical cross section πa² with supernumerary ripples from creeping waves. Resonances (Mie resonances) occur when the denominator of an or bn nearly vanishes, leading to sharp peaks in σsca used in dielectric resonator antennas. Exam problems often ask to compute the first electric dipole resonance condition for a dielectric sphere (m x ≈ π), to identify the scaling of RCS with frequency in the Rayleigh regime, or to apply the optical theorem to relate forward scattering amplitude to total extinction.

  • Using the London penetration depth formula without checking whether the material is in the clean or dirty limit (mean free path vs coherence length).
  • Applying the dipole radiation formula to a source whose size is not ≪ λ; higher multipoles then dominate.
  • Neglecting the factor of 2 in the radiation pressure for a perfectly reflecting surface when the problem explicitly states a mirror.
  • Treating the Mie coefficients as real numbers; they are complex and their phases determine interference in the scattering pattern.
  • Assuming the array factor alone gives the total pattern; the element pattern must be multiplied, especially for scanned arrays where element gain drops.
  • Forgetting that the optical theorem relates the forward scattering amplitude to the total extinction, not just scattering.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Anisotropic Crystals

In anisotropic media the constitutive relations become tensors: D = ε·E and B = μ·H, where ε and μ are symmetric second‑rank tensors. For a non‑magnetic crystal μ = μ₀I, so the wave equation reduces to ∇×(∇×E) = −μ₀∂²/∂t² (ε·E). Assuming a plane wave E = E₀ ei(k·r−ωt) leads to the Fresnel equation (k·ε⁻¹·k) = (ω/c)². The dispersion surface in k‑space is an ellipsoid whose principal axes are aligned with the dielectric tensor’s principal axes. Two orthogonal polarisation eigenmodes (ordinary and extraordinary) propagate with different phase velocities; their wavevectors satisfy k_o = n_o ω/c and k_e(θ) = n_e(θ) ω/c where n_e(θ) = (n_o n_e)/√(n_o² sin²θ + n_e² cos²θ). The Poynting vector S = E×H is not parallel to k for the extraordinary wave; the walk‑off angle ρ is given by tan ρ = (1/2 n_e²) d(n_e²)/dθ. Birefringence Δn = n_e − n_o determines the phase retardation Γ = (2π/λ) Δn L for a slab of thickness L. In uniaxial crystals (e.g., calcite, quartz) the optic axis is the direction where n_o = n_e; in biaxial crystals (e.g., mica) there are two optic axes and the index ellipsoid has three distinct principal indices n_x, n_y, n_z. Applications include polarisers (Glan‑Thompson, Nicol), wave plates (quarter‑wave, half‑wave), and electro‑optic modulators where an applied field changes ε via the Pockels tensor rijk. The phase matching condition for nonlinear processes (e.g., second harmonic generation) requires k(2ω) = 2k(ω), which in anisotropic media is achieved by angle tuning or temperature tuning of the refractive indices.

Nonlinear Optical Phenomena: Second Harmonic Generation and Kerr Effect

When the electric field amplitude is comparable to the interatomic field (≈10¹¹ V/m), the polarisation expands as P_i = ε₀(χ(1)ij E_j + χ(2)ijk E_j E_k + χ(3)ijkl E_j E_k E_l + …). The second‑order susceptibility χ(2) vanishes in centrosymmetric media; thus second harmonic generation (SHG) occurs only in non‑centrosymmetric crystals (e.g., KDP, BBO, LiNbO₃). The coupled‑wave equations for fundamental (ω) and second harmonic (2ω) under the slowly varying envelope approximation are dA_ω/dz = i κ A2ω A_ω* e-iΔk z and dA2ω/dz = i κ A_ω² eiΔk z, where κ = ω χ(2)/(n_ω n2ω c) and Δk = k2ω − 2k_ω. Perfect phase matching (Δk = 0) yields intensity I2ω ∝ I_ω² L²; with mismatch the conversion oscillates with coherence length L_c = π/|Δk|. Quasi‑phase matching (QPM) uses periodic poling of χ(2) (period Λ = 2π/Δk) to reset the phase every half‑period, giving effective κ_eff = (2/π) κ. The third‑order susceptibility χ(3) produces the optical Kerr effect: n = n₀ + n₂ I, where n₂ = 3 χ(3)/(4 n₀² ε₀ c). Self‑phase modulation broadens the spectrum of an intense pulse; self‑focusing occurs when the peak power exceeds the critical power P_cr = (3.77 λ²)/(8π n₀ n₂). In fibres, the nonlinear Schrödinger equation i ∂A/∂z − (β₂/2) ∂²A/∂t² + γ|A|² A = 0 governs soliton formation when anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0) balances Kerr nonlinearity (γ = n₂ ω₀/(c A_eff)). Four‑wave mixing (FWM) generates sidebands at ω_s = 2ω_p − ω_i, enabling wavelength conversion and parametric amplification. Raman gain and Brillouin scattering are additional χ(3) processes that limit high‑power transmission.

Worked Example: Phase‑Matching Angle in a Uniaxial Crystal
A KDP crystal (n_o(ω)=1.507, n_e(ω)=1.467; n_o(2ω)=1.556, n_e(2ω)=1.512) is used for type‑I SHG (oo→e). Find the internal angle θ between the propagation direction and the optic axis that satisfies Δk=0.
For type‑I, the fundamental waves are ordinary (n_o(ω)) and the second harmonic is extraordinary (n_e(2ω,θ)). Phase matching requires n_e(2ω,θ) = n_o(ω). The extraordinary index varies as 1/n_e²(θ) = cos²θ/n_e² + sin²θ/n_o². Insert n_e(2ω)=1.512, n_o(2ω)=1.556, and set n_e(θ)=1.507. Solve: 1/1.507² = cos²θ/1.512² + sin²θ/1.556². Compute reciprocals: 0.4395 = 0.4370 cos²θ + 0.4135 sin²θ. Rearranged: 0.4395 = 0.4135 + (0.4370−0.4135) cos²θ → 0.0260 = 0.0235 cos²θ → cos²θ = 1.106 → no real solution. Therefore type‑I oo→e is not phase‑matchable at these wavelengths; one must use type‑II (oe→e) or temperature tuning. The calculation illustrates that phase matching is not guaranteed for every geometry.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem gives principal refractive indices at two wavelengths, first identify the interaction type (I, II, or III). Write the phase‑matching condition explicitly, then substitute the extraordinary index formula. If the algebra yields cos²θ > 1 or < 0, state that phase matching is impossible for that geometry and suggest the alternative (angle tuning, temperature tuning, or QPM). This logical step may earn partial credit even if the final angle is not computable.

Quantum Electrodynamics: Vacuum Fluctuations and the Casimir Effect

Quantum electrodynamics (QED) quantises the electromagnetic field in terms of creation and annihilation operators for each mode k,λ. The zero‑point energy of a mode is ½ ħ ω_k, leading to an infinite vacuum energy density. Physical effects arise from changes in the mode structure imposed by boundaries. For two perfectly conducting parallel plates separated by distance a, the allowed wavevectors perpendicular to the plates are k_z = nπ/a (n = 1,2,…). Summing ½ ħ ω over all modes and subtracting the free‑space contribution yields the Casimir energy per unit area E(a) = −(π² ħ c)/(720 a³). The resulting attractive pressure is P = −∂E/∂a = −(π² ħ c)/(240 a⁴). For a = 1 µm, P ≈ 1.3 mPa. Real metals have finite conductivity and surface roughness; the Lifshitz theory replaces perfect‑conductor boundary conditions with frequency‑dependent dielectric functions ε(iξ), giving a correction factor η(a) that reduces the force at sub‑micron separations. The Casimir effect has been measured with torsion balances and atomic force microscopes, confirming QED predictions to within a few percent. Related phenomena include the dynamical Casimir effect (photon generation from moving mirrors), the Casimir‑Polder force between a neutral atom and a surface (∝ a⁻⁴ at short range, ∝ a⁻⁵ at long range), and the influence of vacuum fluctuations on spontaneous emission rates (Purcell effect). In cavity QED, the modified density of states enhances or inhibits emission, described by the Purcell factor F_P = (3/4π²)(λ³/Q V_mode). These concepts underpin modern quantum technologies such as circuit QED, where superconducting qubits couple to microwave resonators.

Electromagnetic Pulse (EMP) Generation, Coupling, and Protection

A nuclear detonation at high altitude produces a high‑altitude EMP (HEMP) with three components: E1 (prompt gamma‑ray Compton scattering, rise time < 1 ns, field up to 50 kV/m), E2 (scattered gamma and neutron interactions, similar to lightning, 1 µs–1 s), and E3 (magnetohydrodynamic distortion of the Earth’s magnetic field, 1–1000 s). Non‑nuclear EMP (NNEMP) weapons use flux‑compression generators or vircators to produce fields of 10–100 kV/m over limited areas. Coupling to systems occurs via front‑door (antennas, apertures) and back‑door (cable penetrations, seams) paths. The induced voltage on a straight conductor of length L in a uniform field E is V_ind = ∫ E·dl ≈ E L for L ≪ λ. For resonant structures (e.g., a half‑wave dipole at frequency f), the effective length is L_eff = λ/π, giving voltage gain. Shielding effectiveness (SE) of an enclosure is SE = R + A + B (reflection, absorption, multiple reflections) in dB. For a plane wave incident on a conductive sheet of thickness t, conductivity σ, permeability μ, the absorption loss A = 8.686 t/δ where δ = √(2/ωμσ) is the skin depth. Aperture leakage follows Bethe’s small‑hole theory: SE_aperture ≈ 20 log₁₀(λ/2d) for a circular hole of diameter d ≪ λ. Protection measures include Faraday cages with conductive gaskets, filtered penetrations (feed‑through capacitors, ferrite beads), surge arresters (gas‑discharge tubes, MOVs) on power lines, and fibre‑optic links to break conductive paths. System‑level hardening uses the “protect‑detect‑react” paradigm: shielding, transient voltage suppression, and watchdog timers that reset logic after an upset. Standards such as MIL‑STD‑461 and IEC 61000‑4‑24 define test waveforms (double‑exponential 1.2/50 µs voltage, 8/20 µs current) and performance criteria.

Magnetohydrodynamic Waves in Conducting Fluids

Magnetohydrodynamics (MHD) treats an electrically conducting fluid (plasma, liquid metal) as a single continuum coupled to the magnetic field. The governing equations are the Navier‑Stokes equation with Lorentz force J×B, Ohm’s law for a moving conductor J = σ(E + v×B), Maxwell’s equations (neglecting displacement current for low frequencies), and the induction equation ∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) + η∇²B where η = 1/μ₀σ is the magnetic diffusivity. Linearising about a uniform equilibrium (B₀, ρ₀, p₀) yields three wave modes: (1) Alfvén wave – incompressible, transverse, propagates parallel to B₀ with phase velocity v_A = B₀/√(μ₀ρ₀); group velocity equals phase velocity, no density perturbation. (2) Fast magnetosonic wave – compressive, propagates at all angles, phase velocity v_f² = ½(c_s² + v_A² + √((c_s² + v_A²)² − 4c_s² v_A² cos²θ)), where c_s = √(γp₀/ρ₀) is the sound speed and θ is the angle between k and B₀. (3) Slow magnetosonic wave – compressive, phase velocity v_s² = ½(c_s² + v_A² − √(...)). In the limit c_s ≪ v_A (low‑β plasma) the fast wave becomes a compressional Alfvén wave (v_f ≈ v_A) and the slow wave becomes an acoustic wave guided by field lines (v_s ≈ c_s cosθ). In the opposite limit v_A ≪ c_s (high‑β) the fast wave is essentially a sound wave and the slow wave is a shear Alfvén wave with v_s ≈ v_A cosθ. Resistive dissipation introduces a damping rate γ ∼ η k². MHD waves are central to solar coronal heating, magnetospheric dynamics, and fusion confinement (e.g., sawtooth crashes in tokamaks are triggered by internal kink modes, an MHD instability). The dimensionless Lundquist number S = μ₀ L v_A/η determines whether the plasma behaves ideally (S ≫ 1) or resistively (S ∼ 1).

  • Confusing phase‑matching angle for type‑I and type‑II processes; the extraordinary index formula differs because the polarisation of the second harmonic changes.
  • Using the perfect‑conductor Casimir formula for real metals at sub‑micron gaps without applying the Lifshitz correction.
  • Treating EMP coupling as only front‑door; back‑door coupling through cable shields often dominates system upset.
  • Neglecting the skin‑depth dependence of shielding effectiveness at high frequencies; SE drops 20 dB per decade once t ≫ δ.
  • Mixing up fast and slow magnetosonic wave polarisation; the fast wave has magnetic pressure and gas pressure in phase, the slow wave has them out of phase.
  • Forgetting that the Alfvén wave carries no density fluctuation, so it does not appear in compressive diagnostics like interferometry.

Electromagnetic Radiation from Accelerated Charges: Larmor Formula, Synchrotron Radiation, and Bremsstrahlung

An accelerating charge radiates electromagnetic energy. The non‑relativistic Larmor formula gives the total radiated power P = q² a² / (6π ε₀ c³), where q is the charge, a the acceleration magnitude, ε₀ the vacuum permittivity and c the speed of light. This follows from the far‑field terms of the Liénard‑Wiechert potentials and is valid for v ≪ c. For relativistic motion the expression generalises to the Liénard formula P = (q² γ⁶ / 6π ε₀ c³) [ a² − (v × a)² / c² ], with γ = (1 − v²/c²)⁻¹ᐟ². The angular distribution peaks sharply forward, scaling as γ⁴ for acceleration parallel to velocity and γ⁶ for perpendicular acceleration.

Synchrotron radiation arises when a relativistic electron moves in a magnetic field B with velocity perpendicular to B. The centripetal acceleration is a = v² / ρ = q v B / (γ m), where ρ is the orbit radius. The radiated power is P = q⁴ B² γ² / (6π ε₀ m² c) (for v ≈ c). The spectrum extends to a critical frequency ω_c = (3/2) γ³ c / ρ, and the spectral power per unit frequency follows a universal function S(ω/ω_c) involving modified Bessel functions. Synchrotron radiation is highly polarised (linear in the orbital plane, elliptical off‑plane) and underpins modern light sources.

Bremsstrahlung (braking radiation) occurs when a fast charge decelerates in the Coulomb field of an atomic nucleus. In the Born approximation the differential cross‑section for photon energy ħω at angle θ is dσ/dω ∝ (Z² α r_e² / ω) [ (1 + (1 − ω/E)²) − (2/3)(1 − ω/E) sin²θ ], where Z is the nuclear charge, α the fine‑structure constant, r_e the classical electron radius, and E the incident electron energy. The total radiated energy fraction scales as Z² E. In thick targets multiple scattering modifies the angular distribution; the resulting continuous X‑ray spectrum is used in medical imaging and industrial inspection.

Key examiner points: the Larmor formula is strictly non‑relativistic; the Liénard formula must be used for particle‑accelerator energies. Synchrotron critical energy scales as γ³ B, not γ² B. Bremsstrahlung yield rises linearly with target thickness only while electron energy loss per radiation length remains small; beyond one radiation length the spectrum saturates.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Cold Plasma: Dispersion Relations, Cutoffs, and Resonances

A cold, collisionless plasma is described by the dielectric tensor derived from the linearised equation of motion m d v/dt = q (E + v × B₀) for each species about a uniform field B₀ = B₀ ẑ. The relative permittivity components are ε_xx = ε_yy = 1 − Σ_s ωps² / (ω² − ωcs²), ε_xy = −ε_yx = i Σ_s (ωcs/ω) ωps² / (ω² − ωcs²), and ε_zz = 1 − Σ_s ωps² / ω², where ωps = √(n_s q_s² / ε₀ m_s) is the plasma frequency and ωcs = q_s B₀ / m_s the cyclotron frequency of species s.

For propagation parallel to B₀ (k ∥ B₀) the tensor decouples into right‑hand (R) and left‑hand (L) circularly polarised modes with refractive indices n_R² = 1 − Σ_s ωps² / [ω (ω − ωcs)] and n_L² = 1 − Σ_s ωps² / [ω (ω + ωcs)]. The R‑mode has a resonance at ω = ωce (electron cyclotron resonance) and a cutoff at ω_R satisfying n_R = 0. The L‑mode resonates at the ion cyclotron frequency ωci and cuts off at ω_L. For perpendicular propagation (k ⟂ B₀) the ordinary (O) mode sees n_O² = ε_zz and the extraordinary (X) mode sees n_X² = (ε_xx ε_zz − ε_xy²) / ε_zz. The X‑mode exhibits the upper hybrid resonance ωUH² = ωpe² + ωce² and the lower hybrid resonance ωLH² ≈ ωpi² + ωci² (for ωci ≪ ω ≪ ωce).

Cutoffs occur where n² = 0; waves become evanescent beyond them. Resonances occur where n² → ∞; the wave electric field grows and energy is absorbed (collisionless damping). In the ionosphere the O‑mode cutoff at ω = ωpe sets the maximum usable frequency for sky‑wave communication. The X‑mode cutoff at ω_R and the lower hybrid resonance are exploited in plasma heating for magnetic confinement fusion.

Examiner traps: confusing R‑ and L‑mode cutoffs (R‑mode cutoff lies above ωpe, L‑mode below); forgetting the ordinary mode is unaffected by B₀ (polarisation parallel to B₀) while the extraordinary mode couples to both E components; misidentifying the upper hybrid resonance as the electron cyclotron resonance (they coincide only when ωpe = 0).

Worked Example: Cutoff Frequencies in a Magnetised Plasma
A cold electron‑proton plasma has electron density n_e = 10¹⁸ m⁻³ and magnetic field B₀ = 0.1 T. Compute the R‑mode and L‑mode cutoff frequencies for parallel propagation. Use e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C, m_e = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, m_p = 1.67×10⁻²⁷ kg, ε₀ = 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m.
First calculate plasma and cyclotron frequencies. ω_pe = √(n_e e² / ε₀ m_e) ≈ 5.64×10¹⁰ rad/s (f_pe ≈ 8.98 GHz). ω_ce = e B₀ / m_e ≈ 1.76×10¹⁰ rad/s (f_ce ≈ 2.80 GHz). ω_pi = √(n_e e² / ε₀ m_p) ≈ 1.32×10⁹ rad/s. ω_ci = e B₀ / m_p ≈ 9.58×10⁶ rad/s. For parallel propagation the R‑mode cutoff satisfies ω (ω − ω_ce) = ω_pe². Solve ω² − ω_ce ω − ω_pe² = 0 → ω_R = (ω_ce + √(ω_ce² + 4 ω_pe²))/2 ≈ 6.53×10¹⁰ rad/s (f_R ≈ 10.4 GHz). The L‑mode cutoff satisfies ω (ω + ω_ce) = ω_pe² → ω² + ω_ce ω − ω_pe² = 0 → ω_L = (−ω_ce + √(ω_ce² + 4 ω_pe²))/2 ≈ 4.77×10¹⁰ rad/s (f_L ≈ 7.6 GHz). Thus the R‑mode cutoff exceeds the electron plasma frequency, while the L‑mode cutoff lies below it.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the three key frequencies per species: plasma frequency ω_p, cyclotron frequency ω_c, and hybrid resonances ω_UH and ω_LH. For a cutoff, set n² = 0 and solve the quadratic; for a resonance, set the relevant refractive‑index denominator to zero. Sketch the CMA diagram (ω vs k) for parallel and perpendicular propagation — a frequent diagram‑based question.

Electromagnetic Energy in Dispersive and Lossy Media: Generalized Poynting Theorem and Group Velocity

In a linear, isotropic medium with frequency‑dependent complex permittivity ε(ω) = ε′(ω) + i ε″(ω) and permeability μ(ω) = μ′(ω) + i μ″(ω), the time‑averaged Poynting vector for a monochromatic field E(r,t) = Re{E₀ ei(k·r − ωt)} is ⟨S⟩ = ½ Re{E₀ × H₀*}. The electromagnetic energy density is not simply ½ (ε′|E|² + μ′|H|²) when dispersion is present. The Brillouin formula gives the correct time‑averaged energy density for a narrow‑band packet centred at ω₀: ⟨u⟩ = ¼ [ d(ω ε′)/dω |E₀|² + d(ω μ′)/dω |H₀|² ] at ω₀. This reduces to ½ ε′|E|² + ½ μ′|H|² only when ε′ and μ′ are frequency‑independent.

The group velocity v_g = dω/dk governs energy and information transport. For a plane wave the dispersion relation is k² = ω² μ(ω) ε(ω). Differentiating yields v_g = c / n_g where the group index n_g = n + ω (dn/dω) and n = √(μ ε) is the phase index. In anomalous dispersion (dn/dω < 0) the group velocity can exceed c or become negative, but the signal (front) velocity remains ≤ c because high‑frequency components defining the wavefront see ε → ε₀, μ → μ₀.

Losses enter via ε″, μ″. The time‑averaged power dissipated per unit volume is ⟨p_d⟩ = ½ ω (ε″ |E₀|² + μ″ |H₀|²). The generalized Poynting theorem in the frequency domain reads ∇·⟨S⟩ + ⟨p_d⟩ + i ω (⟨u_e⟩ − ⟨u_m⟩) = 0, with ⟨u_e⟩ = ¼ d(ω ε′)/dω |E₀|² and ⟨u_m⟩ = ¼ d(ω μ′)/dω |H₀|². The reactive term i ω (⟨u_e⟩ − ⟨u_m⟩) represents energy oscillating between electric and magnetic forms; it vanishes in a lossless, non‑dispersive medium where ⟨u_e⟩ = ⟨u_m⟩.

Practical consequences: in optical fibres the group‑velocity dispersion parameter β₂ = d²k/dω² determines pulse broadening; in metamaterials with negative ε′ and μ′ the group velocity opposes the phase velocity, producing backward waves. Examiner focus: distinguish phase velocity v_p = ω/k, group velocity v_g = dω/dk, and energy velocity v_e = ⟨S⟩/⟨u⟩; they coincide only in lossless, non‑dispersive media.

Electromagnetic Induction Heating: Eddy Current Distribution, Skin Effect in Conductors, and Power Loss Calculations

When a time‑varying magnetic field penetrates a conducting workpiece, eddy currents are induced via Faraday’s law ∇ × E = −∂B/∂t. For a semi‑infinite slab of conductivity σ and permeability μ subjected to a uniform tangential magnetic field H₀ eiωt at its surface, the field decays as H(z) = H₀ e−(1+i)z/δ where the skin depth δ = √(2 / ω μ σ). The induced current density is J(z) = (1+i) H₀/δ e−(1+i)z/δ. The time‑averaged power dissipated per unit surface area is P/A = ½ |H₀|² √(ω μ / 2σ) = ½ |H₀|² R_s, with surface resistance R_s = 1/(σ δ).

For a cylindrical billet of radius a inside a solenoidal coil carrying I(t) = I₀ cos ωt, the magnetic field satisfies the diffusion equation ∂B/∂t = (1/μσ) ∇² B. In the quasi‑static limit (δ ≪ a) the field is confined to a surface layer and the total loss is P = (π a l) (|H₀|² / σ δ), where l is the length. When δ is comparable to a, Bessel‑function solutions are required: H(r) = H₀ I₀( (1+i) r/δ ) / I₀( (1+i) a/δ ), with I₀ the modified Bessel function of the first kind. The power loss becomes P = (π a l) (|H₀|² / σ δ) Re[ I₁( (1+i) a/δ ) / I₀( (1+i) a/δ ) ].

In induction heating of ferromagnetic materials the permeability μ is strongly temperature‑dependent and drops sharply at the Curie temperature T_C, abruptly increasing the skin depth and altering the heating profile. Power‑control strategies therefore monitor coil impedance (reflecting effective μ) to maintain constant power. For non‑magnetic metals (copper, aluminium) the skin depth at 10 kHz is ∼0.5 mm; at 1 MHz it falls to ∼0.05 mm, making high‑frequency heating suitable for surface hardening.

Examiner traps: using the plane‑wave skin‑depth formula for a thick cylinder without Bessel‑function correction when a/δ < 3; forgetting that surface resistance R_s scales as √ω, not linearly with ω; confusing the coil‑surface field with the workpiece‑surface field (the coupling coefficient must be included for accurate power prediction).

  • Applying the simple skin‑depth loss formula P = ½ H₀² R_s to a cylinder whose radius is only a few skin depths without Bessel‑function correction.

Photonic Crystals and Electromagnetic Band Gaps

Photonic crystals are periodic dielectric structures that create allowed and forbidden frequency bands for electromagnetic waves, analogous to electronic band gaps in semiconductors. The periodicity can be one‑dimensional (multilayer stacks), two‑dimensional (arrays of rods or holes), or three‑dimensional (inverse opal, woodpile). The central quantity is the photonic band structure ω(k) obtained by solving Maxwell’s equations with Bloch‑periodic boundary conditions: ∇×E = iωμ₀H, ∇×H = −iωε(r)E, where ε(r) = ε(r+R) for lattice vectors R. For a 1‑D stack of alternating layers with refractive indices n₁, n₂ and thicknesses d₁, d₂, the dispersion relation follows from the transfer‑matrix method: cos(kΛ) = cos(δ₁)cos(δ₂) − ½ (n₁/n₂ + n₂/n₁) sin(δ₁)sin(δ₂), with δᵢ = (ω/c)nᵢdᵢ and Λ = d₁+d₂. A complete photonic band gap typically opens when the index contrast exceeds a threshold (≈2 for 1‑D, ≈2.8 for 2‑D square lattice of rods, ≈2.0 for 2‑D triangular lattice of holes). In 3‑D, the diamond‑like structure yields among the largest complete gaps (Δω/ω₀ ≈ 0.3). The gap centre frequency scales as c/Λ, so micron‑scale periods yield gaps in the near‑infrared. Defects introduced by removing or altering a single layer create localized cavity modes with quality factors Q exceeding 10⁶, enabling ultralow‑threshold lasers and single‑photon sources. Line defects act as waveguides with sharp bends and low loss because the gap suppresses radiation into the bulk. Examiner focus: distinguish between a complete gap (for all polarizations and propagation directions) and a partial gap (only for TE or TM); know that the gap‑midgap ratio Δω/ω₀ is the figure of merit; recall that the group velocity v_g = dω/dk vanishes at the band edge, leading to slow‑light enhancement of nonlinear effects.

Jefimenko's Equations and the Near‑Field/Far‑Field Decomposition of Radiation

Jefimenko’s equations give the electric and magnetic fields directly in terms of the retarded charge and current distributions without invoking potentials: E(r,t) = 1/(4πε₀) ∫ [ρ(r′,t_r)R̂/R² + (∂ρ/∂t)(r′,t_r)R̂/(cR) − (∂J/∂t)(r′,t_r)/(c²R)] d³r′, B(r,t) = μ₀/(4π) ∫ [J(r′,t_r)×R̂/R² + (∂J/∂t)(r′,t_r)×R̂/(cR)] d³r′, where R = r−r′, R̂ = R/R, and t_r = t−R/c. The three terms in each integrand scale as 1/R², 1/R, and 1/R; for a localized source these produce the familiar 1/R³ (static), 1/R² (induction), and 1/R (radiation) field components. In the far zone (R ≫ λ, source size) only the radiation terms survive, yielding the transverse fields E_rad = −μ₀/(4πR) ∂J_⊥/∂t|_ret, B_rad = R̂×E_rad/c. The near zone (R ≪ λ) is dominated by the quasi‑static terms; the induction term bridges the two regimes. For a harmonic dipole p(t)=p₀e−iωt, the exact fields are E = (k²/(4πε₀)) (R̂×p)×R̂ eikR/R + (1/(4πε₀)) [3R̂(R̂·p)−p](1/R³−ik/R²) eikR, B = (μ₀k²/(4π)) (R̂×p) eikR/R + (μ₀/(4π)) (R̂×p)(1/R²−ik/R) eikR. The 1/R³ term is the electrostatic field, the 1/R² term the induction field, and the 1/R term the radiation field. Radiated power is obtained by integrating the far‑zone Poynting vector ⟨S⟩ = (μ₀p₀²ω⁴/(32π²c)) sin²θ R̂, giving the Larmor formula for an oscillating dipole: P = p₀²ω⁴/(12πε₀c³). Examiner traps: using the far‑field approximation for distances comparable to λ; forgetting that the induction term contributes to reactive power but not to net radiated power; confusing the retarded time in the source coordinates with the observation time.

Worked Example: Near‑Field Dominance
A short dipole of length ℓ ≪ λ carries a sinusoidal current I(t)=I₀cos ωt. At a distance r = λ/10 from the dipole, compute the ratio of the magnitudes of the induction electric field (∝1/r²) to the radiation electric field (∝1/r). Assume the dipole moment amplitude p₀ = I₀ℓ/ω.

Cherenkov Radiation and Transition Radiation in Dielectric Media

Cherenkov radiation occurs when a charged particle moves through a dielectric with velocity v exceeding the phase velocity of light in that medium, v > c/n(ω). The emitted radiation forms a cone with opening angle θ_c given by cos θ_c = c/(nv). The spectral energy loss per unit path length is d²E/dx dω = (μ₀q²ω/(4π)) (1 − c²/(n²v²)) for frequencies where n(ω) > c/v. The Frank‑Tamm formula integrates over ω to give the total energy loss per unit length: dE/dx = (μ₀q²/(4π)) ∫v>c/n ω (1 − c²/(n²v²)) dω. In a dispersive medium n(ω), the threshold condition becomes v > c/n(ω) for each frequency component, so the spectrum is limited to a band where the inequality holds. Transition radiation arises when a charged particle crosses an interface between two media with different dielectric constants ε₁, ε₂. The radiation is emitted both forward and backward, with a characteristic angular distribution peaked at θ ∼ 1/γ for relativistic particles (γ = 1/√(1−v²/c²)). The spectral energy per unit frequency for a single interface is dE/dω = (q²/(4πε₀c)) ( (ε₁−ε₂)²/(ε₁ε₂) ) ln(γ) for γ ≫ 1, assuming the formation zone length ℓ_f = γ²c/ω exceeds the foil thickness. For a foil of thickness d, interference between the two interfaces modulates the spectrum, producing oscillations with period Δω ≈ 2πc/(dγ²). Both Cherenkov and transition radiation are used in particle detectors: Cherenkov counters identify particles by measuring θ_c, while transition radiation detectors (TRDs) discriminate electrons from hadrons by exploiting the γ‑dependence of the X‑ray yield. Examiner focus: derive the Cherenkov angle from the Huygens construction; apply the Frank‑Tamm formula with a given n(ω); distinguish the formation zone concept in transition radiation; recognize that Cherenkov radiation is continuous while transition radiation is a boundary effect.

Eddy Current Testing and Nondestructive Evaluation of Conductors

Eddy current testing (ECT) exploits the skin effect and electromagnetic induction to detect surface and subsurface flaws in conductive materials. A probe coil carrying an alternating current I(t)=I₀cos ωt generates a primary magnetic field that induces eddy currents in the test piece. Flaws such as cracks, corrosion, or material property variations perturb the eddy current distribution, altering the coil impedance ΔZ = ΔR + iΔX. The standard depth of penetration (skin depth) is δ = √(2/(ωμσ)). For a surface‑breaking crack of depth a ≪ δ, the impedance change scales approximately as ΔZ/Z₀ ∝ (a/δ) for non‑magnetic conductors, while for ferromagnetic materials the permeability contrast (μ_r−1) enhances the signal. The phase angle of the impedance change distinguishes between conductivity variations (phase near 0°) and magnetic permeability variations (phase near 90°). Multi‑frequency inspection uses several excitation frequencies simultaneously; plotting the impedance trajectory in the complex plane (impedance plane diagram) separates lift‑off effects (vertical shift) from flaw signals (horizontal shift). Pulsed eddy current (PEC) employs a step‑function excitation and measures the transient decay of the induced voltage; the time‑domain response provides depth information because early times correspond to shallow depths (high frequencies) and late times to deeper regions (low frequencies). The diffusion equation ∂B/∂t = (1/μσ)∇²B governs the transient, and the voltage induced in a receive coil is V(t) ∝ ∫ J(r,t)·dA. For a planar crack, the voltage perturbation scales as V_flaw/V₀ ∝ (a/δ) exp(−t/τ) with τ = μσδ²/2. Advanced array probes (e.g., meander‑line coils) enable imaging by scanning; the spatial resolution is limited by the coil width and the skin depth. Examiner traps: using the plane‑wave skin depth formula for a coil geometry without accounting for field non‑uniformity; neglecting the lift‑off signal which often dominates the impedance change; assuming the impedance change is linear with flaw depth for deep cracks where a > δ.

Wireless Power Transfer: Resonant Inductive Coupling and Rectenna Fundamentals

Wireless power transfer (WPT) via resonant inductive coupling uses two high‑Q LC resonators (transmitter and receiver) tuned to the same angular frequency ω₀ = 1/√(LC). The coupling coefficient k = M/√(L₁L₂) determines the power transfer efficiency η = (k²Q₁Q₂)/(1+√(1+k²Q₁Q₂))² for matched loads, where Qᵢ = ω₀Lᵢ/Rᵢ are the unloaded quality factors. Strong coupling (k√(Q₁Q₂) ≫ 1) yields η → 1, but requires precise alignment. The figure of merit is U = k√(Q₁Q₂). For a planar spiral coil of N turns, radius a, and trace width w, the inductance is approximated by L ≈ μ₀N²a[ln(8a/w)−2] and the series resistance includes skin‑effect loss R_ac = R_dc√(ω/ω₀) plus proximity effect. The mutual inductance between coaxial coils separated by distance d is M = μ₀πN₁N₂a₁²a₂²/(2(a₁²+d²)3/2) for a₂ ≪ a₁. Rectennas (rectifying antennas) convert incident electromagnetic waves into DC power. A typical rectenna consists of a patch antenna, a matching network, and a Schottky diode bridge. The RF‑to‑DC conversion efficiency η_rect = P_DC/P_RF depends on the diode’s I‑V characteristic I = I_s(eV/(nV_T)−1) and the input power level; maximum efficiency occurs at an optimal input power where the diode operates near its turn‑on voltage. The overall end‑to‑end efficiency is η_total = η_coupling × η_rect. Safety limits (ICNIRP) restrict the power density to 10 W/m² at 2.45 GHz for occupational exposure, which caps the maximum transferable power at a given distance. Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Warm Plasma: Landau Damping and Kinetic Effects

In a warm plasma the particle velocity distribution is no longer a delta function; finite temperature introduces a spread described by a Maxwell‑Boltzmann function. When an electromagnetic wave propagates through such a medium, resonant particles with velocities close to the wave phase velocity exchange energy with the wave. This resonant interaction leads to Landau damping, a collisionless attenuation mechanism that does not rely on binary collisions. The dielectric tensor for a warm, magnetized plasma is obtained by integrating the Vlasov equation over velocity space, yielding the plasma dispersion function Z(ζ) where ζ = ω/(k v_th). The imaginary part of Z gives the damping rate γ = −(π/2)^(1/2) (ω_p2/ k3 v_th3) ω exp(−ω2/2 k2 v_th2) for electrostatic Langmuir waves. For electromagnetic waves the damping is weaker because the electric field is largely transverse, but the same kinetic formalism applies and predicts finite absorption even when ω > ω_p.

The kinetic description also modifies the real part of the dispersion relation. The Bohm‑Gross correction ω2 = ω_p2 + 3 k2 v_th2 emerges from the principal value integral of the plasma dispersion function. In a magnetized plasma the warm‑plasma dielectric tensor contains the modified Bessel functions I_n(b) with b = k_⊥2 v_th2/Ω_c2, where Ω_c is the cyclotron frequency. These functions account for finite‑Larmor‑radius effects and give rise to Bernstein modes — electrostatic waves that exist at harmonics of Ω_c. The presence of these modes changes the accessibility of heating schemes such as ion cyclotron resonance heating, because the wave can mode‑convert into a Bernstein wave near the resonance layer.

Landau damping is not a dissipative process in the thermodynamic sense; the wave energy is transferred to the coherent motion of resonant particles, which subsequently phase‑mix and produce an irreversible increase of entropy. In numerical simulations the damping appears as a decay of the wave amplitude, but the total energy of the Vlasov‑Poisson system is conserved. This subtlety is often tested by asking whether the damping rate can be derived from a fluid model with an artificial viscosity term — the answer is no, because fluid closures cannot capture the velocity‑space resonance. The correct kinetic treatment requires solving the linearized Vlasov equation with the Landau contour prescription, which shifts the pole in the complex velocity plane and yields the imaginary contribution.

Experimental verification of Landau damping has been reported in laboratory devices such as the Large Plasma Device (LAPD) and in space plasma measurements by the Cluster and MMS missions. In the laboratory a Langmuir wave is launched by a grid and its amplitude recorded by a movable probe; the measured decay rate agrees with kinetic predictions within experimental uncertainty. In the solar wind, the observed spectral break near the ion gyroscale is often interpreted as the onset of kinetic Alfvén wave damping, a process closely related to Landau damping but involving both parallel and perpendicular resonances. These observations confirm that collisionless damping shapes the turbulent cascade in astrophysical plasmas.

For competitive examinations the key points are: (1) the plasma dispersion function Z(ζ) and its asymptotic expansions for |ζ|≫1 and |ζ|≪1; (2) the Bohm‑Gross shift of the Langmuir wave frequency; (3) the role of finite‑Larmor‑radius Bessel factors in the warm dielectric tensor; (4) the distinction between collisional and collisionless damping; and (5) the physical interpretation of Landau damping as reversible energy exchange followed by phase mixing. A typical trap is to apply the cold‑plasma dispersion relation ω2 = ω_p2 + c2 k2 to a warm plasma and ignore the 3 k2 v_th2 term, which leads to an overestimate of the phase velocity and an incorrect prediction of zero damping.

Electromagnetic Field Quantization in Cavities: Casimir‑Polder Forces

Quantizing the electromagnetic field inside a perfectly conducting cavity replaces the classical mode amplitudes by annihilation and creation operators that satisfy [a_λ, a_λ'†] = δ_λλ'. The zero‑point energy of each mode is (1/2)ħω_λ, and the sum over all modes diverges. The Casimir effect arises when the boundary conditions change — for example, two parallel plates at separation a support only discrete wavevectors k_z = nπ/a, while free space allows a continuum. The difference in zero‑point energy per unit area is E(a) = (ħcπ2)/(720 a3), giving an attractive pressure P = −∂E/∂a = −(ħcπ2)/(240 a4). This result assumes perfect conductors and zero temperature; real metals introduce a plasma frequency ω_p that cuts off high‑frequency modes, reducing the force at separations below the plasma wavelength λ_p = 2πc/ω_p.

The Casimir‑Polder force generalizes the plate‑plate geometry to an atom (or a polarizable particle) near a surface. The atom’s dynamic polarizability α(iξ) at imaginary frequencies couples to the modified vacuum fluctuations. The interaction energy is U(z) = −(ħ/2π) ∫0^∞ dξ α(iξ) Tr[G^(1)(r,r;iξ)], where G^(1) is the scattering part of the dyadic Green’s function for the electromagnetic field. For a ground‑state atom with static polarizability α(0) and a perfect conductor, the retarded limit (z ≫ c/ω0) yields U(z) = −(3ħc α(0))/(8π z4), while the non‑retarded van der Waals limit (z ≪ c/ω0) gives U(z) = −(ħ ω0 α(0)2)/(32π2 ε02 z3). The crossover distance is set by the dominant atomic transition wavelength λ0 = 2πc/ω0.

Finite temperature adds a Matsubara sum over discrete imaginary frequencies ξ_n = 2π n k_B T/ħ. At separations larger than the thermal wavelength λ_T = ħc/(k_B T) ≈ 7.6 µm at 300 K, the force becomes linear in T and the zero‑point contribution is exponentially suppressed. The Lifshitz formula unifies the Casimir, Casimir‑Polder, and van der Waals interactions for planar multilayer systems by expressing the free energy in terms of the reflection coefficients r_TE, r_TM of the media evaluated at imaginary frequencies. This formalism is essential for interpreting precision measurements with micro‑electromechanical systems (MEMS) where surface roughness, patch potentials, and finite conductivity all modify the ideal Casimir force.

Experimental verification has progressed from the classic torsion‑balance measurement by Sparnaay (1958) to modern atomic force microscopy (AFM) and micromechanical torsional oscillators that achieve sub‑percent agreement with the Lifshitz theory. Experiments using a gold‑coated sphere and a plate with a thin dielectric layer found that the measured force gradient matched theoretical predictions only when measured optical data for gold (including the Drude vs. plasma model debate) were used as input. The Drude model includes dissipation and predicts a reduced TE zero‑mode contribution, while the plasma model omits dissipation and yields a larger force. This subtlety is an active research area and a frequent source of conceptual questions.

For examination purposes the essential items are: (1) the derivation of the Casimir pressure between perfect conductors using mode summation or the argument principle; (2) the Casimir‑Polder potential in the retarded and non‑retarded limits; (3) the Lifshitz formula and the role of reflection coefficients at imaginary frequencies; (4) the temperature correction and the thermal wavelength λ_T; (5) the distinction between Drude and plasma models for real metals and their impact on the TE zero mode. A common trap is to quote the T=0 perfect‑conductor result for a realistic experiment at room temperature and micron separations, ignoring both finite conductivity and thermal photons.

Nonlinear Wave Mixing in Optical Fibers: Four‑Wave Mixing and Raman Scattering

In silica fibers the third‑order susceptibility χ^(3) mediates parametric interactions among co‑propagating waves. Four‑wave mixing (FWM) occurs when two pump photons at frequencies ω1 and ω2 generate a signal at ω3 = 2ω1 − ω2 and an idler at ω4 = 2ω2 − ω1, provided the phase‑matching condition Δβ = β3 + β4 − β1 − β2 + 2γ(P1 + P2) = 0 is satisfied. Here β_j are the propagation constants expanded around the pump frequencies, and γ = n2 ω0/(c A_eff) is the nonlinear coefficient with n2 the Kerr index and A_eff the effective mode area. The phase mismatch Δβ is dominated by the fiber dispersion β2 (group‑velocity dispersion) and β3 (third‑order dispersion). In the anomalous dispersion regime (β2 < 0) the nonlinear phase shift can compensate the linear mismatch, enabling broadband FWM used for wavelength conversion and supercontinuum generation.

Spontaneous Raman scattering provides a competing gain mechanism. The Raman gain coefficient g_R(Ω) peaks at a frequency shift Ω_R ≈ 13.2 THz (≈ 440 cm^−1) in silica, with a bandwidth of ≈ 6 THz. The coupled‑mode equations for the pump (A_p) and Stokes (A_s) envelopes are ∂_z A_p = −(α/2) A_p − iγ(|A_p|2 + 2|A_s|2) A_p − (g_R/2) |A_s|2 A_p and ∂_z A_s = −(α/2) A_s − iγ(|A_s|2 + 2|A_p|2) A_s + (g_R/2) |A_p|2 A_s. The Raman term transfers energy from pump to Stokes even when phase matching is imperfect, because the material response provides the required momentum. In high‑power fiber amplifiers Raman scattering can deplete the pump and limit the achievable output power, a phenomenon known as the Raman threshold.

Interplay between FWM and Raman scattering shapes the spectral evolution of intense pulses. For picosecond pulses in the normal dispersion regime, Raman‑induced self‑frequency shift (SSFS) moves the pulse toward longer wavelengths, while FWM generates symmetric sidebands around the pump. The combined effect can produce a continuum that spans more than an octave. Numerical simulations using the generalized nonlinear Schrödinger equation (GNLSE) include the Raman response function h_R(t) = (τ12 + τ22)/(τ1 τ22) e^(−t/τ2) sin(t/τ1) with τ1 = 12.2 fs, τ2 = 32 fs, and the fractional Raman contribution f_R = 0.18. The GNLSE also accounts for self‑steepening (1/ω0 ∂_t) and third‑order dispersion.

Phase‑matching engineering via photonic‑crystal fibers (PCFs) allows control of the zero‑dispersion wavelength (ZDW). By designing the air‑hole lattice, the ZDW can be shifted into the visible, enabling FWM between a near‑infrared pump and visible signal/idler pairs. The effective area A_eff in PCFs can be as low as 1 µm2, enhancing γ by an order of magnitude compared to standard single‑mode fiber. However, small A_eff also raises the peak intensity, increasing the risk of damage and stimulating higher‑order nonlinear effects such as self‑phase modulation and optical wave breaking.

Examination focus: (1) write the phase‑matching condition for degenerate and non‑degenerate FWM including the nonlinear contribution; (2) derive the Raman gain spectrum from the imaginary part of χ^(3)(Ω); (3) explain how the Raman response function enters the GNLSE; (4) discuss the impact of dispersion engineering on supercontinuum bandwidth; (5) identify the Raman threshold condition P_th ≈ 16 A_eff/(g_R L_eff) for a fiber of effective length L_eff. A typical trap is to neglect the nonlinear phase term 2γP in the phase‑matching condition, which leads to an incorrect prediction that FWM is impossible in the normal dispersion regime.

Electromagnetic Topology and Helicity Conservation

Electromagnetic helicity is the integral of the Chern‑Simons 3‑form h = ∫ (A·B − C·E) d3x, where A is the vector potential (∇×A = B) and C is the dual potential (∇×C = −E). In vacuum, Maxwell’s equations are symmetric under electric‑magnetic duality, and the helicity density χ = A·B − C·E is conserved locally: ∂_t χ + ∇·(E×A + B×C) = 0. This conservation law follows from Noether’s theorem applied to the duality rotation symmetry. In the presence of charges and currents the continuity equation acquires source terms proportional to J·A + ρ_e C, showing that helicity is not gauge invariant unless the potentials satisfy the Lorenz gauge and the fields vanish at infinity.

The topological interpretation of helicity emerges when field lines are knotted or linked. For a pair of closed magnetic flux tubes with fluxes Φ1, Φ2 and linking number Lk, the magnetic helicity is H_m = 2 Lk Φ1 Φ2. This integer linking number is a topological invariant under ideal magnetohydrodynamics (MHD), where the magnetic field is frozen into a perfectly conducting fluid. The conservation of magnetic helicity in ideal MHD constrains the relaxation of turbulent plasmas toward minimum‑energy force‑free states (Taylor relaxation). The final state satisfies ∇×B = λ B with constant λ, and the total helicity determines λ through the relation H = 2∫ A·B dV = (2/λ) ∫ B2 dV.

In optical fields, the helicity density reduces to the difference between right‑ and left‑circularly polarized photon densities. The total helicity equals the expectation value of the spin angular momentum operator per photon, ℏσ, where σ = ±1. For structured light such as Laguerre‑Gaussian beams, the orbital angular momentum (OAM) is separate from spin, but the total angular momentum J = L + S is conserved. The topological charge of an optical vortex (the winding number of the phase) is robust against perturbations that preserve the beam’s paraxial nature, making it useful for classical and quantum communication.

Helicity conservation also underpins the optical theorem for scattering of electromagnetic waves by chiral objects. The extinction cross‑section for a plane wave of definite helicity is related to the forward scattering amplitude of the same helicity. For a reciprocal, lossless scatterer the helicity‑preserving and helicity‑flipping scattering matrices satisfy unitarity constraints that lead to sum rules such as the Kerker conditions for zero backscattering. These concepts are increasingly relevant in nanophotonics and metamaterial design.

Key examination points: (1) definition of electromagnetic helicity and its gauge‑invariant formulation using the Riemann‑Silberstein vector F = E + i c B; (2) magnetic helicity in ideal MHD and Taylor relaxation; (3) separation of spin and orbital angular momentum in paraxial beams; (4) helicity‑preserving vs. helicity‑flipping scattering channels; (5) the role of duality symmetry and its breaking by charges. A common trap is to confuse magnetic helicity (A·B) with the total electromagnetic helicity (A·B − C·E) and to assume the former is conserved in the presence of electric fields.

Time‑Domain Electromagnetics: Finite‑Difference Time‑Domain Method and Stability Criteria

The finite‑difference time‑domain (FDTD) method discretizes Maxwell’s curl equations on a staggered Yee lattice, where electric field components are centered on edges and magnetic field components on faces of a cubic cell of size Δx = Δy = Δz = Δ. The leap‑frog update advances E at integer time steps nΔt and H at half‑integer steps (n+½)Δt. For a 3‑D Cartesian grid the update equations are E_x^(n+1) = E_x^n + (Δt/ε) [(H_z^(n+½) − H_z^(n+½))/Δy − (H_y^(n+½) − H_y^(n+½))/Δz] and analogous expressions for the other components. The method is second‑order accurate in both space and time provided the fields are smooth.

Stability of the explicit FDTD scheme is governed by the Courant‑Friedrichs‑Lewy (CFL) condition. For a uniform cubic grid the maximum time step is Δt_max = Δ/(c√3). If the grid is anisotropic (Δx ≠ Δy ≠ Δz) the condition generalizes to 1/Δt2 ≥ c2 (1/Δx2 + 1/Δy2 + 1/Δz2). Violating the CFL limit leads to exponential growth of numerical noise. The numerical dispersion relation for a plane wave propagating at angles (θ, φ) is (1/Δt sin(ωΔt/2))2 = c2 [ (1/Δx sin(k_xΔx/2))2 + (1/Δy sin(k_yΔy/2))2 + (1/Δz sin(k_zΔz/2))2 ]. This relation shows that phase velocity depends on direction and wavelength, causing anisotropy errors that are most severe for waves propagating along grid diagonals.

Absorbing boundary conditions are essential for open‑region problems. The perfectly matched layer (PML) introduces a complex coordinate stretching s_x = 1 + σ_x/(iωε) in the frequency domain, which in the time domain becomes a split‑field formulation with auxiliary differential equations for the electric and magnetic components. A polynomial grading of the conductivity σ_x(x) = σ_max (x/d)^m with m = 3–4 yields reflection coefficients below −40 dB for a layer thickness of 8–10 cells. Alternative approaches include the convolutional PML (CPML) which uses a recursive convolution to implement a frequency‑independent stretching, improving performance for evanescent waves.

Material dispersion is incorporated via auxiliary differential equation (ADE) or recursive convolution (RC) techniques. For a Lorentz medium with susceptibility χ(ω) = ω_p2/(ω02 − ω2 − iγω), the time‑domain polarization P satisfies ∂_t2 P + γ ∂_t P + ω02 P = ε0 ω_p2 E. This second‑order ODE is discretized alongside the field updates. For Debye relaxation χ(ω) = χ0/(1 − iωτ) a first‑order ADE suffices. The Drude model for metals (ω0 = 0) requires careful treatment of the static conductivity to avoid numerical instability at low frequencies.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Magnetized Plasma: Whistler, Alfvén, and Magnetosonic Modes

When a plasma is permeated by a static magnetic field B₀, the dielectric response becomes a tensor. The cold‑plasma dispersion relation for waves propagating at an angle θ to B₀ follows from the Appleton‑Hartree formula. For parallel propagation (θ = 0) two circularly polarized eigenmodes exist: the right‑hand (R) and left‑hand (L) modes. Their refractive indices are

n²_R = 1 − ωₚ² / [ω(ω − Ωₑ)] , n²_L = 1 − ωₚ² / [ω(ω + Ωₑ)]

where ωₚ is the electron plasma frequency and Ωₑ = eB₀/mₑ is the electron cyclotron frequency (sign included). The R‑mode exhibits a resonance at ω = Ωₑ (electron cyclotron resonance) and a cutoff at ω = ω_R = ½[Ωₑ + √(Ωₑ² + 4ωₚ²)]. The L‑mode has a cutoff at ω = ω_L = ½[−Ωₑ + √(Ωₑ² + 4ωₚ²)] and no electron resonance (ion cyclotron resonance appears at much lower frequencies).

In the low‑frequency limit ω ≪ Ωᵢ (ion cyclotron frequency) the shear Alfvén wave emerges with phase velocity v_A = B₀/√(μ₀ρ) and dispersion ω = k_∥ v_A. The compressional (fast) magnetosonic wave propagates across B₀ with ω² = k²(v_A² + c_s²) where c_s is the sound speed. The slow magnetosonic wave is largely electrostatic and follows ω² = k_∥² c_s² v_A²/(v_A² + c_s²). Whistler waves occupy the frequency band Ωᵢ ≪ ω ≪ Ωₑ; their dispersion for parallel propagation reduces to ω = (Ωₑ/ωₚ²) k² c², giving a group velocity that increases with frequency — the characteristic “whistler” tone heard in magnetospheric radio observations.

Oblique propagation couples transverse and longitudinal components, producing the full Stix dispersion surface. The resonance cone appears when the wave normal approaches the angle satisfying n² → ∞ for the extraordinary mode; energy then flows nearly perpendicular to k. This cone governs heating in electron cyclotron resonance heating (ECRH) and the design of plasma diagnostics such as reflectometry. In space physics, the same formalism explains the propagation of chorus emissions, hiss, and the formation of the plasmapause. Examination questions often ask for the cutoff and resonance frequencies of the R and L modes, the condition for whistler propagation (ω < Ωₑ), and the physical distinction between shear Alfvén and compressional magnetosonic waves.

Topological Insulators and the Axion Electrodynamics of Surface States

Three‑dimensional topological insulators (TIs) are bulk insulators with an odd number of Dirac‑like surface states protected by time‑reversal symmetry. The electromagnetic response of a TI is captured by the axion term added to the Maxwell Lagrangian: ℒ_θ = (α/π) θ **E·B**, where α is the fine‑structure constant and θ = π (mod 2π) for a strong TI. This term modifies the constitutive relations to

**D** = ε**E** + (α/π)θ **B**, **H** = **B**/μ − (α/π)θ **E** .

The spatial variation of θ at a surface (θ jumps from π inside to 0 outside) yields a half‑quantized surface Hall conductivity σ_xy = (e²/2h) per Dirac cone. Consequently, a static electric field induces a surface current **J**_s = σ_xy **ẑ × E**, and a magnetic field induces a surface charge density ρ_s = −σ_xy **ẑ·B**. These are the topological magnetoelectric effect (TME). In a slab geometry the Faraday and Kerr rotation angles become quantized: θ_F = θ_K = α (mod π) when the film thickness is much smaller than the wavelength.

Experimental signatures include the observation of a half‑integer quantum Hall plateau in magnetotransport of thin TI films, the universal Faraday rotation measured in terahertz time‑domain spectroscopy, and the prediction of an image magnetic monopole when an electric charge is placed near a TI surface (the “axion electrodynamics” mirror charge). The axion angle θ can be tuned by magnetic doping (breaking time‑reversal) or by applying an external magnetic field, leading to a continuous interpolation between θ = 0 (trivial insulator) and θ = π (strong TI). In the presence of a domain wall where θ changes by 2π, a chiral edge mode appears, realizing the (2+1)‑dimensional quantum anomalous Hall effect.

From an examination perspective, candidates should be able to: (1) write the modified Maxwell equations with the axion term; (2) derive the surface Hall conductivity from the jump in θ; (3) explain why the Faraday rotation is quantized in units of the fine‑structure constant; (4) discuss the role of time‑reversal symmetry breaking in gapping the surface Dirac cone and its impact on θ. A frequent trap is to confuse the axion term with the ordinary magnetoelectric effect in multiferroics, which is not quantized and does not require topological band structure.

Electromagnetic Energy Harvesting: Rectenna Architecture, Impedance Matching, and Thermodynamic Limits

A rectifying antenna (rectenna) converts incident electromagnetic power into direct current. The system comprises an antenna, a matching network, a nonlinear rectifying element (typically a Schottky diode or a CMOS transistor), and a low‑pass filter with a storage capacitor. The antenna captures power P_inc = (1/2) Re{**E** × **H***}·**A_eff**, where **A_eff** = (λ²/4π) G is the effective aperture and G the gain. The available RF power at the diode terminals is P_RF = η_match P_inc, with η_match the matching efficiency.

The diode I‑V characteristic I = I_s[exp(qV/nkT) − 1] determines the rectified DC voltage V_DC for a given RF voltage amplitude V_RF. In the small‑signal regime (V_RF ≪ nkT/q) the conversion efficiency η_rect ≈ (V_RF²/4) (q/nkT)² (R_L/(R_s+R_L)²), where R_s is the diode series resistance and R_L the load. For large signals the diode operates near its turn‑on voltage V_on, and the optimum load is R_opt ≈ V_on/I_s exp(qV_on/nkT). The overall RF‑to‑DC efficiency η = η_match η_rect η_filter can exceed 80 % at 2.45 GHz with a well‑designed Schottky diode (e.g., GaAs/AlGaAs) and a high‑Q matching network.

Thermodynamic limits arise from the second law: a rectenna at temperature T_c harvesting radiation from a source at temperature T_h cannot exceed the Carnot efficiency η_C = 1 − T_c/T_h if the radiation is treated as a heat bath. However, for monochromatic coherent illumination the limit is the Landsberg efficiency η_L = 1 − (4/3)(T_c/T_h) + (1/3)(T_c/T_h)⁴, which for solar radiation (T_h ≈ 6000 K, T_c = 300 K) gives ≈ 93 %. Practical rectennas are limited by diode breakdown voltage, harmonic re‑radiation, and impedance mismatch across the bandwidth. Multi‑band and array configurations use power‑combining networks (e.g., Wilkinson dividers) to increase harvested power while maintaining matching.

Key examination points: (1) derive the expression for optimal load resistance from the diode equation; (2) explain the role of the matching network in maximizing power transfer (conjugate match at fundamental, open/short at harmonics); (3) distinguish between coherent (laser, microwave) and incoherent (thermal) illumination limits; (4) calculate the maximum DC power for a given incident power density, antenna gain, and diode parameters. A common trap is to apply the solar‑cell Shockley‑Queisser limit to a rectenna, which assumes a p‑n junction under broadband illumination, not a single‑frequency driven diode.

Signal Integrity in High‑Speed Interconnects: Crosstalk, Return‑Path Discontinuities, and Eye‑Diagram Analysis

Modern serial links (PCIe 5.0, 112 Gb/s PAM‑4) require careful electromagnetic design of differential pairs on printed‑circuit boards. The differential characteristic impedance Z₀_diff = 2Z₀(1 − k) where Z₀ is the single‑ended impedance and k the coupling coefficient. Crosstalk arises from both near‑end (NEXT) and far‑end (FEXT) coupling. For a uniform coupled line of length ℓ, the FEXT voltage on the victim line is V_FEXT ≈ (1/2)(Z₀_diff) (C_m/C_d + L_m/L_d) (dV_aggressor/dt) ℓ/v_p, where C_m, L_m are mutual capacitance and inductance per unit length, C_d, L_d the differential values, and v_p the propagation velocity. NEXT is roughly twice FEXT for the same length.

Return‑path discontinuities (vias, split planes, connector transitions) create impedance mismatches that generate reflections. The reflection coefficient Γ = (Z_L − Z₀)/(Z_L + Z₀) determines the voltage step seen at the receiver. A via stub of length ℓ_stub acts as a shunt resonant stub with input impedance Z_stub = jZ₀ tan(βℓ_stub); at frequencies where βℓ_stub = π/2 the stub presents a short, causing a deep notch in the insertion loss (S₂₁). Back‑drilling removes the unused stub portion, pushing the resonance beyond the signal bandwidth.

Eye‑diagram analysis aggregates many unit intervals to visualize inter‑symbol interference (ISI), jitter, and noise. The eye height at the sampling instant is V_eye = V_signal − (ISI + V_noise). Random jitter (RJ) follows a Gaussian distribution; deterministic jitter (DJ) includes duty‑cycle distortion, inter‑symbol interference, and periodic crosstalk. Total jitter at a target bit‑error rate (BER) is TJ(BER) = DJ + n·RJ where n = √2 erfc⁻¹(2·BER). For BER = 10⁻¹², n ≈ 7.0. The eye mask specifies minimum eye height and width; compliance requires TJ < UI·(1 − mask_width) and V_eye > mask_height.

Examination focus: (1) compute FEXT/NEXT for a given geometry using per‑unit‑length parameters; (2) determine the via stub resonance frequency and required back‑drill depth; (3) extract RJ and DJ from a measured eye diagram and predict BER; (4) explain the difference between single‑ended and differential signaling in terms of common‑mode rejection and crosstalk immunity. A frequent trap is to treat differential crosstalk as the simple difference of single‑ended crosstalk, ignoring the common‑mode conversion that occurs at asymmetries.

Electromagnetic Field Quantization in Open Quantum Systems: Input‑Output Formalism and Quantum Langevin Equations

Quantizing the electromagnetic field in the presence of lossy, dispersive media requires coupling the system modes to a continuum of bath oscillators. dâ/dt = −(iω₀ + κ/2)â + √κ â_in(t) ,

The memory kernel K(t−t′) = ∫ dω J(ω) e−iω(t−t′) leads to an integro‑differential Langevin equation. In the strong‑coupling regime (g > κ, γ) the system exhibits vacuum Rabi splitting, observable as two peaks in the output spectrum S_out(ω) = |S(ω)|². Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Nonlinear Media: Solitons and Self‑Focusing

In linear media the principle of superposition holds and each frequency component propagates independently. In a nonlinear dielectric the polarization P acquires terms proportional to higher powers of the electric field E. The lowest‑order nonlinearity is the third‑order susceptibility χ⁽³⁾, giving a field‑dependent refractive index n = n₀ + n₂|E|² where n₂ = 3χ⁽³⁾/(4n₀²ε₀c). When n₂ > 0 the medium exhibits a Kerr self‑focusing effect: the central, higher‑intensity part of a beam experiences a larger index and bends toward the axis, counteracting diffraction. If the beam power exceeds the critical power Pcr = (3.77 λ²)/(8π n₀ n₂) (for a Gaussian beam in a bulk medium), self‑focusing dominates and the beam can collapse to a filament. In optical fibers the transverse confinement modifies the threshold; the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLSE) i∂A/∂z − (β₂/2)∂²A/∂t² + γ|A|²A = 0 governs pulse evolution, where β₂ is the group‑velocity dispersion (GVD) and γ = n₂ω₀/(cAeff) the nonlinear coefficient. For anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0) the Kerr nonlinearity can balance GVD, producing a fundamental soliton with peak power P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²) and sech‑shaped temporal profile A(t) = √P₀ sech(t/T₀). Higher‑order solitons (N > 1) undergo periodic breathing. In the normal‑dispersion regime (β₂ > 0) self‑phase modulation broadens the spectrum without temporal compression, leading to supercontinuum generation when combined with higher‑order dispersion and Raman scattering. Self‑focusing in bulk media can also generate multiple filaments, each carrying approximately the critical power, a phenomenon exploited in laser‑induced plasma channels and remote sensing. The interplay of nonlinearity, dispersion, diffraction, and plasma generation (via multiphoton ionization) determines the ultimate filament length and the spectral content of the emitted light. Competitive examinations often test the ability to (1) derive the critical power expression from the NLSE variational approximation, (2) identify the sign of β₂ required for bright solitons, (3) compute the soliton period z₀ = πT₀²/(2|β₂|), and (4) discuss the role of the Raman self‑frequency shift in long‑distance soliton propagation.

Electromagnetic Field Theory in Curved Spacetime: Gravitational Lensing of Light

General relativity describes gravity as curvature of a four‑dimensional Lorentzian manifold with metric gμν. Maxwell’s equations in curved spacetime retain their covariant form ∇μFμν = μ₀Jν, ∇[μFνρ] = 0, where ∇ is the Levi‑Civita connection. In the geometric‑optics limit (wavelength ≪ curvature radius) the electromagnetic field is represented by a rapidly varying phase S(x) and a slowly varying amplitude aμ. The wave‑vector kμ = ∂μS satisfies the null condition gμνkμkν = 0, so light follows null geodesics. The deflection angle for a ray passing a spherically symmetric mass M at impact parameter b is, to first order in GM/(c²b), Δθ = 4GM/(c²b). This result, confirmed during the 1919 solar eclipse, is twice the Newtonian prediction because both space and time curvature contribute. For extended lenses (galaxies, clusters) the lens equation β = θ − α(θ) relates source position β, image position θ, and reduced deflection angle α = (Dls/Ds)∫∇⊥Φ dl, where Φ is the Newtonian potential and Dls, Ds are angular‑diameter distances. Strong lensing produces multiple images, arcs, and Einstein rings when the source lies within the Einstein radius θE = √(4GM Dls/(c² Dl Ds)). Weak lensing measures the statistical shear γ of background galaxy shapes, providing a probe of dark matter distribution. Microlensing by compact objects (stars, planets) causes transient magnification of a point source, with a characteristic Paczyński light curve. In the wave‑optics regime (wavelength comparable to Schwarzschild radius) diffraction modifies the amplification factor; the Kirchhoff integral over the lens plane yields the complex amplification F(w,y) = (w/2πi)∫exp[iw(½|x−y|² − ψ(x))] d²x, where w = 4πGMf/c³ is the dimensionless frequency and ψ the lens potential. This formalism is essential for interpreting gravitational‑wave lensing and for proposed tests of quantum gravity using interferometric observations of lensed electromagnetic counterparts. Examination questions frequently require (1) writing the null geodesic equation in Schwarzschild coordinates, (2) deriving the Einstein radius for a point mass lens, (3) distinguishing strong, weak, and micro lensing regimes, and (4) explaining why the geometric‑optics approximation fails for low‑frequency gravitational waves.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Quasi‑Periodic Structures: Fibonacci and Thue‑Morse Sequences

Quasi‑periodic dielectric stacks lack translational symmetry but possess long‑range order described by substitution rules. The Fibonacci sequence is generated by the rule S → SL, L → S, producing layer thicknesses that follow the golden ratio τ = (1+√5)/2. The Thue‑Morse sequence follows the complementary rule 0 → 01, 1 → 10, yielding a binary pattern with no periodic repetition. Both sequences generate photonic band structures that exhibit a hierarchy of mini‑gaps and critical states, unlike the single complete gap of a periodic Bragg stack. Transfer‑matrix analysis shows that the trace map for the Fibonacci stack obeys the invariant I = (Tr M_n)2 + (Tr Mn-1)2 + (Tr Mn-2)2 - 2 Tr M_n Tr Mn-1 Tr Mn-2 - 1, which remains constant across generations. This invariant governs the scaling of transmission spectra and leads to a fractal‑like density of states. In the Thue‑Morse case, the trace map is not invariant but the spectrum is singular‑continuous with zero Lebesgue measure, implying that extended Bloch‑like states are absent. Experimental realizations using porous silicon or polymer multilayers confirm the predicted transmission dips at frequencies satisfying the generalized Bragg condition 2 n_eff d_eff = m λ, where the effective optical thickness d_eff follows the quasi‑periodic sequence. The presence of critical states — wavefunctions that are neither fully localized nor fully extended — makes these structures attractive for broadband filters, sensors, and slow‑light devices where group velocity dispersion can be engineered by adjusting the generation order. Competitive examinations often ask for the derivation of the trace map invariant, the identification of the scaling exponent for the transmission coefficient near a mini‑gap, and the physical distinction between the singular‑continuous spectrum of Thue‑Morse and the pure‑point spectrum of a random Anderson model.

Electromagnetic Field Theory in Moving Media: Fresnel Drag and Relativistic Constitutive Relations

When a dielectric medium moves with velocity v relative to the laboratory frame, the constitutive relations acquire magnetoelectric coupling terms. In the rest frame of the medium the fields satisfy D' = ε E' and B' = μ H'. Applying the Lorentz transformation for the electromagnetic field tensor yields the laboratory‑frame relations D = ε E + (ε μ - 1/c2) v × H and B = μ H - (ε μ - 1/c2) v × E, where c is the vacuum speed of light. The term (ε μ - 1/c2) v × H represents the Fresnel drag coefficient, first measured by Fizeau in 1851 using water flowing in a tube. For a non‑magnetic medium (μ ≈ μ0) the drag coefficient reduces to f = 1 - 1/n2, where n = √(ε/ε0) is the refractive index. This result explains why light propagating parallel to the flow experiences a phase velocity v_p = c/n + v (1 - 1/n2). In modern optics the same formalism describes the Sagnac effect in fiber‑optic gyroscopes, where the effective optical path length changes by ΔL = 2 A Ω / c for a loop of area A rotating at angular velocity Ω. Relativistic constitutive relations also predict the inverse Faraday effect: a circularly polarized wave in a moving medium induces a static magnetization proportional to the Poynting vector. Examinations frequently test the derivation of the transformed permittivity and permeability tensors for a medium moving at arbitrary velocity, the calculation of the Fresnel drag for a dispersive medium where n(ω) depends on frequency, and the application of the Minkowski momentum density g = D × B versus the Abraham momentum density g = E × H / c2 in moving media. A common trap is to neglect the magnetoelectric cross terms when v is not collinear with the wavevector, leading to incorrect predictions for non‑reciprocal phase shifts in integrated photonic circuits.

Derivation of Fresnel Drag Coefficient
A light beam of wavelength λ0 in vacuum propagates through a water column of length L moving at speed v = 5 m/s parallel to the beam. The refractive index of water at λ0 is n = 1.33. Compute the fringe shift observed in a Fizeau interferometer when the flow direction is reversed, assuming the interferometer uses a Michelson configuration with the moving column in one arm.
The phase velocity in the laboratory frame for light co‑propagating with the flow is v_+ = c/n + v (1 - 1/n2). For counter‑propagation v_- = c/n - v (1 - 1/n2). The optical path difference for a round trip through the column is Δφ = (2π/λ0) [ (L/v_+) - (L/v_-) ] ≈ (2π/λ0) (2 L v / c2) (n2 - 1). Substituting n = 1.33 gives n2 - 1 = 0.7689. With L = 1 m, v = 5 m/s, c = 3×108 m/s, λ0 = 600 nm, Δφ ≈ (2π/6×10-7) × (2 × 1 × 5 / 9×1016) × 0.7689 ≈ 1.8×10-3 rad. The fringe shift is Δφ/(2π) ≈ 2.9×10-4 fringes, detectable with phase‑shifting interferometry. The key steps are: (1) write the relativistic addition of velocities for phase velocity, (2) linearize for v ≪ c, (3) compute round‑trip phase difference, (4) convert to fringe count.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem involves moving media, first write the rest‑frame constitutive relations, then apply the Lorentz transformation to the field tensor. Keep terms only up to first order in v/c unless the question explicitly asks for higher‑order relativistic corrections. Remember that the Fresnel drag coefficient is (1 - 1/n2) for non‑magnetic media; for magnetic media replace n2 with ε μ / ε0 μ0. Check whether the wavevector is parallel or perpendicular to v, because the cross‑product terms change sign and magnitude accordingly.

Electromagnetic Radiation from Time‑Varying Currents: Retarded Potentials and Near‑Field/Far‑Field Transition

The fields of an arbitrary current distribution J(r', t) are obtained from the retarded scalar and vector potentials φ(r, t) = (1/4π ε0) ∫ ρ(r', t_r)/|r - r'| d3r' and A(r, t) = (μ0/4π) ∫ J(r', t_r)/|r - r'| d3r', where the retarded time t_r = t - |r - r'|/c. Expanding the denominator for observation distances r ≫ source size yields the familiar multipole series. The leading term gives the radiation fields E_rad = -μ0 ∂/∂t [ ∫ J(r', t_r) d3r' ]_⊥ / (4π r) and B_rad = μ0 [ ∂/∂t ∫ J(r', t_r) d3r' ]_⊥ × r̂ / (4π r), where the subscript ⊥ denotes the component transverse to r̂. The next order produces the induction (1/r2) and electrostatic (1/r3) fields that dominate the near zone. The boundary between near and far field is conventionally taken at r ≈ λ/2π, where the magnitudes of the 1/r and 1/r2 terms become comparable. For a short dipole of length ℓ ≪ λ carrying a sinusoidal current I0 e-i ω t, the exact fields are E_θ = (i η0 k I0 ℓ / 4π) (1 + 1/(i k r) - 1/(k r)2) sin θ ei(k r - ω t) / r and H_φ = (i k I0 ℓ / 4π) (1 + 1/(i k r)) sin θ ei(k r - ω t) / r, with η0 = √(μ0/ε0). The term proportional to 1/(k r)2 is the quasi‑static electric field, the 1/(k r) term is the induction field, and the constant term is the radiation field. In the near zone (k r ≪ 1) the electric field behaves as 1/r3 and stores reactive energy; in the far zone (k r ≫ 1) the 1/r term carries real power. Examinations often require the derivation of the far‑field pattern from the vector potential, the calculation of the radiation resistance R_rad = 2 P_rad / |I0|2 for a short dipole (R_rad = 80 π2 (ℓ/λ)2 Ω), and the identification of the distance at which the reactive power equals the radiated power. A frequent mistake is to apply the far‑field approximation inside the reactive near zone, leading to an overestimate of received power in near‑field communication systems such as RFID.

Electromagnetic Field Quantization in Waveguides: Photon States and Casimir Effect in Confined Geometries

Quantization of the electromagnetic field in a perfectly conducting rectangular waveguide of dimensions a × b proceeds by expanding the vector potential in the complete set of transverse electric (TE) and transverse magnetic (TM) modes. The mode functions satisfy the Helmholtz equation (∇_t2 + k_c2) ψ = 0 with boundary conditions ψ = 0 for TM and ∂ψ/∂n = 0 for TE on the walls. The cutoff wavenumber is k_c = √((mπ/a)2 + (nπ/b)2) for integers m, n ≥ 0 (not both zero for TE). Each mode behaves as an independent harmonic oscillator with frequency ωmn = c √(k_c2 + k_z2), where k_z is the longitudinal wavevector. The Hamiltonian becomes H = Σmn ∫ dk_z ħ ωmn (amn^†(k_z) amn(k_z) + 1/2). The zero‑point energy Σ ħ ω/2 diverges; regularization via the zeta‑function or exponential cutoff yields a finite Casimir energy per unit length E_C = - (π2 ħ c / 720) (1/a3 + 1/b3) for a square waveguide a = b. This result differs from the parallel‑plate Casimir energy by a geometry‑dependent factor. In realistic waveguides with finite conductivity, the mode structure is modified by surface impedance boundary conditions, leading to a reduction of the Casimir force at separations comparable to the skin depth. The photon states in a waveguide are not plane waves but guided modes with a well‑defined transverse profile; they carry momentum ħ k_z along the guide and exhibit a group velocity v_g = dω/dk_z = c2 k_z / ω. Experiments using superconducting coplanar waveguides have measured the Lamb shift of artificial atoms coupled to waveguide photons, confirming the modified density of states. Competitive questions may ask for the derivation of the mode normalization constant, the calculation of the Casimir force between two parallel waveguides separated by a gap, or the explanation of why the TE10 mode has no cutoff in a parallel‑plate guide but does in a rectangular guide. A common pitfall is to forget that the zero‑point sum includes both TE and TM polarizations and that the m = 0 or n = 0 TE modes are allowed while TM modes require both indices non‑zero.

Electromagnetic Scattering from Rough Surfaces: Kirchhoff Approximation and Small Perturbation Method

Scattering from a randomly rough surface z = h(x, y) with height statistics ⟨h⟩ = 0 and correlation function C(ρ) = ⟨h(r) h(r+ρ)⟩ is a classic problem in remote sensing and radar cross‑section analysis. Two complementary analytical approaches are the Kirchhoff approximation (KA) and the small perturbation method (SPM). The KA, also known as the tangent‑plane approximation, assumes the surface radius of curvature is large compared to the wavelength λ and that the local surface can be replaced by its tangent plane. The scattered field in the far zone is then E_s(k_s) ≈ (i k2 / 2π) (ε - 1) ∫_S e-i (k_s - k_i)·r' [ n̂·(E_i + E_r) ] dS', where k_i and k_s are incident and scattered wavevectors, n̂ is the local normal, and E_r is the reflected field from the tangent plane. The KA yields the well‑known geometric‑optics scattering coefficient σ0 = |R(θ_i)|2 / (cos4 θ_i) (1/2π) ∫ e-i q·ρ e-q2 σ_h2 d2ρ, where q = k_s_t - k_i_t is the transverse wavevector transfer, σ_h2 is the height variance, and R is the Fresnel reflection coefficient. The SPM expands the fields in powers of the surface height h, assuming k σ_h ≪ 1 and k l_c ≪ 1 where l_c is the correlation length. The first‑order SPM gives a scattering amplitude proportional to the Fourier transform of the surface profile: f_SPM(q) ∝ (ε - 1) k_i_z k_s_z ∫ h(ρ) e-i q·ρ d2ρ. The resulting bistatic scattering coefficient is σ0_SPM = k4 cos2 θ_i cos2 θ_s |ε - 1|2 W(q) / (4π), where W(q) is the surface power spectral density. The KA is accurate for gently undulating surfaces with large correlation lengths (k l_c ≫ 1), while SPM works for slightly rough surfaces with small correlation lengths (k l_c ≪ 1). A unified model such as the two‑scale model combines both by separating the surface into large‑scale facets treated with KA and small‑scale roughness treated with SPM. Examination problems often require the derivation of the first‑order SPM scattering coefficient for a Gaussian correlation function C(ρ) = σ_h2 exp(-ρ2 / l_c2), the identification of the validity domains in the (k σ_h, k l_c) plane, and the explanation of why the KA predicts a specular peak that narrows as the surface becomes smoother. A frequent error is to apply the KA to surfaces with slopes exceeding the critical angle for total internal reflection, where shadowing and multiple scattering become significant.

  • Applying the far‑field radiation formula inside the reactive near zone (k r ≪ 1) and overestimating received power.
  • Omitting the TE0n or TE_m0 modes when summing zero‑point energies for waveguide Casimir calculations.
  • Using the Kirchhoff approximation for surfaces with rms slope > 0.3 rad, where shadowing invalidates the tangent‑plane assumption.
  • Treating the first‑order SPM result as valid for k σ_h > 0.3, ignoring the need for higher‑order terms.
  • Forgetting that the Fresnel drag coefficient for a magnetic medium involves ε μ rather than n2 alone.

Electromagnetic Field Theory in Nonlocal Media: Spatial Dispersion and the Hydrodynamic Model

In a local medium the constitutive relation D(ω) = ε(ω) E(ω) assumes that the polarization at a point depends only on the electric field at that same point. Real metals and doped semiconductors exhibit spatial dispersion: the response at wavevector k depends on the field distribution over a finite range. The hydrodynamic model treats the free‑electron gas as a charged fluid with pressure term β²∇(∇·J) where β² = 3/5 v_F² for a degenerate Fermi gas (v_F is the Fermi velocity). The resulting nonlocal dielectric function reads ε(ω, k) = ε_∞ − ω_p² / (ω² + iγω − β²k²). This expression predicts a longitudinal bulk plasmon at ω_L² = ω_p² + β²k² and modifies the transverse dispersion to ω² = c²k²/ε(ω, k). In the limit k → 0 the model recovers the Drude formula, but for wavevectors comparable to the Thomas‑Fermi screening wavevector k_TF the nonlocal term becomes dominant. Studies using electron‑energy‑loss spectroscopy (EELS) on thin metal films show that the dispersion of the surface plasmon polariton deviates from the local prediction when film thicknesses approach the nonlocal length scale. Typical exam topics include the derivation of the nonlocal boundary condition (the Feibelman d‑parameter) and the shift of the surface‑plasmon resonance in a nanosphere when βR/ω_p is non‑negligible. A common mistake is to apply the local Fresnel coefficients to structures whose feature size is smaller than the nonlocal length ℓ_nl = β/ω_p, leading to a significant overestimation of field enhancement.

Electromagnetic Radiation Reaction in the Quantum Regime: Quantum Radiation Reaction and the Landau‑Lifshitz Equation

The classical Abraham‑Lorentz force F_rad = (μ₀ q² / 6πc) d³x/dt³ suffers from runaway solutions and pre‑acceleration. The Landau‑Lifshitz reduction replaces the third derivative by the Lorentz force derivative, yielding F_LL = (μ₀ q² / 6πc) [ d/dt (q/m (E + v×B)) − (q/m)² (E + v×B)² v / c² ]. In the quantum regime the electron interacts with the quantized electromagnetic field; the radiation‑reaction effect appears as a correction to the electron’s self‑energy and as a stochastic force described by the quantum Langevin equation. The quantum radiation‑reaction parameter χ = (γ / E_S) √( (E + v×B)² − (v·E)² ) where E_S = m²c³ / (eħ) ≈ 1.3×10¹⁸ V/m governs the transition from classical to quantum emission. For χ ≪ 1 the Landau‑Lifshitz equation remains accurate; for χ ≳ 1 the emission becomes discrete, the electron recoil per photon is significant, and the radiation spectrum must be computed from the Baier‑Katkov operator method or from quantum electrodynamics (QED) using the Furry picture. Laser‑plasma experiments at intensities near 10²³ W/cm² have measured electron energy loss and observed a χ‑dependent suppression of radiated power (quantum quenching) consistent with quantum models. Examination questions may require the calculation of the average radiated power P = (2/3) (e²c / 4πε₀) γ⁴ (E² + B² − (v·E)²) in the classical limit, and the identification of the χ regime where quantum corrections become appreciable. A frequent error is to use the classical Larmor formula P = (e² a²) / (6πε₀ c³) for ultra‑relativistic electrons in a laser field without the γ⁴ enhancement, underestimating the loss by orders of magnitude.

Worked Example: Quantum Radiation‑Reaction Parameter
An electron with Lorentz factor γ = 2000 collides head‑on with a linearly polarised laser pulse of wavelength λ = 800 nm and peak intensity I = 5×10²² W/cm². Compute the quantum parameter χ and state whether quantum corrections are needed.
The laser electric field amplitude is E₀ = √(2I / (cε₀)) ≈ 6.1×10¹⁴ V/m. In the electron rest frame the field is boosted by γ, so the effective field is E' ≈ 2γE₀ ≈ 2.4×10¹⁸ V/m. The Schwinger field is E_S = 1.3×10¹⁸ V/m. Thus χ = E' / E_S ≈ 1.8. Since χ > 1, quantum recoil and discrete photon emission are significant; the classical Landau‑Lifshitz equation underestimates the energy loss and a QED‑based Monte‑Carlo simulation is required.
Exam‑Day Strategy
Memorise the definition of χ and the scaling χ ∝ γ√I λ. When a problem gives γ, I, and λ, compute χ in one line; if χ < 0.1 you may safely use the classical Landau‑Lifshitz force, otherwise state that quantum corrections are mandatory and cite the Baier‑Katkov spectrum.

Electromagnetic Energy‑Momentum Tensor in Material Media: Minkowski vs Abraham Controversy and Experimental Tests

The electromagnetic momentum density in a dielectric has two historically competing forms. The Minkowski tensor gives g_M = (1/c²) E×H = n S/c², where n is the refractive index and S the Poynting vector. The Abraham tensor yields g_A = (1/c²) E×H / n² = S/(n c²). The difference Δg = g_M − g_A = (n²−1) S/c² is interpreted as the mechanical momentum transferred to the medium. In a dispersive, lossless medium the total momentum (field + material) is conserved and both tensors can be made equivalent by adding the appropriate material momentum density. The controversy was tested by experiments measuring radiation pressure on a mirror immersed in a liquid (Jones‑Richards 1954) and the photon‑drag effect in semiconductors (Gibson et al. 1980). Modern cavity‑optomechanics experiments with high‑Q whispering‑gallery resonators have measured momentum transfer to mechanical modes, supporting the interpretation that the Minkowski momentum is the canonical momentum conjugate to the field coordinate, while the Abraham momentum corresponds to the kinetic momentum of the polaritons. Examination questions often ask to derive the force density f = ∇·T − ∂g/∂t for each tensor, to show that the difference integrates to the Abraham force F_A = (εμ−1)/c² d/dt ∫ E×H dV, and to explain why the measured displacement of a free‑floating dielectric slab in a pulsed laser field is consistent with the Abraham prediction when the slab’s centre‑of‑mass motion is tracked. A typical trap is to assume that the Minkowski momentum is “wrong” because it exceeds the vacuum momentum; the correct statement is that the extra momentum resides in the medium’s mass‑density wave.

Electromagnetic Wave Propagation in Gyrotropic Media: Faraday Rotation, Nonreciprocal Devices, and Magneto‑Optical Kerr Effect

Gyrotropic media are characterised by a permittivity tensor ε_ij = ε δ_ij + i ε_g ε_ijk B_k, where ε_g is the gyration coefficient proportional to the static magnetic bias B. For a wave propagating parallel to B the eigenmodes are circularly polarised with refractive indices n_± = √(ε ± ε_g). The phase‑velocity difference Δβ = (ω/c)(n_+ − n_-) produces Faraday rotation: the plane of linear polarisation rotates by θ_F = (Δβ L)/2 = (ω ε_g L)/(2c n). In the Voigt configuration (propagation perpendicular to B) the eigenmodes are linearly polarised and the medium exhibits birefringence (Cotton‑Mouton effect). Nonreciprocal devices such as optical isolators exploit the fact that the Faraday rotation is independent of propagation direction, whereas natural optical activity reverses sign. A typical isolator uses a 45° Faraday rotator sandwiched between crossed polarisers; forward light passes, backward light is blocked. The magneto‑optical Kerr effect (MOKE) measures the change in polarisation upon reflection from a magnetised surface; the complex Kerr angle Φ_K = θ_K + i ε_K is proportional to the off‑diagonal element of the reflected Jones matrix. In competitive examinations, candidates may be asked to derive the dispersion relation for the two circular eigenmodes from Maxwell’s equations with the gyrotropic tensor, to calculate the required magnetic field for a given rotation angle in a TGG crystal of known Verdet constant, or to explain why a Faraday isolator cannot be built with a reciprocal birefringent crystal alone. A common error is to confuse the Faraday rotation (nonreciprocal) with natural optical activity (reciprocal) and to apply the same Jones matrix for forward and backward propagation.

Electromagnetic Scattering by Periodic Gratings: Rigorous Coupled‑Wave Analysis and Wood's Anomalies

A diffraction grating with period Λ imposes a discrete set of spatial harmonics on the incident field. The rigorous coupled‑wave analysis (RCWA) expands the fields and the permittivity profile in Fourier series along the periodic direction, leading to a matrix eigenvalue problem for the modal amplitudes in each homogeneous layer. For a binary lamellar grating the Fourier coefficients are ε_m = (ε₁−ε₂) sinc(mπ f) where f is the fill factor. The S‑matrix formalism propagates the modal amplitudes through the stack, enforcing continuity of tangential E and H at each interface. Wood's anomalies appear when a diffracted order becomes tangent to the grating surface (grazing emergence), i.e. when the in‑plane wavevector component k_x,m = k₀ sinθ_i + 2πm/Λ equals ±k₀. At these wavelengths the diffraction efficiency shows sharp peaks or dips because the corresponding mode couples to a surface‑bound Bloch wave (guided‑mode resonance). In metallic gratings the anomalies are associated with surface‑plasmon polariton excitation; the condition is k_x,m = Re(k_sp) where k_sp = k₀ √(ε_m ε_d/(ε_m+ε_d)). Examination problems often require the construction of the RCWA matrix for a given grating profile, the identification of the Rayleigh wavelengths λ_R = Λ/(|m| ± sinθ_i), and the explanation of why the zeroth‑order transmission drops at a Wood anomaly (energy is redistributed into evanescent orders). A frequent mistake is to treat the grating as a thin phase mask and apply scalar diffraction theory when the groove depth is comparable to the wavelength, neglecting the vectorial coupling between TE and TM polarisations that RCWA captures.
