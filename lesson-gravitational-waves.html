Part 1

🌊 Gravitational Wave Theory

Linearized GR
The Wave Equation, the TT Gauge, Quadrupole Formula, and GW Polarizations
Linearized gravity: write the metric as g_{μν} = η_{μν} + h_{μν} with |h_{μν}| ≪ 1. In the Lorentz gauge ∂^μ h̄_{μν} = 0 where h̄_{μν} = h_{μν} − ½ η_{μν} h (the trace-reversed metric), the Einstein equations become the wave equation □ h̄_{μν} = −16πG T_{μν}/c⁴. In vacuum, □ h̄_{μν} = 0 with plane wave solutions h̄_{μν} = ε_{μν} e^{i k_σ x^σ}. The transverse-traceless (TT) gauge imposes h_{0μ} = 0, h^i_i = 0, and ∂^j h_{ij} = 0, leaving two independent polarizations: h⁺ and h^×. The plus polarization h⁺ stretches and squeezes along the x and y axes; the cross polarization h^× does the same at 45°. The quadrupole formula (Einstein, 1918): h̄_{ij}(t, r) = (2G/c⁴ r) d² I_{ij}(t − r/c)/dt² where I_{ij} = ∫ ρ x_i x_j d³x is the mass quadrupole moment. The energy flux in GWs: dE/dt = (G/5c⁵) ⟨Q⃛_{ij} Q⃛^{ij}⟩, where Q_{ij} = I_{ij} − ½ δ_{ij} I^k_k is the reduced quadrupole. The GW luminosity: L_GW ∼ (G/c⁵) (M² R⁴ ω⁶) for a binary with separation R and orbital frequency ω. The binary inspiral due to GW emission: dE/dt = −L_GW, leading to the shrinkage of the orbit: da/dt = −(64G³ m₁ m₂ (m₁+m₂))/(5c⁵ a³). The chirp mass M_c = (m₁ m₂)^{3/5}/(m₁+m₂)^{1/5} determines the GW frequency evolution: df/dt = (96π^{8/3}/5) (G M_c/c³)^{5/3} f^{11/3}. The characteristic strain h_c(f) ∼ (G M_c/c²)/D (π G M_c f/c³)^{2/3}.
Gravitational waves are ripples in spacetime generated by accelerating mass quadrupoles; the TT gauge reveals the two transverse polarizations h⁺ and h^× that stretch and squeeze space perpendicular to the propagation direction.
Part 2

🔭 LIGO and Interferometric Detectors

Detection
The LIGO Interferometer, Fabry-Pérot Cavities, Quantum Noise, and the First Detection GW150914
The LIGO detector is a modified Michelson interferometer with 4 km arms. A passing GW changes the arm lengths differentially: ΔL/L = h/2. Fabry-Pérot cavities in each arm (with finesse F ∼ 300) increase the effective arm length to ∼1200 km. The power recycling mirror between the laser and the beamsplitter reuses light not exiting toward the photodiode, increasing the circulating power to ∼700 W. The signal recycling mirror at the output port can tune the detector's frequency response. The strain sensitivity of Advanced LIGO: ∼10^{-23} / √Hz at 100 Hz. The noise sources: seismic noise at low frequencies (< 10 Hz) from ground motion, suppressed by multi-stage pendulums. Thermal noise from the suspension fibers and mirror coatings in the 10–50 Hz band. Quantum noise: shot noise dominates at high frequencies (h_SN ∝ 1/√P), and radiation pressure noise at low frequencies (h_RP ∝ √P), together giving the standard quantum limit (SQL). The squeezed light injection reduces quantum noise below the SQL. GW150914 (September 14, 2015): the first direct detection of GWs by LIGO Hanford and Livingston. The signal lasted ∼0.2 s, swept from 35 Hz to 250 Hz with peak strain h ∼ 3 × 10^{-21}. The source parameters: m₁ = 36^{+5}_{−4} M_⊙, m₂ = 29^{+4}_{−4} M_⊙, final mass M_f = 62^{+4}_{−4} M_⊙, radiated energy E_rad = 3.0^{+0.5}_{−0.5} M_⊙ c², distance D_L = 410^{+160}_{−180} Mpc. The sky localization of GW150914 was ∼600 deg² (from two detectors). The matched filtering technique correlates the data with template waveforms (IMR templates from numerical relativity and post-Newtonian theory). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR): ρ = ⟨s, h⟩/√⟨h, h⟩. Detection requires ρ > 8. The false alarm rate (FAR) for GW150914 was < 1 in 203,000 years.
LIGO's interferometers achieve attometer-level displacement sensitivity through Fabry-Pérot cavities, power recycling, and sophisticated noise mitigation — enabling the first direct observation of gravitational waves in 2015.

🔗 Binary Mergers and Numerical Relativity

Mergers
Post-Newtonian Theory, the Inspiral-Merger-Ringdown Waveform, Numerical Relativity, and the BSSN Formalism
The complete inspiral-merger-ringdown (IMR) waveform is modeled in three phases. Inspiral (orbital separation > 3 M): described by post-Newtonian (PN) theory. The 3.5PN phase evolution: d²f/dt² = (96π^{8/3}/5) (G M_c/c³)^{5/3} f^{11/3} × [1 + (…)× (PN corrections up to (v/c)^7)]. The stationary phase approximation gives the frequency-domain waveform h̃(f) ∝ f^{-7/6} exp(i Ψ(f)). Merger (from ∼3 M to the formation of a common horizon): requires numerical relativity. The BSSN (Baumgarte-Shapiro-Shibata-Nakamura) formalism rewrites the ADM equations in a strongly hyperbolic form: the evolution variables are the conformal metric γ̃_{ij} = e^{-4φ} γ_{ij}, the conformal factor φ, the trace of the extrinsic curvature K, the traceless conformal extrinsic curvature Ã_{ij}, and the conformal connection functions Γ̃^i. The moving puncture gauge (the 1+log lapse and the Gamma-driver shift) allows stable evolution of black hole spacetimes without excision. The puncture method: the initial data for black holes uses the Brill-Lindquist or Bowen-York solutions with punctures (coordinate singularities) at each BH location. The ringdown phase: the merged black hole rings down by emitting GWs at its quasinormal mode (QNM) frequencies ω_{ℓmn} = ω_{ℓm0} + i/τ_{ℓm}. The fundamental mode (ℓ=m=2) of a Kerr black hole has frequency f_QNM ≈ (c³/2πG M) [1 − 0.63(1−a)^{0.3}] and damping time τ ∼ 2π/Im(ω). The Kerr solution parameters: spin a = J/M² (0 ≤ a ≤ 1). The area theorem (Hawking): the horizon area A = 8π M² (1 + √{1−a²}) cannot decrease — for GW150914, A_final/A_initial > 1 was verified. The binary neutron star inspiral: the tidal deformability Λ = (2/3) k₂ (R/M)^5, where k₂ is the tidal Love number, imprints a phase shift in the GW waveform: δΨ_tidal ∝ Λ̃ (v/c)^{10} where Λ̃ is the effective tidal parameter. The equation of state (EOS) of nuclear matter is constrained by the measurement of Λ from GW170817: Λ(1.4 M_⊙) = 190^{+390}_{−120} (for the high-spin prior).
The IMR waveform combines post-Newtonian theory for the inspiral, numerical relativity for the merger, and black hole perturbation theory for the ringdown — the gravitational-wave signal encodes the masses, spins, and tidal properties of the merging compact objects.

🔭 Multimessenger Astronomy and Future Detectors

Multimessenger
GW170817, the Kilonova AT2017gfo, Standard Sirens, LISA, and Third-Generation Detectors
GW170817 (August 17, 2017): the first detected binary neutron star (BNS) merger by LIGO and Virgo. The signal lasted ∼100 s in the LIGO band (24–1784 Hz). The source was localized to ∼28 deg² (with three detectors). The electromagnetic counterpart AT2017gfo (a kilonova) was detected in the galaxy NGC 4993 at D_L ≈ 40 Mpc. The kilonova is powered by the radioactive decay of r-process elements (lanthanides and actinides) synthesized in the neutron-rich merger ejecta. The early blue component (∼1 day) is from lanthanide-poor ejecta; the later red component (∼1 week) from lanthanide-rich ejecta with high opacity. The gamma-ray burst GRB 170817A (detected 1.7 s after the merger) confirmed that BNS mergers produce short GRBs. The jet launched by the merger was observed off-axis, producing a structured afterglow. Standard siren: the GW luminosity distance D_L is directly measured from the waveform amplitude (h ∝ 1/D_L), independent of the cosmic distance ladder. Combined with the redshift from the host galaxy, GW170817 gave H₀ = 70^{+12}_{−8} km/s/Mpc — a measurement of the Hubble constant independent of the CMB and supernovae. The r-process nucleosynthesis: the merger ejecta (∼0.01 M_⊙) produce heavy elements via the rapid neutron capture process, explaining the cosmic abundances of elements like gold, platinum, and uranium. The LISA (Laser Interferometer Space Antenna): three spacecraft in a heliocentric orbit forming an equilateral triangle with sides 2.5 million km, sensitive to mHz GWs (0.1 mHz – 0.1 Hz). LISA will detect supermassive black hole mergers (10⁴–10⁷ M_⊙), galactic white dwarf binaries (verification binaries like J0806.3+1527), and extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs). The Einstein Telescope (ET) and Cosmic Explorer (CE): third-generation ground-based detectors with 10× better sensitivity than Advanced LIGO, extending the redshift reach to z ∼ 100 for BNS mergers. The pulsar timing arrays (PTAs) (NANOGrav, EPTA, PPTA) detect nHz GWs from supermassive black hole binaries (∼10⁸–10¹⁰ M_⊙) by measuring precision timing residuals of millisecond pulsars. The Hellings-Downs curve is the quadrupolar correlation pattern between pulsar pairs that confirms the GW origin of the signal. The stochastic GW background from the superposition of unresolved binary mergers can be detected through the Hellings-Downs cross-correlation.
GW170817 inaugurated multimessenger astronomy with gravitational waves, showing that BNS mergers produce kilonovae, short GRBs, and r-process elements; future detectors like LISA and the Einstein Telescope will probe GW sources across cosmic time.
