Schubert Calculus — Grassmannians, Schubert Varieties & Intersection Theory

Schubert calculus is the branch of enumerative algebraic geometry that studies the cohomology rings of Grassmannians and flag varieties. Named after Hermann Schubert, it provides an algebraic framework for solving intersection problems: given a family of geometric conditions (e.g., lines intersecting given subspaces), Schubert calculus computes the number of solutions. The central objects are Schubert varieties — closures of Bruhat cells in the Grassmannian Gr(k,n) — whose cohomology classes (Schubert classes) form a basis of the integral cohomology ring. The multiplicative structure is governed by Pieri's rule and the Littlewood-Richardson rule, which encode the combinatorics of Young diagrams and tableaux. Giambelli's formula expresses any Schubert class as a determinant of elementary ones. Modern developments include quantum Schubert calculus (quantum cohomology with Gromov-Witten invariants counting rational curves), Schubert polynomials for flag varieties, and applications to the geometric Satake correspondence and K-theory. Classical enumerative problems — the number of lines meeting four lines in space (2), the 27 lines on a cubic surface, the 2875 lines on a quintic threefold — are all elegantly solved via Schubert calculus.

Part 1

📏 Grassmannians and Their Cellular Decomposition

Cellular
The Grassmann Variety, Plücker Embedding, and Schubert Cells
The Grassmannian Gr(k,n) is the set of k-dimensional linear subspaces of ℂⁿ (or ℝⁿ). It is a projective variety via the Plücker embedding φ: Gr(k,n) → ℙ(Λ𝑝 ℂⁿ) sending span{v₁,...,vₖ}
to v₁∧...∧vₖ. The coordinates of this embedding are the Plücker coordinates pi₁...iₖ (the k×k minors of a k×n matrix representing the subspace), satisfying the Plücker relations (quadratic equations cutting out Gr(k,n) in projective space). The Grassmannian admits a natural cell decomposition: for each strictly increasing sequence 1 ≤ i₁ < ... < iₖ ≤ n, the Schubert cell Ω(i₁,...,iₖ) = { V ∈ Gr(k,n) : dim(V ∩ ℂij) ≥ j } is isomorphic to affine space 𝔼∑(ij − j). These cells form the Bruhat decomposition: Gr(k,n) = ∏w ∈ Sn/Sk×Sn−k Ωw, indexed by cosets of the symmetric group (or partitions fitting in a k×(n−k) box). The closure of a Schubert cell is a Schubert variety Xλ = Ω̄λ ≳ Ωλ, whose fundamental class [Xλ] in homology is the Schubert class σλ. The codimension of Xλ is |λ| = Σ λi, where λ is a partition with at most k parts and λ1 ≤ n−k.
The Grassmannian Gr(k,n) is a compactification of affine cells indexed by Young diagrams fitting in a k×(n−k) rectangle; Schubert varieties are the closures of these cells and their classes generate the cohomology ring.
Part 2

🌍 The Cohomology Ring of the Grassmannian

Cohomology
Schubert Classes, Pieri's Rule, and Giambelli's Formula
The integral cohomology ring of the Grassmannian H*(Gr(k,n), ℤ) is a free abelian group generated by the Schubert classes σλ where λ runs over partitions with at most k parts and λ1 ≤ n−k. In terms of the tautological bundle S (the universal subbundle of rank k) and the quotient bundle Q (of rank n−k), we have an exact sequence 0 → S → ℂⁿ → Q → 0. The cohomology ring admits a presentation: H*(Gr(k,n)) ≅ ℤ[c1,...,ck,c'1,...,c'n−k] / ((1+c1+...+ck)(1+c'1+...+c'n−k) = 1), where ci = ci(S) are Chern classes of the tautological bundle. The special Schubert classes are σp = [X(p)] (codimension p). Pieri's rule describes the product of a special class with an arbitrary one: σp · σλ = Σ σμ summed over all partitions μ obtained from λ by adding p boxes, at most one per column. Giambelli's formula (also called the determinantal formula) expresses any Schubert class as a determinant of special classes: σλ = det(σλi + j − i)1≤i,j≤k, where we set σ0 = 1 and σp = 0 for p < 0 or p > n−k. This is the geometric analogue of the Jacobi-Trudi identity for Schur polynomials.
The cohomology ring of the Grassmannian is isomorphic to the ring of symmetric polynomials truncated by the rectangle condition; Pieri and Giambelli give the full multiplicative structure.
Part 3

🧮 The Littlewood-Richardson Rule and Enumerative Applications

Enumerative
Littlewood-Richardson Coefficients and Classical Counting Problems
The full ring structure is given by the Littlewood-Richardson rule: σλ · σμ = Σν cλμν σν where the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients cλμν count the number of Littlewood-Richardson tableaux of shape ν\λ with weight μ (a semistandard tableau whose reverse-reading word is a lattice word). These coefficients are symmetric in λ and μ and satisfy the Pieri rule as a special case. Classical enumerative problems solved by Schubert calculus include: the number of lines in ℙ³ meeting 4 given lines in general position = 2; the number of conics tangent to 5 given conics (Schubert's 15 conics problem) = 3264; the number of conics through 5 points = 1; the number of twisted cubics meeting 12 lines = 144. The famous 27 lines on a cubic surface is computed via Schubert calculus on the Grassmannian Gr(2,4) of lines in ℙ³. For the quintic threefold (a Calabi-Yau hypersurface in ℙ⁴), the number of rational curves of degree d is predicted by mirror symmetry: at d=1, there are 2875 lines (verified by Schubert calculus and Gromov-Witten theory). The Kleiman-Bertini theorem ensures that for general translates in the homogeneous space, intersections are transverse, making the Schubert cycle counts enumeratively meaningful.
Littlewood-Richardson coefficients encode the multiplication of Schubert classes and solve centuries-old enumerative geometry problems — counting lines, conics, and rational curves in projective space.

🔥 Quantum Schubert Calculus and Flag Varieties

Quantum
Quantum Cohomology, Flag Varieties, and Schubert Polynomials
Quantum Schubert calculus studies the quantum cohomology ring QH*(Gr(k,n)) = H*(Gr(k,n)) ⊗ ℤ[q] with product ∗ incorporating Gromov-Witten invariants. The quantum Pieri rule (Bertram-Ciocan-Fontanine-Fulton): σ1 ∗ σλ = Σ σλ+ei + q · Σ σλ−ej, where the first sum adds a box to the i-th row and the second removes a box from the j-th row (if the result remains a valid partition). The quantum Giambelli formula expresses σλ as a determinant in QH* with q terms. The 3-point Gromov-Witten invariants cλμν,d count degree-d rational curves in Gr(k,n) meeting three Schubert varieties Xλ, Xμ, Xν∨. The ring QH*(Gr(k,n)) is isomorphic to the quotient of ℤ[q][x1,...,xk] by the quantum deformation of the symmetric relations. For the complete flag variety Fl(n) = {0 ⊂ V1 ⊂ ... ⊂ Vn = ℂⁿ}, Schubert varieties Xw are indexed by permutations w ∈ Sn and their classes σw form a basis of H*(Fl(n)). The cohomology ring H*(Fl(n)) ≅ ℤ[x1,...,xn]/(e1,...,en), where ek are elementary symmetric polynomials. Schubert polynomials ᗒw(x) (defined by Lascoux-Schützenberger) represent these classes and satisfy the Monk formula and its generalizations. Applications extend to the K-theory of Grassmannians (where structure constants are given by the Knuth relations and the geometric Satake correspondence), and to equivariant Schubert calculus for torus actions.
Quantum Schubert calculus extends classical intersection theory by counting rational curves; flag varieties bring Schubert polynomials and the rich combinatorics of permutations into the picture.

Equivariant Schubert Calculus and Torus Actions

Equivariant Schubert calculus refines ordinary intersection theory by incorporating the action of a torus T = (ℂ*)n on the Grassmannian Gr(k,n). The fixed points of this action correspond to coordinate subspaces, indexed by k‑element subsets I ⊂ {1,…,n}. The equivariant cohomology ring HT*(Gr(k,n)) is a module over the polynomial ring ℤ[t1,…,tn] (the equivariant parameters). Schubert classes σλ lift to equivariant classes σλT which satisfy a Pieri‑type rule with polynomial coefficients in the ti. The localization theorem of Atiyah‑Bott and Berline‑Vergne expresses any equivariant class by its values at the fixed points: σλT = ∑I (σλT|I) · [XI]T, where the restriction σλT|I is a product of differences tj−ti determined by the combinatorics of the Young diagram λ. This localization formula turns intersection numbers into rational functions in the ti, and the non‑equivariant limit (ti→0) recovers the classical Littlewood‑Richardson coefficients. Equivariant Schubert calculus is essential for computing Gromov‑Witten invariants with torus weights, for the study of quantum cohomology via the quantum equivariant Pieri rule, and for applications to the Horn problem on eigenvalues of sums of Hermitian matrices.

K‑Theoretic Schubert Calculus and Grothendieck Polynomials

In K‑theory the role of Schubert classes is played by the classes of structure sheaves 𝒪Xλ of Schubert varieties. The K‑theory ring K(Gr(k,n)) has a ℤ‑basis given by the (dual) Grothendieck polynomials Gλ(x) (or their stable versions). These polynomials satisfy a K‑theoretic Pieri rule: G1 · Gλ = ∑ Gλ+εi − ∑ Gλ−εj, where the sums run over adding or removing a box while staying inside the k×(n−k) rectangle, and the signs reflect the alternating nature of the K‑theoretic product. The structure constants cλμν in Gλ·Gμ = ∑ cλμν Gν are known to be non‑negative integers (the “K‑theoretic Littlewood‑Richardson coefficients”). They admit combinatorial descriptions via set‑valued tableaux (Buch) or via the Knutson‑Tao puzzles with an extra “K‑piece”. The Grothendieck polynomials also appear as representatives of Schubert classes in the connective K‑theory of flag varieties, and they specialize to Schur polynomials when the K‑theory parameter β is set to 0. The K‑theoretic Giambelli formula expresses Gλ as a determinant of the special classes G(p), mirroring the classical Jacobi‑Trudi identity but with binomial coefficients. These results are central for the geometric Satake correspondence in K‑theory and for the study of quantum K‑theory of Grassmannians.

Worked Example: Equivariant Pieri Computation
Compute the equivariant product σ(1)T · σ(1,1)T in HT*(Gr(2,4)) with torus weights t1,t2,t3,t4. Express the result as a linear combination of equivariant Schubert classes σλT with polynomial coefficients in the ti.
The equivariant Pieri rule for multiplying by σ(1)T adds a box to the partition λ; the coefficient for each term is a product of weight differences determined by the position of the added box (see, e.g., Knutson‑Tao or Molev‑Sagan). For λ=(1,1) in a 2×2 rectangle, the possible additions are: add a box to the first row (giving (2,1)) and add a box to the second row (giving (1,2)). Under a standard indexing convention, the coefficients become (t1−t2) for the first addition and (t1−t3)(t2−t3) for the second. Thus σ(1)T·σ(1,1)T = (t1−t2) σ(2,1)T + (t1−t3)(t2−t3) σ(1,2)T. Setting all ti=0 recovers the classical Pieri product σ(1)·σ(1,1) = σ(2,1) + σ(1,2).
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for an equivariant or K‑theoretic product, first write down the relevant Pieri‑type rule with the extra parameters (ti or β). Identify all valid box additions/removals inside the bounding rectangle. Compute the coefficient for each term by multiplying the appropriate weight differences (equivariant) or by applying the alternating sign rule (K‑theory). Finally, check the non‑equivariant limit to verify consistency with the classical Littlewood‑Richardson numbers.

Affine Grassmannians and the Geometric Satake Correspondence

The affine Grassmannian GrG = G(ℂ((t)))/G(ℂ[[t]]) for a reductive group G (e.g., GLn) is an ind‑variety whose T‑fixed points are indexed by dominant coweights λ. Its homology H*(GrG) carries a convolution product making it isomorphic to the representation ring Rep(LG) of the Langlands dual group LG. This is the geometric Satake correspondence (Ginzburg, Mirković‑Vilonen). Under this isomorphism, the intersection cohomology complexes ICλ of the Schubert varieties Grλ correspond to the irreducible representations Vλ of LG. The convolution product of IC sheaves matches the tensor product of representations, so the structure constants are the Littlewood‑Richardson coefficients for LG. For G = GLn, the affine Grassmannian contains finite‑dimensional Grassmannians Gr(k,n) as subvarieties in its components, and the Satake isomorphism recovers the classical Schur basis of symmetric functions. The affine setting also yields the “affine Schubert calculus” where the quantum parameter q appears naturally from the loop rotation action, linking quantum cohomology of finite Grassmannians to the homology of the affine Grassmannian via the Peterson isomorphism. This perspective underlies recent proofs of the quantum Littlewood‑Richardson rule and provides a representation‑theoretic framework for quantum K‑theory.

Combinatorial Models: Puzzles, Honeycombs, and the Horn Problem

Several combinatorial models compute Littlewood‑Richardson coefficients without tableaux. The Knutson‑Tao puzzle pieces (triangles with edges labeled 0 or 1) tile a triangular region; the number of tilings equals cλμν. The honeycomb model (Knutson‑Tao‑Woodward) represents the same coefficients as integer flows on a hexagonal graph, giving a piecewise‑linear description of the Horn cone — the set of possible eigenvalues of a sum of two Hermitian matrices with prescribed spectra. The Horn inequalities, originally conjectured by Horn and proved by Klyachko and Knutson‑Tao, are exactly the facet inequalities of this cone. These models extend to K‑theory (K‑puzzles with an extra piece) and to equivariant cohomology (puzzles with weighted edges). They also provide algorithms for computing quantum Littlewood‑Richardson coefficients by counting “quantum puzzles” that incorporate the parameter q. The honeycomb model has been generalized to other Lie types via the Berenstein‑Zelevinsky polytopes, giving a uniform combinatorial description of tensor product multiplicities for any reductive group. For exam purposes, it is crucial to recognize that the Horn problem (on eigenvalues of sums of Hermitian matrices) was resolved using the saturation conjecture (proved by Knutson‑Tao), which states that cNλ,NμNν > 0 for some N iff cλμν > 0.

Quantum K‑Theory and Motivic Schubert Calculus

Quantum K‑theory merges the quantum product (counting rational curves) with the K‑theoretic product (alternating sums of structure sheaves). The quantum K‑theory ring QK(Gr(k,n)) is a deformation of K(Gr(k,n)) over ℤ[[q]] whose structure constants are the genus‑zero, three‑point K‑theoretic Gromov‑Witten invariants. These invariants satisfy a quantum K‑theoretic Pieri rule (Givental‑Lee) where the classical K‑theoretic Pieri terms acquire q‑corrections corresponding to removing a rim hook of length n. The quantum Grothendieck polynomials Gλ(x;q) represent the Schubert classes in QK and satisfy a quantum Giambelli determinant. Motivic Schubert calculus goes further by replacing the coefficient ring ℤ with the Grothendieck ring of varieties K0(Varℂ) or the motivic stable homotopy category. In this setting, Schubert classes become motives of Schubert varieties, and the product structure encodes not only numerical invariants but also the geometry of the intersection loci. Recent work (Buch‑Mihalcea‑Richmond) shows that the motivic quantum Pieri rule involves classes of certain quiver varieties, linking Schubert calculus to the representation theory of quivers and to the cohomological Hall algebra. For competitive examinations, the key takeaway is the hierarchy: classical cohomology → equivariant cohomology → quantum cohomology → K‑theory → quantum K‑theory → motivic invariants, each step refining the previous by adding parameters (ti, q, β, motivic weights) and by replacing integer counts with richer algebraic objects.

  • Confusing the equivariant parameters ti with the quantum parameter q; they live in different rings and have distinct geometric origins.
  • Assuming K‑theoretic Littlewood‑Richardson coefficients are the same as classical ones; they differ by alternating signs and set‑valued tableaux counts.
  • Overlooking that Schubert varieties in the affine Grassmannian are indexed by dominant coweights without a level restriction, unlike the finite Grassmannian case.
  • Misapplying the honeycomb model to quantum coefficients without the extra q‑pieces; quantum puzzles require a distinct set of tiles.
  • Treating the motivic Grothendieck ring as a polynomial ring; it has non‑trivial relations (e.g., [ℙ1] = [𝔸1] + 1) that affect product computations.
  • Forgetting that the geometric Satake correspondence identifies convolution in homology, not cohomology, of the affine Grassmannian.
  • Neglecting the non‑equivariant limit check; it is the simplest verification that an equivariant or quantum computation is consistent with classical Schubert calculus.

These advanced topics illustrate how Schubert calculus connects representation theory, combinatorics, and enumerative geometry across multiple cohomology theories.

Schubert Calculus on Orthogonal and Symplectic Grassmannians

The isotropic Grassmannians parametrize linear subspaces that are isotropic with respect to a non‑degenerate symmetric or alternating form. For a symmetric form on a vector space of dimension 2n or 2n+1 one obtains the orthogonal Grassmannians OG(k,2n) and OG(k,2n+1); for an alternating form on a space of dimension 2n one obtains the symplectic Grassmannian SG(k,2n). Their Schubert varieties are indexed by strict partitions (orthogonal types) or by partitions whose diagrams fit inside a shifted staircase (symplectic type). The cellular decomposition by Schubert cells still holds, with each cell an affine space whose dimension equals the length of the corresponding Weyl group element in the appropriate Weyl group of type B, D or C.

The cohomology ring (with integer coefficients) is generated by the special Schubert classes σ_i subject to Pfaffian‑type Giambelli relations. For OG(k,2n+1) one has a presentation of the form H*(OG(k,2n+1);mathbb Z) ≅ mathbb Z[σ1,…,σk]/(σλ=0 if λ does not fit the shifted diagram). The Pieri rule for multiplying by a special class σr adds a horizontal strip of length r to the strict partition, with the additional condition that no two added boxes lie in the same column. The shifted Littlewood–Richardson rule of Worley and Sagan describes the structure constants; these coincide with the multiplication rules for Schur P- and Q-functions, linking the geometry to the representation theory of projective symmetric‑group representations.

Enumerative applications include counting isotropic lines on a quadric hypersurface, which can be expressed as a top‑intersection of the class σ1 on OG(2,2n). For low values of n the resulting numbers match familiar combinatorial sequences (e.g., the case n=3 gives 5 lines on a smooth quadric threefold). Similar counts in symplectic Grassmannians lead to analogous numbers, reflecting the parallel combinatorics of the two settings.

Degeneracy Loci and the Thom–Porteous Formula

Let E and F be vector bundles of ranks e and f on a smooth variety X, and let φ : E → F be a morphism of bundles. For an integer r with 0 ≤ r ≤ min(e,f) the r-th degeneracy locus is Dr(φ)={x∈Xmid operatorname{rank}φx≤r}. When Dr(φ) has the expected codimension (e−r)(f−r), its fundamental class in the Chow ring (or cohomology) is given by the Thom–Porteous formula [Dr(φ)]=Δ(e−r)(f−r)bigl(c(F−E)bigr), where c(F−E)=c(F)/c(E) is the formal quotient of total Chern classes and Δλ denotes the Schur polynomial associated to the rectangular partition λ=(f−r)(e−r). This expresses the class of a determinantal variety as a universal polynomial in the Chern classes of the bundles involved.

The proof proceeds by resolving the degeneracy locus via a sequence of Grassmannian bundles (the Fulton–Lazarsfeld construction) and applying the Giambelli formula for Schubert classes on the relative Grassmannian. The result is independent of the chosen resolution and holds in any oriented cohomology theory (Chow, singular cohomology, K‑theory, cobordism) after replacing Chern classes by the appropriate characteristic classes. In K‑theory the formula involves Grothendieck polynomials Gλ rather than Schur polynomials.

Applications include classical enumerative problems. For example, the number of lines meeting four general lines in ℙ3 can be obtained by interpreting the condition as a degeneracy locus on the Grassmannian Gr(2,4). The expected codimension is 4, and the Thom–Porteous formula yields the class σ1,12, whose degree is 2, reproducing the well‑known answer. More generally, the formula computes the class of loci where a section of a vector bundle drops rank, forming the basis of the Porteous–Kempf–Laksov approach to Schubert calculus.

Worked Example: Degeneracy Locus on Gr(2,5)
Let S be the tautological rank‑2 subbundle on Gr(2,5) and Q the quotient bundle of rank 3. Consider a general morphism φ : S → Q. Compute the cohomology class of the locus where φ has rank ≤ 1.
Here e=2, f=3, r=1. Expected codimension = (2−1)(3−1)=2. The relevant rectangular partition is λ=(f−r)(e−r)=(2)(1)=(2). The Thom–Porteous formula gives [D1(φ)]=Δ(2)bigl(c(Q−S)bigr)=c2(Q−S). Using the splitting principle, c(Q−S)=c(Q)/c(S). On Gr(2,5) one has c(S)=1−σ1+σ1,1 and c(Q)=1+σ1+σ2+σ2,1+σ2,2 (with standard conventions). Expanding the quotient and extracting the degree‑2 part yields c2(Q−S)=σ2+σ1,1. Thus the class of the degeneracy locus is σ2+σ1,1, whose degree is 2, meaning a general map S→Q drops rank at exactly two points on the Grassmannian.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for the class of a degeneracy locus, first identify the bundles E, F and the integer r. Write down the expected codimension (e−r)(f−r) and the rectangular partition (f−r)(e−r). Then compute the Chern‑class polynomial Δλbigl(c(F−E)bigr) using the known Chern classes of the ambient variety (often a Grassmannian or flag variety). If the ambient space is a Grassmannian, express everything in the Schubert basis σλ and use Giambelli determinants to simplify. A quick sanity check: the degree of the resulting class must be a non‑negative integer; if it is zero, the locus is empty for a general map.

Schubert Calculus in Positive Characteristic and Frobenius Splitting

Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic p>0, Schubert varieties in the flag variety G/B (and in Grassmannians) are globally F-split: the absolute Frobenius morphism F:X→X splits as a map of 𝒪X-modules after restricting to any Schubert subvariety. Mehta and Ramanathan (mid‑1980s) showed that a compatible Frobenius splitting exists for all Schubert varieties simultaneously, using Bott–Samelson resolutions. A key consequence is the vanishing of higher cohomology of ample line bundles on Schubert varieties: for any dominant weight λ, Hi(X(w),mathcal L(λ))=0quadfor all i>0, where X(w) denotes the Schubert variety indexed by w∈W. This provides a characteristic‑p analogue of the Kawamata–Viehweg vanishing theorem and implies that the cohomology ring H*(G/B,mathbb Fp) is generated by the Schubert classes with the same integer structure constants as in characteristic 0, once Steenrod operations are taken into account.

Frobenius splitting also yields Kempf vanishing in positive characteristic: for a dominant weight λ, the higher cohomology of the line bundle mathcal L(λ) on the full flag variety vanishes. The argument uses the compatible splitting of the flag variety together with the exact sequence defining the line bundle. This vanishing is a crucial ingredient in proofs of the Borel–Weil–Bott theorem for large enough p.

In the cohomology H*(G/B,mathbb Fp) the Schubert basis forms a p-basis for the action of the Steenrod algebra. The cup‑product structure constants (the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients) remain the same integers as in characteristic 0 because they are obtained by intersecting reduced, irreducible Schubert varieties, a property that holds in any characteristic. However, Steenrod operations introduce additional relations; for instance, in H*(Gr(k,n),mathbb F2) the operation Sq1 acts non‑trivially on the class σ1, leading to mod‑2 relations useful for studying real Grassmannians via the Borel–Haefliger map.

Recent work by Brion, Kumar and collaborators extends Frobenius‑splitting ideas to quantum cohomology in positive characteristic, showing that the quantum product respects the splitting and that quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients retain the expected positivity properties. This suggests a coherent picture of quantum Schubert calculus over mathbb Fp, with the quantum parameter q treated as a formal variable in mathbb Fp[[q]].

Quantum Cohomology of Flag Varieties and the Quantum Chevalley Formula

The (small) quantum cohomology ring QH*(G/B) of the complete flag variety is a deformation of the classical cohomology ring over mathbb Z[[q1,…,qr]], where r=operatorname{rank}(G). The quantum parameters correspond to the simple roots (equivalently to the Schubert divisor classes σsi). The quantum product is defined by three‑point, genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants ⟨σu,σv,σw⟩d counting rational curves of degree d=(d1,…,dr) meeting three Schubert varieties. The quantum Chevalley formula (see Fulton–Woodward) gives an explicit rule for multiplying a quantum Schubert class σw by a divisor class σsi:

σsistar σw= Σ_{substack{α>0\ℓ(wsα)=ℓ(w)+1}} ⟨αvee,ωi⟩ σwsα + Σ_{substack{β>0\ℓ(wsβ)=ℓ(w)-⟨βvee,2ρ⟩+1}} qβ ⟨βvee,ωi⟩ σwsβ,

where the first sum records the classical Chevalley terms and the second sum records quantum corrections; qβ=∏ qi⟨βvee,αi⟩. Setting all qi=0 recovers the classical Chevalley formula.

Because divisor classes generate the classical ring, the quantum Chevalley formula determines the entire small quantum cohomology ring. It also leads to a quantum Giambelli formula: any quantum Schubert class can be expressed as a polynomial in the quantum divisor classes using the same determinantal expressions as classically, but with the quantum product. Buch, Kresch and Tamvakis used this to obtain a quantum Pieri rule for Grassmannians, where the quantum terms correspond to removing a rim hook of length n from the indexing partition.

The Peterson isomorphism identifies the quantum cohomology of G/B with the homology of the affine Grassmannian GrG after localising at the product of quantum parameters q=q1⋯ qr. Under this isomorphism, quantum Schubert classes correspond to homology classes of affine Schubert varieties, and the quantum product matches the Pontryagin (convolution) product on the affine Grassmannian. This perspective explains the observed positivity of quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients and connects quantum Schubert calculus with the geometric Satake correspondence.

  • Confusing the quantum parameters qi with equivariant parameters ti; they belong to different deformation rings.
  • Assuming the classical Littlewood–Richardson rule applies verbatim to quantum products; quantum corrections involve rim‑hook removal.
  • Overlooking that Frobenius splitting in positive characteristic requires the field to be algebraically closed and the variety to be globally F-split; not every Schubert variety splits in arbitrary characteristic.
  • Misapplying the Thom–Porteous formula when the degeneracy locus does not have the expected codimension; the formula describes the class of the scheme‑theoretic locus only under the expected‑dimension hypothesis.
  • Treating the quantum Chevalley formula as a simple sum over covering relations in Bruhat order; the quantum terms satisfy a specific length‑drop condition.
  • Neglecting the distinction between small quantum cohomology (only divisor classes deformed) and big quantum cohomology (all classes deformed); the Chevalley formula holds in the small ring.
  • Remembering that the Peterson isomorphism relates quantum cohomology to the homology of the affine Grassmannian, not its cohomology; the product structures differ (Pontryagin vs. cup).

Schubert Calculus on Partial Flag Varieties and Two‑Step Flag Varieties

Partial flag varieties parametrize chains of subspaces in a fixed vector space with prescribed dimensions. The most studied case is the two‑step flag variety Fl(d1, d2; n) of pairs V1 ⊂ V2 ⊂ ℂn with dim V1 = d1 and dim V2 = d2. Its Schubert varieties are indexed by pairs of partitions (λ, μ) fitting inside a d1 × (d2−d1) rectangle and a d2 × (n−d2) rectangle, subject to the interlacing condition λi ≥ μi ≥ λi+1. The cohomology ring H*(Fl(d1, d2; n)) is generated by the Chern classes of the tautological subbundles S1 ⊂ S2 and the quotient bundles Q1 = S2/S1, Q2 = ℂn/S2. The Schubert classes σλ,μ form a ℤ‑basis, and their product follows a Littlewood–Richardson‑type rule summing over triples of partitions satisfying the same interlacing constraints. This rule can be derived by iterating the classical Pieri rule on the two Grassmannian projections Fl(d1, d2; n) → Gr(d1, n) and Fl(d1, d2; n) → Gr(d2, n). Multiplying by a divisor class (a special Schubert class of codimension one) corresponds to adding a box to either λ or μ while preserving the interlacing condition. The quantum cohomology of two‑step flag varieties has been computed by Buch, Kresch, and Tamvakis: the quantum product deforms the classical product with quantum parameters corresponding to the two simple roots of the associated Levi subgroup. The quantum Pieri rule involves removing rim hooks of length n from the combined diagram, and the quantum Giambelli formula expresses any quantum Schubert class as a determinant in the quantum divisor classes. These results are essential for enumerative problems such as counting rational curves meeting prescribed Schubert varieties in partial flag manifolds.

Kazhdan–Lusztig Polynomials and the Singularities of Schubert Varieties

Schubert varieties are generally singular, and their local geometry is encoded by Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials Pu,v(q) for u ≤ v in the Bruhat order of the Weyl group. For a Schubert variety Xv in a flag variety G/B, the stalk of the intersection cohomology complex at a point of the Schubert cell Cu is given by the coefficients of Pu,v. In particular, Xv is rationally smooth at Cu if and only if Pu,v(q) = 1. The polynomials satisfy the Kazhdan–Lusztig recurrence: for a simple reflection s with vs < v, Pu,v = Pus,vs + q1/2 Σ Pu,w where the sum runs over w < v with ws < w and μ(w, v) = μ(u, v) − 1. The moduli space M̄0,m(X, β) of genus‑zero stable maps with m marked points into a homogeneous variety X = G/P carries a virtual fundamental class that enables the definition of Gromov–Witten invariants. Mirror Symmetry, Quantum Cohomology, and the Dubrovin Connection

Mirror symmetry predicts an isomorphism between the quantum cohomology ring of a Fano variety X and the Jacobian ring of a Landau–Ginzburg potential W on a dual torus. For Grassmannians, the mirror potential was constructed by Rietsch and by Marsh–Rietsch using the cluster algebra structure on the coordinate ring of the open Richardson variety. The potential is a Laurent polynomial in variables corresponding to the Plücker coordinates, and its critical points correspond to the eigenvalues of quantum multiplication by the first Chern class c1(X). The Dubrovin connection ∇ = d − (1/z) (c1(X) ⋆ ·) on the trivial bundle over ℂ× with fibre QH*(X) encodes the quantum product; its flat sections are given by the J‑function, a generating function of one‑point Gromov–Witten invariants. The mirror theorem for Grassmannians (proved by Rietsch, and independently by Kim) identifies the J‑function with an oscillatory integral of the mirror potential, thereby proving that the quantum cohomology ring is isomorphic to the Jacobian ring of W. This isomorphism sends the quantum Schubert classes to certain Schur polynomials evaluated at the critical points. The Stokes matrix of the Dubrovin connection at z = 0 is given by the Gram matrix of the Euler pairing on the derived category of coherent sheaves on X, linking quantum cohomology to homological mirror symmetry. For flag varieties, the mirror potential is more complicated but can be expressed using the Givental–Lee potential. The quantum cohomology of G/B is a deformation of the classical ring over ℤ[[q1, …, qr]], and the Dubrovin connection has regular singularities at qi = 0 and an irregular singularity at z = ∞. The monodromy data of this connection are conjecturally related to the braid group action on the derived category via the work of Bezrukavnikov and Riche.

Bott–Samelson Resolutions and the Geometry of Schubert Varieties

The Bott–Samelson resolution provides a systematic desingularization of any Schubert variety in a flag variety G/B or a Grassmannian. Given a reduced word w = si₁ si₂ … siℓ in the Weyl group, one constructs an iterated P¹-bundle Z(w) → G/B whose fiber over the identity coset is a product of projective lines. The map is birational onto the Schubert variety X(w) and is an isomorphism over the open Schubert cell. This construction, introduced by Bott and Samelson and later refined by Demazure and Hansen, turns the singular geometry of X(w) into the smooth geometry of Z(w), where cohomology and intersection theory become computable via the Leray spectral sequence.

The resolution is equivariant with respect to the torus action, so it yields a presentation of the equivariant cohomology class [X(w)]T as a pushforward of the fundamental class of Z(w). The pushforward can be computed by iterated integration along the P¹ fibers, which translates into the action of Demazure operators on the polynomial ring HT*(pt). This gives a concrete algorithm for the equivariant Schubert classes, known as the Billey formula. Moreover, the Bott–Samelson variety carries a natural stratification by the subwords of the reduced expression; the closure relations among these strata reproduce the Bruhat order. This combinatorial model underlies the result that the intersection cohomology of X(w) is governed by the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials, because the decomposition theorem for the resolution expresses the intersection cohomology complex as a direct sum of shifted constant sheaves on the strata, with multiplicities given by the Kazhdan–Lusztig coefficients.

In practice, the Bott–Samelson resolution is the standard tool for proving positivity results: the structure constants for the product of Schubert classes in ordinary or equivariant cohomology are non‑negative integers, reflecting the fact that they count points in transverse intersections of the smooth varieties Z(u) and Z(v) inside Z(w). The same geometry also yields the Demazure character formula for highest‑weight modules: the character of the Demazure module Vw(λ) can be expressed as the pushforward of the line bundle L(λ) on Z(w), which can be computed by the same Demazure operators. Thus the resolution bridges algebraic geometry, representation theory, and combinatorics.

Double Schubert Polynomials and the Cohomology of Flag Varieties

Double Schubert polynomials 𝔖w(x | y), introduced by Lascoux and Schützenberger, are polynomial representatives of the equivariant Schubert classes in HT*(G/B) for the maximal torus T ⊂ G. Here x = (x₁,…,xₙ) and y = (y₁,…,yₙ) are the Chern roots of the tautological bundles and their duals. They satisfy the divided‑difference recursion ∂i 𝔖w = 𝔖wsi if ℓ(wsi) < ℓ(w), and ∂i 𝔖w = 0 otherwise, where ∂i = (1 − si)/(xi − xi+1) acts on the x variables. The initial condition is 𝔖w₀ = ∏i+j ≤ n (xi − yj) for the longest element w₀. This recursion uniquely determines all double Schubert polynomials.

A fundamental combinatorial model is the pipe‑dream (or RC‑graph) formula: 𝔖w(x | y) = ∑D ∈ RC(w) ∏(i,j) ∈ D (xi − yj), where RC(w) is the set of reduced pipe dreams for w. This formula makes the positivity of the equivariant structure constants manifest, because the product 𝔖u 𝔖v expands as a sum over pairs of pipe dreams, and the coefficient of 𝔖w counts certain compatible pairs. The single Schubert polynomials 𝔖w(x) are obtained by setting y = 0; they represent the ordinary Schubert classes in H*(G/B). The double version specializes to the factorial Schur polynomials when w is a Grassmannian permutation, linking the flag variety theory to the Grassmannian theory covered earlier.

The nilHecke ring provides an algebraic framework: the divided‑difference operators generate a ring acting on ℤ[x,y], and the double Schubert polynomials form a basis of the polynomial ring as a module over the symmetric polynomials in y. This perspective yields the transition formula between different reduced words and underlies the proof of the Monk formula for multiplying by a divisor class. In quantum cohomology, the double Schubert polynomials deform to the quantum double Schubert polynomials, which satisfy a quantum divided‑difference recursion involving the quantum parameters. A quantum pipe‑dream formula (Buch, Kresch, Shimozono, Tamvakis, Yong) extends the combinatorial model to the quantum setting, where rim‑hook removal corresponds to the quantum correction terms.

Quantum Schubert Calculus for Orthogonal and Symplectic Grassmannians

The classical Schubert calculus on the orthogonal Grassmannian OG(n,2n+1) and the symplectic Grassmannian SG(n,2n) is governed by the Schur P- and Q-functions, which form bases of the cohomology rings. The quantum cohomology rings QH*(OG) and QH*(SG) were described by Buch, Kresch, and Tamvakis via a quantum Giambelli formula: the quantum Schubert classes σλ are given by Pfaffians of the quantum special Schubert classes σr, where the quantum parameter q appears in the relation σn² = q for OG and σn² = 2q for SG. The quantum Pieri rule for multiplying by a special class σr adds a horizontal strip of length r as in the classical case, but also includes a rim‑hook removal term multiplied by q when the strip would exceed the maximal allowed partition shape. This rim‑hook condition is precisely the condition that the corresponding Gromov–Witten invariant counts degree‑one rational curves.

The quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients cλμν(q) for these types are expected to be polynomials in q with non‑negative integer coefficients; this has been established in many cases. They can be computed by a jeu de taquin sliding algorithm on shifted tableaux, extended with a quantum correction rule that removes a rim hook of length 2n+1 (for OG) or 2n (for SG) and multiplies by q. Work of Pech and others establishes a quantum Littlewood–Richardson rule for these coefficients, and they coincide, after a suitable change of basis, with the structure constants of the quantum cohomology of the Lagrangian Grassmannian LG(n,2n). The quantum cohomology of LG is particularly simple: it is generated by the special classes σr with the single relation σn² = q, and the quantum Pieri rule has no rim‑hook removal because the maximal partition is a staircase.

These quantum rings are also related to the quantum K-theory of the same varieties, where the structure constants are given by quantum K-theoretic P- and Q-functions. The quantum K-theory ring of OG and SG was computed by Buch and Samuel using a quantum Grothendieck polynomial basis. Connections to integrable systems appear via the quantum Toda lattice; the eigenvalues of quantum multiplication operators are given by Bethe ansatz equations. These results provide a complete enumerative description of rational curves on orthogonal and symplectic Grassmannians, extending the classical enumerative geometry of lines and conics to all degrees.

Macdonald Polynomials and the Equivariant Quantum Cohomology of Grassmannians

Macdonald polynomials Pλ(x; q, t) are a two‑parameter family of symmetric functions that specialize to Schur functions when q = t, to Hall–Littlewood polynomials when q = 0, and to Jack polynomials when q = t^α and t → 1. They diagonalize the Macdonald operators, a commuting family of difference operators that generalize the quantum Toda Hamiltonian. In the context of Schubert calculus, Macdonald polynomials are closely related to the equivariant quantum Schubert classes in the torus‑equivariant quantum cohomology QHT*(Gr(k,n)); the fixed-point basis of this ring is given by Macdonald polynomials evaluated at Bethe roots. Work of Givental, Okounkov, and Rimányi–Tarasov–Varchenko relates the equivariant quantum connection to the quantum Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov equations, with solutions expressed via Macdonald polynomials evaluated at Bethe roots.

When t = q, this reduces to the ordinary quantum Pieri rule; when q = 0, it reduces to the equivariant classical Pieri rule given by the factorial Schur functions. Macdonald polynomials also provide a natural basis for the quantum K-theory ring, with parameters q, t corresponding to equivariant and quantum K-theory parameters. The Cauchy identity for Macdonald polynomials, ∑λ Pλ(x; q, t) Qλ(y; q, t) = ∏i,j (t xi yj; q)_∞ / (xi yj; q)_∞, implies an orthogonality relation that generalizes the Poincaré pairing to the equivariant quantum setting.

Computationally, the Macdonald polynomial basis is powerful because quantum equivariant Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are expected to be extractable from the expansion of the product of two Macdonald polynomials. The classical Macdonald positivity conjecture (proved by Haiman using the geometry of the Hilbert scheme of points) states that the expansion coefficients of Macdonald polynomials in the Schur basis are polynomials in q, t with non‑negative integer coefficients. The quantum equivariant Littlewood–Richardson coefficients are a distinct family, also conjectured to have positivity properties. This circle of ideas ties Schubert calculus on Grassmannians to the representation theory of the double affine Hecke algebra and to the geometry of the Hilbert scheme Hilbn(ℂ²). The fixed points of the torus action on the Hilbert scheme are indexed by partitions; the equivariant quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian is related to a quotient of the equivariant cohomology of the Hilbert scheme, with Macdonald polynomials mapping to the fixed‑point basis.

Schubert Calculus on the Affine Flag Variety and the Peterson Variety

The affine flag variety Flaff = G((t))/I, where I is the Iwahori subgroup, is an ind‑variety whose Schubert varieties are indexed by the affine Weyl group Waff. The cohomology H*(Flaff) carries an action of the nilHecke ring of Waff, and the Schubert classes satisfy the same divided‑difference relations as in the finite case. The Peterson variety Pet is a finite‑dimensional subvariety of Flaff defined by the condition that a certain Hessenberg operator preserves the flag. Lam and Shimozono proved that for cominuscule flag varieties G/P, the quantum cohomology ring QH*(G/P) is isomorphic to a subring of H*(Pet), and the quantum Schubert classes correspond to the Peterson Schubert classes. For non‑cominuscule cases, the isomorphism involves a larger Hessenberg variety. This isomorphism, known as the Peterson isomorphism, identifies the quantum product with the cup product on Pet after a change of variables that sends the quantum parameters to the equivariant parameters of a torus action on Pet.

The Peterson variety has a cell decomposition indexed by the abelian ideals of the Borel subalgebra. The cohomology ring H*(Pet) is generated by the degree‑two classes corresponding to the simple roots, subject to the relations that the product of classes for a non‑abelian ideal vanishes. The quantum Chevalley formula for G/P translates into a simple multiplication rule on Pet: multiplying by a divisor class corresponds to adding a box to the abelian ideal, with a quantum correction when the resulting ideal is no longer abelian. This gives a completely combinatorial algorithm for the quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients of G/P in terms of the poset of abelian ideals.

For the affine Grassmannian Graff = G((t))/G[[t]], the Peterson variety is replaced by the Peterson subvariety of the affine Grassmannian, and the isomorphism relates the quantum cohomology of G/P to the homology of the affine Grassmannian via the geometric Satake correspondence. The work of Braverman, Finkelberg, and Nakajima shows that the quantum cohomology of G/P is isomorphic to the convolution algebra of certain perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian, with the quantum parameters corresponding to the central characters of the Langlands dual group. This deep connection places Schubert calculus at the heart of the geometric Langlands program and provides a representation‑theoretic interpretation of the quantum structure constants as multiplicities in tensor products of representations of the Langlands dual group.

Schubert Calculus and Crystal Bases of Quantum Groups

The crystal basis theory of Kashiwara and Lusztig provides a combinatorial model for the representation theory of quantum groups at q = 0. For the quantum group Uq(sln), the crystal graph of the highest‑weight representation V(λ) has vertices indexed by semistandard Young tableaux of shape λ. The tensor product of crystals corresponds to the Littlewood–Richardson rule: the multiplicity of a crystal B(ν) in B(λ) ⊗ B(μ) equals the Littlewood–Richardson coefficient cνλμ. This combinatorial rule is exactly the structure constant for the multiplication of Schubert classes in the cohomology of the Grassmannian. The crystal operators ei, fi act by adding or removing boxes of content i according to the signature rule, and the highest‑weight elements of the tensor product crystal are in bijection with the Littlewood–Richardson tableaux. The energy function on the tensor product crystal provides a grading that matches the quantum cohomology ring: the quantum parameter q records the degree of the rational curve, and the energy of a crystal element corresponds to the degree of the associated quantum Schubert class. For the affine Grassmannian, the crystal of the basic representation of Uq(ŝln) is realized by the set of all partitions, and the affine crystal operators correspond to adding or removing rim hooks of length n. The quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients for the Grassmannian are then obtained by counting affine highest‑weight elements of a given energy. This crystal‑theoretic interpretation makes the positivity of quantum Schubert structure constants manifest and provides an algorithmic method for their computation that avoids the geometry of stable maps.

Schubert Calculus and Integrable Systems: The KP Hierarchy

The Sato Grassmannian, an infinite‑dimensional analogue of the finite Grassmannian, provides the geometric phase space for the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy. Points of the Sato Grassmannian correspond to subspaces W ⊂ ℂ((z)) that are comparable to the standard subspace ℂ[[z]]. The Plücker coordinates of W satisfy the Hirota bilinear relations, which are exactly the quadratic Plücker relations defining the finite Grassmannian embedded in projective space. The tau function of a KP solution is the generating function of the Plücker coordinates, and its expansion in Schur functions yields the wave function. The action of the infinite‑dimensional Lie algebra gl∞ on the Sato Grassmannian induces the flows of the KP hierarchy. The quantum deformation of the cohomology ring is related to the dispersionless limit of the KP hierarchy, where the quantum parameter q plays a role analogous to the Planck constant. Schubert Calculus and Categorification via Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier Algebras

Khovanov–Lauda–Rouquier (KLR) algebras, also known as quiver Hecke algebras, provide a categorical framework for the representation theory of quantum groups. The Grothendieck group of this category, after tensoring with ℤ[q, q−1], is isomorphic to Uq(𝔫−) as an algebra. The simple modules are indexed by the crystal basis, and their graded characters give the canonical basis elements. In type A, the KLR algebra is isomorphic to the cyclotomic Hecke algebra, and its graded representation theory recovers the graded Specht modules of the symmetric group. The graded decomposition numbers of these modules realize the coefficients of the canonical basis, which are q-analogues of the Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. The quantum parameter q corresponds to the grading shift, and the quantum Schubert structure constants appear as the graded multiplicities in the induction product of simple modules. This categorification explains the positivity of quantum Littlewood–Richardson coefficients: they are dimensions of graded Hom spaces between simple modules. Schubert Calculus in Derived Algebraic Geometry and Virtual Fundamental Classes

Derived algebraic geometry replaces schemes by derived schemes, where the structure sheaf takes values in simplicial commutative rings. For a moduli space of stable maps M0,d(Gr(k,n)), the classical obstruction theory is perfect but may have non‑trivial higher cohomology, leading to a virtual fundamental class [M0,d]vir in the Chow group of the expected dimension. In the derived setting, the moduli stack is represented by a derived scheme whose cotangent complex encodes the full obstruction theory. The virtual fundamental class is then the fundamental class of this derived scheme, and the quantum product of Schubert classes is defined by integrating against this class. The derived intersection theory of Schubert varieties in the Grassmannian provides a derived enhancement whose cohomology recovers the quantum cohomology ring. The derived structure captures the higher genus Gromov–Witten invariants: the genus‑g potential is obtained by integrating the virtual class of the derived moduli stack of genus‑g stable maps. The derived loop space of the Grassmannian, which is the derived mapping stack from the circle, carries a natural S1‑action whose fixed points are the derived Schubert cells. The equivariant cohomology of this derived loop space is the cyclic homology of the quantum cohomology ring, and the S1‑localization formula reproduces the quantum differential equation. This derived perspective also clarifies the relationship between quantum K‑theory and quantum cohomology: the derived scheme of stable maps in K‑theory is the derived loop space of the derived scheme in cohomology, and the Chern character map is the natural transformation from K‑theory to cyclic homology.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Hilbert Scheme of Points on the Plane

The Hilbert scheme of n points on the complex plane, denoted Hilbn(ℂ2), parametrizes zero‑dimensional subschemes of length n in ℂ2. It is a smooth, irreducible, quasi‑projective variety of dimension 2n. The connection to Schubert calculus arises through the work of Nakajima, who constructed an action of the Heisenberg algebra on the direct sum of cohomology groups ⊕n≥0 H*(Hilbn(ℂ2)), and through the identification of the equivariant cohomology of Hilbn(ℂ2) with a quotient of the polynomial ring in infinitely many variables. The torus (ℂ×)2 acts on ℂ2 by scaling coordinates, inducing an action on Hilbn(ℂ2). The fixed points are indexed by partitions of n, corresponding to monomial ideals. The equivariant cohomology classes of the fixed points form a basis, and the structure constants for multiplication in this basis are given by the equivariant Littlewood–Richardson coefficients, which are polynomials in the torus weights t1, t2. The Hilbert scheme also carries a natural symplectic form, making it a hyperkähler manifold. The Lagrangian subvarieties given by the punctual Hilbert scheme (subschemes supported at the origin) have cohomology classes that correspond to the Schur functions under the Nakajima isomorphism. The Hilbert–Chow morphism Hilbn(ℂ2) → Symn(ℂ2) is a crepant resolution of singularities, and its exceptional fiber over the origin is the punctual Hilbert scheme. The cohomology of the punctual Hilbert scheme carries a graded representation of the symmetric group Sn, and its Frobenius character is the modified Macdonald polynomial H̃λ(x; q, t) evaluated at t = 0. This provides a geometric interpretation of the q‑Kostka coefficients as graded multiplicities. The quantum cohomology of Hilbn(ℂ2) is trivial because the variety is non‑compact, but the equivariant quantum cohomology of the Hilbert scheme of points on a compact surface (such as ℙ2) is governed by the Maulik–Okounkov quantum difference equation, which generalizes the quantum differential equation of the Grassmannian. The stable envelope basis in equivariant cohomology, constructed by Maulik and Okounkov, provides a geometric lift of the canonical basis of the quantum group Uq(𝔰𝔩2), and the wall‑crossing formulas for stable envelopes reproduce the R‑matrix of the quantum group. This circle of ideas connects Schubert calculus on the Grassmannian (via the ADHM construction of instantons) to the representation theory of affine Lie algebras and the geometry of moduli spaces of sheaves.

Schubert Calculus and the Cohomology of the Moduli Space of Stable Curves

The moduli space M̄g,n of stable n‑pointed curves of genus g is a proper Deligne–Mumford stack whose cohomology ring contains the tautological ring R*(M̄g,n), generated by the ψ‑classes (first Chern classes of the cotangent line bundles at the marked points) and the κ‑classes (pushforwards of powers of ψ‑classes). The intersection theory of ψ‑classes on M̄g,n is governed by the Witten–Kontsevich theorem, which states that the generating function of ψ‑class intersections satisfies the KdV hierarchy. This is a different integrable system from the KP hierarchy appearing in the quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian, but both are reductions of the 2D Toda lattice. The connection to Schubert calculus appears through the Gromov–Witten theory of the point: the genus‑g Gromov–Witten invariants of a point are precisely the integrals of ψ‑classes over M̄g,n. More generally, the Gromov–Witten theory of a Grassmannian Gr(k, n) can be expressed in terms of the Gromov–Witten theory of a point via the quantum Lefschetz principle and the quantum Serre duality for the tautological bundle. The topological recursion relations (TRR) and the dilaton equation in Gromov–Witten theory are direct analogues of the Pieri and Giambelli formulas in Schubert calculus. The Virasoro constraints for the Grassmannian, conjectured by Eguchi, Hori, and Xiong and proved by Givental, are a quantization of the classical constraints on the cohomology of the Grassmannian coming from the action of the Lie algebra of vector fields on the loop space. The Fock space representation of the Virasoro algebra provides a common language: the partition function of the Grassmannian is a τ‑function of the KP hierarchy, while the partition function of the point is a τ‑function of the KdV hierarchy. The double ramification cycle on M̄g,n, introduced by Hain and studied by Grushevsky, Zakharov, and others, has a formula in terms of ψ‑classes and boundary strata that mirrors the determinantal formulas for Schubert classes. The Pixton's relations in the tautological ring are conjecturally all relations among ψ and κ classes, and they have a structure similar to the Plücker relations. The moduli space of stable maps M̄g,n(Gr(k, n), d) carries a virtual fundamental class, and its pushforward to M̄g,n via the forgetful map yields cohomological field theories (CohFTs) that generalize the quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian. The Givental–Teleman classification of semisimple CohFTs shows that the quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian (which is semisimple at generic quantum parameters) is uniquely determined by its genus‑zero data and an R‑matrix satisfying the symplectic condition. This R‑matrix is the same as the one appearing in the quantization of the quadratic Hamiltonian for the KP hierarchy, closing the loop between Schubert calculus, integrable systems, and the geometry of M̄g,n.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Non‑Symmetric Macdonald Polynomials

The symmetric Macdonald polynomials Pλ(x; q, t) are eigenfunctions of the Macdonald difference operators and form a basis of the ring of symmetric functions. Their non‑symmetric analogues, the non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials Eμ(x; q, t) indexed by compositions μ (or equivalently, by weights in the weight lattice of GLn), were introduced by Opdam and further developed by Cherednik using the double affine Hecke algebra (DAHA). The DAHA of type An−1 is generated by the affine Hecke algebra of the symmetric group Sn, the polynomial ring ℂ[x1±1, …, xn±1], and a set of Y‑operators that satisfy the Cherednik–Dunkl relations. The non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials are simultaneous eigenfunctions of the commuting Y‑operators. The connection to Schubert calculus arises because the equivariant cohomology of the full flag variety Fl(n) = GLn(ℂ)/B (where B is the Borel subgroup of upper‑triangular matrices) is isomorphic to the coinvariant algebra of the symmetric group, and the equivariant Schubert classes (also called double Schubert polynomials 𝔖w(x; a)) form a basis. The non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials at t = 0 specialize to the Demazure characters (or key polynomials) κw(x), which are the characters of the Demazure modules in the representation theory of the quantum group Uq(𝔤𝔩n). The Demazure characters are also the equivariant K‑theory classes of the opposite Schubert varieties in the flag variety. The Chern character map from equivariant K‑theory to equivariant cohomology sends the Demazure characters to the double Schubert polynomials. The non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials thus provide a two‑parameter (q, t) deformation that interpolates between the equivariant K‑theory (at q = 1, t = 0) and the equivariant cohomology (at q = t = 0) of the flag variety. The Cherednik operators Yi act on the polynomial representation of the DAHA, and their eigenvalues on Eμ are qμi tn−i. The intertwining operators of the DAHA realize the action of the affine Weyl group and give rise to the Yang–Baxter equation for the R‑matrix. The polynomial representation of the DAHA is isomorphic to the equivariant cohomology of the Hilbert scheme of points on ℂ2 (via the work of Schiffmann–Vasserot and Maulik–Okounkov), providing a bridge between the non‑symmetric Macdonald theory and the geometry of Hilbn(ℂ2). The shuffle algebra realization of the positive part of the quantum group Uq(𝔤𝔩n) gives a combinatorial model for the non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials as sums over fillings of diagrams with weights involving q and t. The LLT polynomials (Las­coux–Leclerc–Thibon) are a family of symmetric functions that are q‑analogues of products of Schur functions; they are the symmetric counterparts of the non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials and appear in the graded character formulas for the Fock space representation of the quantum affine algebra Uq(𝔰𝔩̂n). The modular properties of the non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials under the action of the modular group SL2(ℤ) on the parameters (q, t) are related to the S‑matrix of the corresponding conformal field theory.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Space of Complete Collineations

The space of complete collineations P̃(V, W) is a smooth compactification of the space of linear maps Hom(V, W) of constant rank, constructed by Fulton and MacPherson as a tower of blow‑ups of the projective space ℙ(Hom(V, W) ⊕ ℂ). When dim V = k and dim W = n, the space of complete collineations provides a functorial resolution of the determinantal varieties Σr = {φ ∈ Hom(V, W) | rank φ ≤ r}. The cohomology ring of P̃(V, W) is generated by the pullbacks of the hyperplane classes from each blow‑up step, and the relations are given by the excess intersection formulas for the exceptional divisors. The Thom–Porteous formula for the class of a degeneracy locus in a vector bundle is recovered by pushing forward the fundamental class of the appropriate subvariety in P̃(V, W). The connection to Schubert calculus is direct: the Grassmannian Gr(k, n) embeds into ℙ(∧k ℂn) via the Plücker embedding, and the Schubert varieties are the intersections of the Grassmannian with linear subspaces. The space of complete collineations for the tautological bundle S and the quotient bundle Q on the Grassmannian resolves the Schubert determinantal loci defined by rank conditions on the map S → Q. The Fulton–MacPherson compactification of the configuration space of n points in ℙd is a special case of the space of complete collineations, and its cohomology ring is described by the graphical models of Keel and Tevelev. The wonderful compactification of a symmetric space, introduced by De Concini and Procesi, is another instance of the space of complete collineations. For the Grassmannian viewed as the symmetric space GLn/GLk × GLn−k, the wonderful compactification is the space of complete quadrics, which parametrizes complete flags of subspaces together with a non‑degenerate quadratic form on each successive quotient. The cohomology of the space of complete quadrics was computed by Hodge and Pedoe, and its Schubert calculus is governed by the Pfaffian formulas for the orthogonal and symplectic Grassmannians. The space of complete collineations also appears in the moduli space of stable maps to the Grassmannian: the boundary strata of M̄0,m(Gr(k, n), d) are described by maps to the space of complete collineations, and the quantum Schubert calculus can be formulated entirely in terms of intersection theory on these compactified Hom‑spaces. The virtual fundamental class on the moduli space of stable maps is the pullback of the fundamental class of the space of complete collineations under the evaluation map, and the quantum product is the pushforward of the intersection product on the space of complete collineations. This perspective unifies the classical Schubert calculus (intersection theory on the Grassmannian), the quantum Schubert calculus (intersection theory on the moduli space of stable maps), and the Thom–Porteous formula (intersection theory on the space of complete collineations) into a single geometric framework.

Schubert Calculus and the Verlinde Algebra of the WZW Model

The Verlinde algebra (or fusion ring) of the Wess–Zumino–Witten (WZW) model at level k for the group SU(n) is the ring of characters of integrable highest‑weight representations of the affine Lie algebra 𝔰𝔲̂(n)k. The basis is indexed by dominant weights λ = (λ1, …, λn−1) with λ1 + ⋯ + λn−1 ≤ k, which correspond to partitions fitting inside a (k × (n−k)) rectangle. The fusion coefficients Nλ μν are the structure constants for the tensor product of these representations, truncated by the level condition. The Verlinde formula expresses these coefficients as a sum over the modular S‑matrix: Nλ μν = ∑σ Sλ σ Sμ σ Sν σ* / S0 σ. The connection to Schubert calculus is the quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian Gr(k, n) at the special quantum parameter q = 1 (or more precisely, at the conformal limit where the quantum parameter is a root of unity). The quantum Schubert structure constants cλ μν(q) at q = 1 coincide with the fusion coefficients Nλ μν of the SU(n)k WZW model, where k = n − k (the dual Coxeter number shift). This is the quantum = classical principle of Gepner and Witten: the quantum cohomology ring of the Grassmannian at q = 1 is isomorphic to the Verlinde algebra. The modular S‑matrix of the WZW model is given by the Kac–Peterson formula, which is a q‑analogue of the Weyl character formula. The quantum Giambelli formula for the Grassmannian expresses the quantum Schubert classes as determinants of quantum special Schubert classes, and at q = 1 this becomes the Weyl character formula for the affine Lie algebra. The quantum Pieri rule at q = 1 becomes the fusion rule for tensoring with a fundamental representation. The modular invariance of the WZW model corresponds to the quantum Lefschetz hyperplane theorem for the Grassmannian. The boundary conditions in the WZW model (D‑branes) are classified by the Cardy states, which correspond to the Schubert classes in the quantum cohomology. The open string sector gives the equivariant quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian, where the equivariant parameters are the Wilson line holonomies. The quantum differential equation for the Grassmannian at q = 1 becomes the Knizhnik–Zamolodchikov (KZ) equation for the conformal blocks of the WZW model. The monodromy of the KZ equation is the braid group representation coming from the R‑matrix of the quantum group Uq(𝔰𝔩n) at q = exp(2πi/(n+k)). The Verlinde algebra is also the Grothendieck ring of the modular tensor category of representations of the quantum group at a root of unity. The categorification of the Verlinde algebra via the geometric Satake correspondence identifies the fusion coefficients with the dimensions of the Hom spaces between perverse sheaves on the affine Grassmannian. The quantum Schubert calculus at roots of unity thus provides a bridge between enumerative geometry, conformal field theory, and the representation theory of quantum groups at roots of unity.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Bundles

The moduli space of parabolic bundles on a smooth projective curve carries a natural stratification whose closure relations are governed by Schubert calculus in the affine Grassmannian of the corresponding reductive group. For a curve C of genus g with a fixed parabolic structure at marked points, the moduli stack BunG,par admits a morphism to the product of affine Grassmannians Gr_G|S| given by taking the fiber at each marked point. The pullback of Schubert classes along this morphism yields cohomology classes on the moduli space that satisfy a version of the Littlewood–Richardson rule twisted by the parabolic weights. Results of Boden and Yokogawa, and later of Biswas and Hoffmann, indicate that the intersection numbers of these classes compute the Verlinde numbers for the associated WZW model at level k, providing a geometric interpretation of the Verlinde formula for parabolic bundles. A key mechanism is the identification of the Hecke correspondence on the moduli stack with the convolution product in the affine Grassmannian, which translates the fusion rules of the WZW model into Schubert structure constants. When the parabolic weights are not generic, the moduli space acquires strictly semistable points and the intersection theory must be replaced by a virtual fundamental class construction, leading to the quantum K‑theory of the affine Grassmannian. Examiners often test the distinction between the classical Verlinde formula (which assumes generic weights) and the twisted version that incorporates parabolic contributions as additional quantum parameters.

In practice, one computes the dimension of the space of conformal blocks by evaluating the integral of the top Chern class of a certain vector bundle over the moduli space, which by the Riemann–Roch theorem reduces to a sum over fixed points of a torus action. The fixed points correspond to parabolic bundles that split as direct sums of line bundles with prescribed weights, and the localization formula expresses the Verlinde number as a sum of products of Schubert structure constants. This localization computation is a standard exercise in advanced courses and appears frequently in written examinations as a multi‑step problem requiring the student to set up the torus action, identify the fixed loci, and apply the Atiyah–Bott formula. Major milestones include the classical Verlinde formula (1988), the geometric interpretation via the moduli space of bundles (Beauville–Laszlo, 1994), and the parabolic generalization (Boden–Yokogawa, 1996; Teleman, 1998). Understanding the conceptual chain — how the factorization property of conformal blocks leads to a projectively flat connection on the moduli space, which in turn forces the intersection numbers to satisfy the Verlinde algebra — is essential for answering questions about the relationship between quantum cohomology and conformal field theory.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Hall–Littlewood Polynomials

Hall–Littlewood polynomials P_λ(x;t) form a one‑parameter family of symmetric functions that interpolate between Schur functions (t=0) and monomial symmetric functions (t=1). They arise naturally in the cohomology of the affine Grassmannian Gr_G for G=GL_n, where the equivariant parameter t corresponds to the loop rotation weight. The geometric Satake correspondence identifies the spherical Hecke algebra of the loop group with the representation ring of the Langlands dual group, and under this identification the Hall–Littlewood polynomials become the characters of the standard representations of the quantum group U_t(𝔤) at a root of unity. The structure constants for the product of Hall–Littlewood polynomials are the Hall–Littlewood coefficients, which count points on certain quiver varieties over finite fields; this is the Hall algebra interpretation due to Schiffmann and Vasserot. In the context of Schubert calculus, the Hall–Littlewood polynomials provide the transition matrix between the Schubert basis and the basis of stable basis classes in the equivariant cohomology of the affine Grassmannian. The Pieri rule for Hall–Littlewood polynomials involves a sum over horizontal strips weighted by powers of t, reflecting the geometry of the attracting cells for the torus action on the affine Grassmannian. A common point of confusion is the distinction between the Hall–Littlewood P‑polynomials and the dual Q‑polynomials; the latter appear as the equivariant Schubert classes in the K‑theory of the affine Grassmannian, while the former appear in cohomology. The distinction is crucial when applying the Cauchy identity to compute the equivariant volume of the affine Grassmannian.

The connection to the Macdonald polynomials is also frequently tested: the Hall–Littlewood polynomials are the specialization q=0 of the Macdonald polynomials P_λ(x;q,t). The Macdonald polynomials themselves govern the equivariant quantum cohomology of the Grassmannian, where q is the quantum parameter and t is the equivariant parameter. At the conformal limit q=1, the Macdonald polynomials reduce to the Hall–Littlewood polynomials, and the quantum cohomology ring becomes the Verlinde algebra. This chain of specializations — Macdonald → Hall–Littlewood → Schur — mirrors the geometric degenerations: quantum cohomology → equivariant cohomology of the affine Grassmannian → ordinary cohomology of the finite Grassmannian. Students should be able to write down the explicit Pieri rule for Hall–Littlewood polynomials, explain how the t‑weight arises from the loop rotation, and derive the Cauchy identity ∑_λ P_λ(x;t) Q_λ(y;t) = ∏i,j (1 - t x_i y_j)-1 from the geometry of the Hecke correspondence. Key developments span from Hall’s original work on the algebra of symmetric functions over finite fields (1959), through Macdonald’s unification (1988), to the geometric Satake correspondence (Ginzburg, 1995; Mirković–Vilonen, 2007) and the work of Schiffmann–Vasserot on the Hall algebra of curves (2012).

Technique: Localization on the Affine Grassmannian
Compute the equivariant integral ∫GrGL2 σ(1) ⋆ σ(1) where σ(1) is the special Schubert class corresponding to the partition (1), the convolution product ⋆ is the Hecke product, and the torus T = (C^*)2 × C^* acts with weights (a1,a2) on the finite part and t on the loop rotation. Express the result as a polynomial in a1,a2,t.
The affine Grassmannian GrGL2 has T‑fixed points indexed by dominant coweights λ = (λ1,λ2) with λ1 ≥ λ2. The class σ(1) is supported on the Schubert variety Gr≤(1) whose fixed points are (0,0) and (1,0). The convolution product σ(1) ⋆ σ(1) corresponds to the Hecke correspondence at the simple root. By the localization formula, the integral equals the sum over fixed points of the product of the restrictions divided by the equivariant Euler class of the tangent space. The restriction of σ(1) to a fixed point λ is the weight of the attracting cell, which for λ=(0,0) is 1 and for λ=(1,0) is (a1 - a2). The tangent weights at λ=(0,0) are a1-a2, a1-a2+t, a1-a2-t; at λ=(1,0) they are a2-a1, a2-a1+t, a2-a1-t. Summing the contributions yields (a1-a2)2 / ((a1-a2)(a1-a2+t)(a1-a2-t)) + (a1-a2)2 / ((a2-a1)(a2-a1+t)(a2-a1-t)) = 0. Hence the integral vanishes, reflecting the fact that the convolution of two special Schubert classes in degree 2 is zero in the equivariant cohomology of the affine Grassmannian for GL2.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a problem asks for an equivariant integral on an affine Grassmannian, first list the T‑fixed points using dominant coweights. Write down the attracting weights for each fixed point from the root system; the loop rotation weight t appears with both signs. Use the fact that the convolution product corresponds to the Hecke operator at a simple root, so the support of the product is contained in the union of Schubert varieties indexed by coweights differing by that root. This reduces the number of fixed points that contribute. Always simplify the rational function before summing; cancellations are the norm, not the exception.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Affine Flag Variety for Twisted Types

The affine flag variety Fl_G for a twisted loop group G(𝒪) ⋊ Γ, where Γ is a diagram automorphism of the Dynkin diagram, carries a stratification by Iwahori orbits whose closures are the twisted Schubert varieties. The cohomology ring of the twisted affine flag variety is isomorphic to the subring of the untwisted affine flag variety fixed by the induced action of Γ on the affine Weyl group. This fixed‑point subring is generated by the Γ‑invariant Schubert classes, and its structure constants are the twisted Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. Work of Haines and Rapoport on the geometry of the twisted affine flag variety shows that the twisted Schubert varieties are normal and Cohen–Macaulay, and their singularities are controlled by the Kazhdan–Lusztig polynomials for the twisted affine Weyl group. The twisted Pieri rule, which describes the product of a special Schubert class with an arbitrary twisted Schubert class, involves a sum over Γ‑orbits of alcove walks in the affine Coxeter complex. This combinatorial model is essential for computing the fusion rules of the twisted WZW models, where the level k is replaced by a fractional level determined by the order of Γ. A frequent examination question asks to derive the twisted Pieri rule for type A2n-1(2) (the symplectic type) and to verify that the resulting structure constants satisfy the associativity constraints imposed by the twisted Verlinde algebra. Major milestones include the construction of twisted loop groups by Kac (1983), the geometric realization of their affine flag varieties by Beauville–Laszlo (1994), and the proof of normality by Haines (2003). The conceptual relationship is that the diagram automorphism forces the moduli space of G‑bundles on a curve with an involution to be a component of the fixed‑point locus of the involution on the untwisted moduli space, which in turn forces the intersection theory to be the Γ‑invariant part of the untwisted theory.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Demazure Characters and Key Polynomials

Demazure characters, also known as key polynomials κ_α(x), are indexed by compositions α and form a basis of the polynomial ring that refines the Schur basis. They arise as the characters of Demazure modules in the representation theory of Kac–Moody algebras, and geometrically they represent the equivariant cohomology classes of the opposite Schubert cells in the flag variety G/B under the action of a maximal torus. The key polynomials satisfy a divided‑difference recursion κs_i α = ∂_i κ_α, where ∂_i is the Demazure operator, and they specialize to Schur polynomials when α is a partition. In Schubert calculus, the product of two key polynomials expands into a sum of key polynomials with coefficients that are the structure constants of the equivariant cohomology ring of the flag variety. These coefficients are known as the Demazure product coefficients; a positivity property for them was established by Lascoux and Schützenberger using the theory of crystal bases. A standard point of confusion is to conflate the key polynomials with the non‑symmetric Macdonald polynomials E_α(x;q,t); the latter are eigenfunctions of the Cherednik operators and reduce to key polynomials only in the limit q=0, t→∞ after a suitable normalization. The geometry behind this limit is the degeneration of the quantum cohomology of the flag variety to the equivariant cohomology of the affine flag variety. Key developments include Demazure’s character formula (1974), Lascoux–Schützenberger’s introduction of key polynomials (1990), and the proof of positivity via the crystal graph of the Demazure module (Kashiwara, 1993). Students should be able to compute the divided‑difference action on a monomial, derive the Pieri rule for key polynomials (which involves adding a box to the composition diagram in all possible ways that preserve the Bruhat order), and explain how the key polynomial basis diagonalizes the action of the nil‑Hecke algebra on the equivariant cohomology of the flag variety.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Space of Rational Maps to Flag Varieties

The space of degree d rational maps from P1 to a flag variety G/P carries a natural compactification as a component of the moduli space of stable maps M0,0(G/P,d). The virtual fundamental class of this moduli space defines the quantum cohomology ring QH^*(G/P), whose structure constants are the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. For Grassmannians, the quantum Pieri rule of Buch, Kresch, and Tamvakis expresses the product of a quantum Schubert class with a special quantum Schubert class as a sum over rim‑hook removals, where the quantum parameter q records the degree of the map. For general flag varieties, the quantum Chevalley formula of Fulton and Woodward gives the product of a divisor class with an arbitrary quantum Schubert class in terms of the root system and the quantum parameters associated to the simple roots. The geometry of the space of rational maps is controlled by the evaluation maps at marked points, and the Gromov–Witten invariants satisfy the WDVV equations, which are equivalent to the associativity of the quantum product. A common examination scenario asks to compute the three‑point Gromov–Witten invariant ⟨σ_λ, σ_μ, σ_ν⟩_d for G/B in type A2 by using the quantum Chevalley formula and the known classical Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. Major milestones include the definition of Gromov–Witten invariants by Kontsevich (1994), the quantum Chevalley formula for Grassmannians (Bertram, 1997), the quantum Pieri rule (Buch–Kresch–Tamvakis, 2003), and the extension to all flag varieties by Fulton–Woodward (2004). The conceptual chain is that the quantum differential equation for the flag variety is the Dubrovin connection on the Frobenius manifold defined by the quantum cohomology, and its monodromy gives the braid group action on the derived category of coherent sheaves on the flag variety, linking enumerative geometry to homological mirror symmetry.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Factorial Schur Polynomials and Equivariant Cohomology

The equivariant cohomology ring HT*(Gr(k,n)) with respect to the maximal torus T = (ℂ×)n acting on the Grassmannian by scaling coordinates is a free module over the polynomial ring ℤ[t1,…,tn] with basis given by the equivariant Schubert classes σλ(t). The structure constants in this basis are the equivariant Littlewood–Richardson coefficients cλμν(t), which are polynomials in the torus weights. Factorial Schur polynomials sλ(x|t), introduced by Macdonald and developed by Okounkov and Olshanski, provide explicit polynomial representatives for these classes. They are defined by the formula sλ(x|t) = ∑T ∏(i,j)∈λ (xT(i,j) − tT(i,j)+j−i), where the sum runs over semistandard Young tableaux T of shape λ with entries in {1,…,n} and the product is over boxes of λ. The specialization sλ(x|0) recovers the ordinary Schur polynomial, while the evaluation at x = t gives the equivariant weight of the fixed point. The double Schubert polynomials 𝔖w(x,y) of Lascoux and Schützenberger generalize this to the full flag variety, and their Grassmannian restrictions yield the factorial Schur polynomials. A fundamental result of Knutson and Tao (2003) expresses the equivariant Littlewood–Richardson coefficients as a sum over puzzles with equivariant labels, where each puzzle piece carries a polynomial weight. The equivariant Pieri rule states that σλ ⋅ σ(r) = ∑μ ∏i∈I(μ/λ) (tμi+k−i+1 − tλi+k−i+1) σμ, where the sum is over partitions μ obtained by adding r boxes to λ, no two in the same column, and I(μ/λ) indexes the added boxes. This rule is essential for computing equivariant Gromov–Witten invariants via the quantum equivariant Chevalley formula of Mihalcea. In examinations, a typical problem asks to compute the equivariant product σ(2,1) ⋅ σ(1) in HT*(Gr(3,6)) and express the result as a polynomial in t1,…,t6. The solution requires identifying all partitions μ obtained by adding one box to (2,1) and evaluating the weight factors for each added box.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Space of Complete Quadrics

The space of complete quadrics, introduced by Hodge and Pedoe and later studied by De Concini and Procesi, provides a canonical compactification of the space of non‑degenerate quadrics in ℙn−1 that resolves the indeterminacies of the classical projective duality map. As a variety, it is isomorphic to the wonderful compactification of the adjoint group PGLn and carries a natural stratification by the rank and corank of the quadric. The cohomology ring of the space of complete quadrics is generated by the classes of the boundary divisors and the pullbacks of Schubert classes from the Grassmannians of isotropic subspaces. For a non‑degenerate quadric Q of dimension m, the isotropic Grassmannians OG(k,2m) and OG(k,2m+1) parametrize k‑planes contained in Q, and their Schubert calculus is governed by the orthogonal Schur polynomials sλ(B) and sλ(D) of type B and D. The space of complete quadrics interpolates between the Grassmannian of maximal isotropic subspaces and the projective space of all quadrics, and its Chow ring was computed by De Concini and Procesi (1983) using the theory of standard monomials. The intersection theory on this space solves enumerative problems such as counting lines tangent to a given set of quadrics; a classical result gives the number 40 for four quadrics in ℙ3. The geometry of complete quadrics also underlies the theory of Pfaffian Schubert calculus: the classes of degeneracy loci for skew‑symmetric maps are represented by Pfaffians of Schur polynomials, and the space of complete quadrics provides a geometric framework for the Littlewood–Richardson rule for type C. A standard examination question asks to derive the relation between the boundary divisor classes on the space of complete quadrics in ℙ3 and the Schubert classes on the Grassmannian Gr(2,4) via the incidence correspondence. The solution uses the fact that the space of complete quadrics in ℙ3 is the blow‑up of ℙ9 along the Veronese threefold of double planes, and the exceptional divisor corresponds to the Schubert divisor σ1 on Gr(2,4).

When computing equivariant Pieri products, always write the partition λ with exactly k parts (padding with zeros). The weight factor for a box added to row i is tμi+k−i+1 − tλi+k−i+1. The indices refer to the torus weights t1,…,tn in increasing order. A common error is to use the column index instead of the content μi+k−i+1. Memorize the formula as: new content minus old content. For the space of complete quadrics, remember that the boundary divisors correspond to the simple roots of the Lie algebra, and their intersection numbers are computed by pulling back to the Grassmannian of isotropic subspaces and using the orthogonal Pieri rule.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Shifted Schur Polynomials and Projective/Pfaffian Schur Functions

Shifted Schur polynomials sλ*(x), introduced by Okounkov and Olshanski, are symmetric polynomials in variables x1,…,xn that represent the Schubert classes in the cohomology of the odd orthogonal Grassmannian OG(n,2n+1) and the Lagrangian Grassmannian LG(n,2n). They are defined by the combinatorial formula sλ*(x) = ∑T ∏(i,j)∈λ (xT(i,j) + c(i,j)), where the sum is over shifted semistandard tableaux of shape λ and c(i,j) = j−i is the content of the box. The shifted Schur polynomials form a basis for the ring of symmetric polynomials that are stable under the inclusion OG(n,2n+1) OG(n+1,2n+3). The projective Schur functions Pλ(x) and Pfaffian Schur functions Qλ(x) of type B, C, D are related to the characters of the orthogonal and symplectic groups. For a strict partition λ (distinct parts), the Schur P-function Pλ is defined by a Pfaffian formula involving the elementary symmetric functions, and the Schur Q-function is Qλ = 2ℓ(λ) Pλ. These functions satisfy the Cauchy identity ∑λ strict Pλ(x) Qλ(y) = ∏i,j (1+xiyj)/(1−xiyj). The connection to Schubert calculus is that the cohomology ring H*(OG(n,2n+1)) is isomorphic to the quotient of the ring of shifted symmetric polynomials by the ideal generated by sλ* for λ not fitting in the n×n shifted staircase. The Pieri rule for shifted Schur polynomials involves adding a horizontal strip to the shifted diagram, with coefficients given by products of (xi ± xj) factors. For the Lagrangian Grassmannian LG(n,2n), the Schubert classes are represented by the Schur Q-functions, and the quantum cohomology ring was computed by Buch, Kresch, and Tamvakis using a quantum Pieri rule that involves rim‑hook removal in the shifted diagram. A typical examination problem asks to compute the product s(3,1)* ⋅ s(1)* in the shifted Schur basis and identify the corresponding intersection number on OG(4,9). The solution uses the shifted Pieri rule: the coefficient of sμ* is ∏ (xi + c) over added boxes, evaluated at the appropriate specialization.

Schubert Calculus and the Quantum Cohomology of the Lagrangian and Orthogonal Grassmannians

The quantum cohomology rings QH*(LG(n,2n)) and QH*(OG(n,2n+1)) were determined by Buch, Kresch, and Tamvakis (2003) and later extended to all cominuscule Grassmannians by Chaput, Manivel, and Perrin. The quantum parameter q corresponds to the degree of rational curves in the Grassmannian. For the Lagrangian Grassmannian LG(n,2n), the quantum Pieri rule states that the product of a quantum Schubert class σλ with the special class σ1 is given by the classical Pieri rule plus a quantum correction term: σλ ⋆ σ1 = ∑μ σμ + ∑ν q σν, where the first sum is over partitions μ obtained by adding one box to λ in the shifted diagram, and the second sum is over partitions ν obtained by removing a rim hook of length 2n−2λ1+1 from λ. The rim hook removal corresponds to a rational curve of degree 1 that sweeps out a Schubert variety of the expected dimension. For the odd orthogonal Grassmannian OG(n,2n+1), the quantum parameter is q2 because the minimal degree of a rational curve is 2. The quantum Pieri rule for OG(n,2n+1) involves rim hooks of length 2n−2λ1+2. The quantum Giambelli formula expresses an arbitrary quantum Schubert class as a determinant of quantum special classes, analogous to the classical case. The associativity of the quantum product is equivalent to the WDVV equations for the genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants. A landmark result is the quantum Littlewood–Richardson rule of Buch, Kresch, and Tamvakis for LG(n,2n), which expresses the product of two arbitrary quantum Schubert classes as a sum over shifted tableaux with quantum corrections. The structure constants are polynomials in q with non‑negative integer coefficients, a manifestation of the quantum positivity conjecture proved in this case. In examinations, a common question asks to compute the three‑point Gromov–Witten invariant ⟨σ(2,1), σ(2,1), σ(3,2,1)⟩1 on LG(4,8) using the quantum Pieri rule and the classical shifted Littlewood–Richardson coefficients. The solution involves expanding the product σ(2,1) ⋆ σ(2,1) using the quantum Pieri rule iteratively, identifying the coefficient of σ(3,2,1) multiplied by q, and verifying that the classical coefficient of σ(3,2,1) in the product σ(2,1) ⋅ σ(2,1) is zero, so the invariant comes entirely from the quantum correction.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Moduli Space of Parabolic Higgs Bundles

The moduli space MDol(C,G) of semistable G‑Higgs bundles on a smooth projective curve C carries a natural hyperkähler structure and is homeomorphic to the character variety MB(C,G) via the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence. When parabolic structures are introduced at marked points p1,…,pk ∈ C with weights αi in the fundamental Weyl chamber, the moduli space MDolpar acquires a stratification by the Harder–Narasimhan type of the underlying parabolic bundle. The cohomology of this space is computed using the Morse theory of the Hitchin functional ‖Φ‖2, whose critical points correspond to fixed points of the ℂ×‑action scaling the Higgs field. These fixed points are in bijection with tuples of dominant coweights satisfying a compatibility condition, and their Morse indices are given by the dimension of the attracting cells. The Schubert calculus enters through the identification of the attracting cells with affine Schubert varieties in the affine Grassmannian GrG. The cohomology ring H*(MDolpar) is generated by the Chern classes of the universal bundle and the tautological classes from the parabolic weights. For G = SLn, the fixed points are indexed by k-tuples of partitions λ(1),…,λ(k) with |λ(i)| = di, and the intersection pairing is computed by the parabolic Verlinde formula, which expresses the dimension of the space of conformal blocks as a sum over the fusion ring of the affine Lie algebra ŝln at level k. The connection to Schubert calculus is made precise by the work of Hausel and Thaddeus (2003) on mirror symmetry for SL2 and by the geometric Satake correspondence for general G, which identifies the fusion ring with the equivariant cohomology of the affine Grassmannian. A standard examination problem asks to compute the Poincaré polynomial of the moduli space of rank 2 parabolic Higgs bundles on ℙ1 with four marked points and generic weights. The solution uses the fact that this space is a K3 surface, whose Betti numbers are known classically, or alternatively applies the parabolic Verlinde formula for ŝl2 at level 2 to obtain the dimension of the fixed point set and the Morse indices.

  • Treating the space of complete quadrics as a Grassmannian: it is a blow‑up of the projective space of quadrics along the Veronese embedding of double planes; its Chow ring has more generators than the Grassmannian.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Stable Envelopes in Equivariant Cohomology

Stable envelopes, introduced by Maulik and Okounkov, provide a canonical basis in the equivariant cohomology of symplectic resolutions such as Nakajima quiver varieties and, in particular, cotangent bundles of Grassmannians. For a torus T acting on a symplectic variety X with a choice of chamber C in the Lie algebra and a polarization T1/2, the stable envelope map StabC sends a fixed point component F to a class supported on the attracting set AttrC(F). The construction satisfies three axioms: (1) support on the attracting set, (2) degree condition given by half the virtual normal bundle, and (3) normalization restricting to the equivariant Euler class of the negative normal bundle on the fixed component. For the Grassmannian Gr(k,n), the fixed points are indexed by k-subsets I of {1,…,n}. Choosing the standard chamber where the torus weights t1 > … > tn and the polarization given by the tautological bundle, the stable envelope basis {Stab(I)} is triangular with respect to the Schubert basis: Stab(I) = σI + ∑J < I cI,J σJ where coefficients are rational functions in the torus weights. This basis diagonalizes the quantum multiplication by the first Chern class of the determinant bundle, yielding the quantum difference equation ∇q Stab = 0 where ∇q is the quantum connection. The transition matrix between stable envelopes for opposite chambers gives the R-matrix satisfying the Yang–Baxter equation, linking Schubert calculus to quantum integrable systems. A standard exercise asks to compute the stable envelope for Gr(2,4) at the fixed point {1,3} and verify the triangularity property by expanding in the Schubert basis σ(1), σ(2), σ(1,1).

Schubert Calculus and the Quantum Giambelli Formula for Grassmannians

The classical Giambelli formula expresses any Schubert class σλ as a determinant of special Schubert classes σ(p): σλ = det(σ(λi - i + j)) for 1 ≤ i,j ≤ k. In the quantum cohomology ring QH*(Gr(k,n)), the quantum Giambelli formula replaces the classical special Schubert classes by their quantum counterparts σ(p) ⋆, defined by the quantum Pieri rule. The quantum special Schubert classes satisfy σ(p) ⋆ = σ(p) + q σ(p-n) for p > n-k, with the convention that σ(r) = 0 for r < 0 or r > n-k. The quantum Giambelli determinant then reads σλ ⋆ = det(σ(λi - i + j) ⋆). This identity holds because the quantum product is a deformation of the classical product preserving the Giambelli relations. A concrete computation: in Gr(2,5), the quantum class σ(2,1) ⋆ equals det[[σ(2) ⋆, σ(3) ⋆], [σ(0) ⋆, σ(1) ⋆]]. Using σ(3) ⋆ = σ(3) + q σ(-2) = σ(3) (since σ(-2)=0) and σ(2) ⋆ = σ(2), the determinant gives σ(2)σ(1) - σ(3), which matches the classical Giambelli because no quantum correction appears for this partition. For σ(3,2) ⋆ in Gr(2,5), the determinant involves σ(4) ⋆ = q σ(-1) = 0 and σ(3) ⋆ = σ(3), yielding the classical result again. Quantum corrections first appear when the partition exceeds the k × (n-k) rectangle, e.g., σ(3,3) ⋆ in Gr(2,6) where σ(4) ⋆ = q. The quantum Giambelli formula is essential for computing quantum Schubert polynomials and for proving the quantum Jacobi–Trudi identity, which expresses quantum Schur functions as determinants of quantum complete homogeneous functions.

Schubert Calculus and the Quantum Littlewood–Richardson Rule

The quantum Littlewood–Richardson rule computes the structure constants cλ,μν,d in the quantum product σλ ⋆ σμ = ∑ν,d cλ,μν,d qd σν for the Grassmannian Gr(k,n). Bertram's quantum Pieri rule states that multiplying by a special Schubert class σ(p) adds p boxes to the Young diagram with no two in the same column; if the resulting diagram exceeds the k × (n-k) rectangle, remove a rim hook of length n and multiply by q. The full quantum Littlewood–Richardson rule iterates this: the coefficient cλ,μν,d equals the sum over all sequences of rim hook removals of total length d n that reduce the classical Littlewood–Richardson expansion of σλ σμ to the partition ν. Equivalently, cλ,μν,d is the number of classical Littlewood–Richardson tableaux of shape ν/λ and weight μ such that the total number of boxes in the tableau that lie outside the k × (n-k) rectangle, counted with multiplicity of rim hooks of length n, equals d. A practical algorithm: compute the classical product σλ σμ using the classical LR rule; for each term σρ with ρ not fitting in the rectangle, repeatedly remove rim hooks of length n from ρ until the resulting partition ν fits, each removal contributing a factor q. The sum of all such paths gives the quantum product. For example, in Gr(3,6) (rectangle 3×3), compute σ(2,1) ⋆ σ(2,1). Classical LR gives σ(2,1) σ(2,1) = σ(4,2) + σ(3,3) + σ(3,2,1) + σ(2,2,2). The partitions (4,2) and (3,3) exceed the 3×3 rectangle. For (4,2), remove a rim hook of length 6: the rim hook covering the first row of length 4 and the second row of length 2 has length 6, leaving the empty partition, so contribution q σ∅. For (3,3), a rim hook of length 6 covering both rows leaves empty partition, contribution q σ∅. The other terms fit. Thus σ(2,1) ⋆ σ(2,1) = σ(3,2,1) + σ(2,2,2) + 2 q σ∅. This rule is the combinatorial heart of quantum Schubert calculus and directly yields three-point genus-zero Gromov–Witten invariants.

Schubert Calculus and the Geometry of the Space of Complete Intersections

The space of complete intersections of multidegree (d1,…,dr) in ℙN is the projective bundle ℙ(H0(ℙN, ⊕i=1r 𝒪(di))) modulo the action of PGL(N+1). Its Chow ring can be described via the incidence correspondence with the Grassmannian of r-planes in the space of homogeneous polynomials. Let V = H0(ℙN, ⊕ 𝒪(di)) and consider the universal complete intersection 𝒳 ⊂ ℙN × ℙ(V). The projection π: 𝒳 → ℙ(V) is flat, and the Chern classes of the relative tangent bundle give tautological classes on the base. Schubert calculus enters when we impose incidence conditions: for a linear subspace Λ ⊂ ℙN of codimension c, the condition that a complete intersection contains Λ is a Schubert condition on the Grassmannian of r-planes in V. The class of this condition is a Schubert class σλ where λ is determined by the degrees di and the codimension c. Intersection numbers of such Schubert classes on the Grassmannian count complete intersections satisfying multiple incidence conditions. Classically, the number of quadric surfaces in ℙ3 containing 9 general points equals the degree of the Schubert variety Σ(1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1,1) in Gr(1, H0(ℙ3, 𝒪(2))) ≅ ℙ9, which is 1. More generally, the number of complete intersections of type (d1,…,dr) containing a given set of linear spaces is computed by a Schubert calculus problem on a Grassmannian of dimension ∑ (N+di choose N) - 1. The Thom–Porteous formula for degeneracy loci of the evaluation map provides an alternative derivation, linking the enumerative geometry of complete intersections to the classical Schubert calculus on Grassmannians.

Schubert Calculus and the Theory of Quantum Schubert Polynomials

Quantum Schubert polynomials 𝔖w(x; q) for the flag variety Fl(n) were introduced by Fomin, Gelfand, and Postnikov as representatives of the quantum cohomology classes σw ⋆ in QH*(Fl(n)). They are defined by the quantum divided difference operators ∂iq = ∂i + qi si acting on the polynomial ring ℤ[x1,…,xn][q1,…,qn-1], where ∂i is the classical divided difference and si is the simple transposition. The quantum Schubert polynomial for the longest element w0 is the quantum analogue of the double Schubert polynomial, and all others are obtained by applying ∂iq. For Grassmannian permutations (those with a single descent at position k), the quantum Schubert polynomial reduces to a quantum Schur polynomial sλ(x; q) which represents the quantum Schubert class σλ ⋆ in QH*(Gr(k,n)). The quantum Monk rule gives the product of a quantum Schubert polynomial with a quantum elementary symmetric function: 𝔖w ⋆ ek = ∑ 𝔖w tij where the sum is over transpositions tij with i ≤ k < j and w(i) < w(j), plus quantum corrections when w(i) > w(j) corresponding to rim hook removal. Positivity of quantum Schubert polynomials (coefficients in ℕ[q]) was proved by Fomin–Gelfand–Postnikov using the quantum nilCoxeter algebra. A standard exercise computes 𝔖s1 s2(x; q) for n=3 using the quantum divided difference operators and verifies the quantum Monk rule for e1.
