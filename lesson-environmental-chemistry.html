Environmental Chemistry — Air/Water/Soil Pollution, Greenhouse Effect, Ozone Depletion & Green Chemistry

Deep dive into Environmental Chemistry — air pollution including particulate matter (PM₂.₅ and PM₁₀), nitrogen oxides (NOx), sulfur oxides (SOx), volatile organic compounds, photochemical smog (the Los Angeles-type smog from NOx and VOCs reacting under sunlight to produce ozone and PAN), acid rain (SO₂ and NOx converted to H₂SO₄ and HNO₃), water and soil pollution (eutrophication from phosphate and nitrate runoff, heavy metal contamination Hg Pb Cd Cr, pesticides like DDT and organophosphates, oil spills, bioremediation and phytoremediation strategies), the greenhouse effect (CO₂ from fossil fuels, CH₄ from agriculture, N₂O from fertilizers, positive feedback loops, climate change impacts), stratospheric ozone depletion (CFCs, halons, the chlorine catalytic cycle, the Antarctic ozone hole, Montreal Protocol and recovery), and green chemistry (the 12 principles including atom economy, less hazardous synthesis, safer solvents, design for degradation, renewable feedstocks, catalysis, real-time analysis, and inherently safer chemistry for accident prevention).

Part 1

🌻 Air Pollution

Atmosphere
Particulate Matter, NOx, SOx, Photochemical Smog, and Acid Rain
Air pollution involves the release of harmful substances into the atmosphere. Particulate matter (PM) is classified by aerodynamic diameter: PM₁₀ (<10 µm) penetrates the upper airways; PM₂.₅ (<2.5 µm) reaches the alveoli and enters the bloodstream, causing cardiovascular and respiratory disease. Sources include combustion engines, industrial emissions, and dust. Nitrogen oxides (NOx): NO and NO₂ form during high-temperature combustion (N₂ + O₂ → 2 NO, then 2 NO + O₂ → 2 NO₂). NO₂ is a brown gas that contributes to photochemical smog and acid rain. Sulfur oxides (SOx) come mainly from burning sulfur-containing fossil fuels (coal, oil): S + O₂ → SO₂; 2 SO₂ + O₂ → 2 SO₃. SO₂ oxidizes to H₂SO₄ in the atmosphere. Photochemical smog forms when NOx and VOCs react under sunlight: NO₂ photolyzes to NO + O(³P), O(³P) + O₂ → O₃ (ozone), which reacts with VOCs to produce peroxyacyl nitrates (PAN), aldehydes, and other secondary pollutants. Ground-level ozone is a respiratory irritant. Acid rain (pH < 5.6) results from SO₂ and NOx converting to sulfuric and nitric acids: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃ (sulfurous acid), 2 SO₂ + O₂ + 2 H₂O → 2 H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid). Acid rain damages forests (leaches Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ from soil), acidifies lakes (killing fish), and corrodes buildings (especially limestone CaCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → CaSO₄ + CO₂ + H₂O).
Air pollution arises from combustion and industrial processes; its secondary products (tropospheric ozone, acid rain) cause widespread environmental and health damage across regional scales.
Part 2

💦 Water & Soil Pollution

Hydrosphere
Eutrophication, Heavy Metals, Pesticides, and Remediation Strategies
Water pollution encompasses chemical, biological, and physical degradation of water bodies. Eutrophication is the enrichment of water with nutrients (phosphates from detergents and fertilizers, nitrates from agricultural runoff and sewage), causing algal blooms that block sunlight and deplete oxygen when they decompose (hypoxia → dead zones). The limiting nutrient in freshwater is usually phosphorus; in coastal waters, nitrogen. Heavy metals (Hg, Pb, Cd, Cr, As) are toxic even at trace levels. Mercury from coal burning and gold mining enters water as Hg&sup0;, is methylated by bacteria to CH₃Hg⁺ (methylmercury), which bioaccumulates in fish (biomagnification up the food chain), causing neurological damage. Lead from old pipes and paints causes developmental toxicity. Cadmium from battery and fertilizer industries causes itai-itai disease (bone softening, kidney failure). Pesticides: organochlorines (DDT, now banned in most countries due to persistence and eggshell thinning in birds), organophosphates (malathion, parathion — acetylcholinesterase inhibitors causing nerve damage), and herbicides (atrazine, glyphosate). Oil spills (Exxon Valdez, Deepwater Horizon) coat wildlife and shorelines. Soil contamination from industrial waste, landfills, and agricultural chemicals. Remediation strategies: bioremediation (using microorganisms to degrade pollutants, e.g., Pseudomonas for oil spills, Deinococcus radiodurans for radioactive waste), phytoremediation (using plants to absorb heavy metals like Arabidopsis halleri for Zn and Cd, Pteris vittata for As), chemical precipitation (adding lime to remove metals as hydroxides), activated carbon adsorption, and membrane filtration (reverse osmosis).
Water and soil pollution involve complex mixtures of contaminants; biological remediation offers sustainable solutions, but prevention through source control remains the most effective strategy.
Part 3

🌎 Greenhouse Effect & Ozone Depletion

Climate
CO₂, CH₄, CFCs, Ozone Hole, and Climate Change
The greenhouse effect is the natural trapping of infrared radiation by atmospheric gases, keeping Earth ~33°C warmer than it would be otherwise. Greenhouse gases (GHGs) absorb and re-emit IR radiation. The major GHGs: CO₂ (global warming potential GWP = 1, atmospheric lifetime ~100 years, concentration risen from ~280 ppm pre-industrial to ~420 ppm today from fossil fuel burning and deforestation), CH₄ (methane, GWP ~28-34 over 100 years, lifetime ~12 years, from livestock, rice paddies, landfills, natural gas leaks), N₂O (GWP ~265, lifetime ~121 years, from fertilizers and industrial processes), and fluorinated gases (F-gases, high GWPs up to 23,000). The enhanced greenhouse effect from anthropogenic emissions drives global warming (average surface temperature rise of ~1.2°C since pre-industrial) and climate change (extreme weather, sea level rise from thermal expansion and melting ice sheets, ocean acidification from CO₂ dissolving to form H₂CO₃). Positive feedback loops: ice-albedo feedback (melting ice reduces reflectivity, causing more absorption and more melting), permafrost thaw releasing CH₄ and CO₂. Ozone depletion occurs in the stratosphere (15-35 km), where the ozone layer (O₃) absorbs 97-99% of UV-B and UV-C radiation. Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) used as refrigerants and propellants drift to the stratosphere where UV photolysis releases Cl atoms: CFCl₃ + hν → Cl⋅ + CFCl₂⋅. The catalytic cycle: Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂; ClO + O → Cl + O₂ (net: O₃ + O → 2 O₂). A single Cl atom destroys ~100,000 ozone molecules. Polar stratospheric clouds (PSCs) enhance depletion by providing surfaces for heterogeneous reactions that activate chlorine reservoirs (HCl, ClONO₂). The Antarctic ozone hole forms each spring. The Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs and halons; the ozone layer is projected to recover by ~2060-2070.
The greenhouse effect is essential for life, but anthropogenic emissions have dangerously amplified it; ozone depletion, though successfully addressed by the Montreal Protocol, required decisive global action.

🂳 Green Chemistry

Sustainability
The 12 Principles, Atom Economy, Renewable Feedstocks, and Catalysis
Green chemistry is the design of chemical products and processes that reduce or eliminate hazardous substances (Anastas and Warner, 1998). The 12 principles guide sustainable chemical design. 1. Prevention: prevent waste rather than treat it. 2. Atom economy: maximize incorporation of reactants into final product (atom economy = MWproduct / Σ MWreactants × 100%). Trost atom economy: a reaction with 100% atom economy (e.g., Diels-Alder, hydrogenation) generates no waste; a reaction like the Wittig reaction has poor atom economy (~25%) because of the triphenylphosphine oxide byproduct. 3. Less hazardous synthesis: design methods that use and produce substances with minimal toxicity. 4. Safer solvents: avoid organic solvents; use water, supercritical CO₂ (scCO₂), ionic liquids, or solvent-free conditions. 5. Energy efficiency: conduct reactions at ambient temperature and pressure when possible; microwave and ultrasound assistance reduce energy. 6. Renewable feedstocks: use biomass-derived feedstocks (lignocellulose, vegetable oils, terpenes) rather than depletable petroleum. 7. Reduce derivatives: minimize protecting groups and temporary modifications. 8. Catalysis: catalytic reagents (enzymes, organocatalysts, metal catalysts) are superior to stoichiometric reagents; they reduce waste and energy. 9. Design for degradation: products should break down into innocuous substances after use (biodegradability). 10. Real-time analysis: in-process monitoring to prevent hazardous byproduct formation. 11. Inherently safer chemistry: minimize the potential for accidents (releases, explosions, fires). 12. Benign chemistry for accident prevention: choose substances and forms that minimize chemical accident hazards. The E-factor (kg waste per kg product) quantifies environmental impact: pharmaceutical industry has high E-factors (~25-100), oil refining low (~0.1).
Green chemistry redesigns chemical processes at the molecular level to be inherently safer and more sustainable, guided by 12 principles that balance environmental, economic, and societal goals.
