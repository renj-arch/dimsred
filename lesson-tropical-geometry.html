Tropical Geometry — Tropical Curves, Amoebas & the Maslov Torus

Tropical geometry is a piecewise-linear shadow of algebraic geometry obtained by replacing the usual arithmetic with the tropical semiring (ℝ ∪ {∞}, ⊕, ⊗) where a⊕b = min(a,b) and a⊗b = a+b (the min-plus algebra). A tropical polynomial P(x₁,…,xₙ) = ⊕ c_α ⊗ x^α = min(c_α + α·x) is a convex piecewise-linear function, and its tropical hypersurface V(P) = {x ∈ ℝⁿ : the minimum is attained at least twice} is a rational polyhedral complex. The tropicalization of a classical algebraic variety V ⊂ (ℂ^*)ⁿ is the set Trop(V) = lim_{t→0} Log_t(V) where Log_t(z) = (log_t|z₁|,…,log_t|zₙ|) — this is the amoeba limit under Maslov dequantization. The Kapranov theorem states that for a hypersurface defined by a Laurent polynomial f, the tropicalization Trop(V(f)) equals the tropical hypersurface V(trop(f)). The subject has exploded since the 1990s: Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem equates enumerative counts of algebraic curves in toric surfaces with counts of tropical curves, the Adiprasito-Huh-Katz Hodge theory for matroids proved Rota's conjecture, and tropical methods now permeate algebraic geometry, combinatorics, mirror symmetry, and even phylogenetics.

Part 1

📏 The Tropical Semiring and Tropical Polynomials

Semiring
Min-Plus Algebra, Tropical Polynomials, and Tropical Hypersurfaces
The tropical semiring 𝕋 = (ℝ ∪ {∞}, ⊕, ⊗) is defined by a⊕b = min(a,b) and a⊗b = a+b. The additive identity is ∞ and the multiplicative identity is 0. A tropical monomial is c⊗x₁^{a₁}⊗⋯⊗xₙ^{aₙ} = c + Σ aᵢxᵢ, and a tropical polynomial f(x) = ⊕ c_α ⊗ x^α = min_α(c_α + α·x) is a piecewise-linear convex function on ℝⁿ. The tropical hypersurface V(f) = {x ∈ ℝⁿ : the minimum is attained at least twice} is a rational polyhedral complex of codimension 1. The Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry (Kapranov, Speyer-Sturmfels): for an algebraically closed field K with non-Archimedean valuation val, the tropicalization Trop(V(I)) = ⋂_{f∈I} V(trop(f)) is the support of a rational polyhedral complex. The Newton polytope Newt(f) = conv{α : c_α ≠ ∞} determines the combinatorial type of V(f): the tropical hypersurface is the codimension-1 skeleton of the dual subdivision of Newt(f) given by the regular subdivision induced by the coefficients c_α.
Tropical polynomials are convex piecewise-linear functions; their hypersurfaces are rational polyhedral complexes dual to subdivisions of Newton polytopes — this is the combinatorial heart of tropical geometry.
Part 2

🌍 Tropical Curves and Their Geometry

Curves
Balanced Polyhedral Complexes, Genus, and Mikhalkin's Correspondence Theorem
A tropical curve in ℝ² is a finite graph Γ embedded as a 1-dimensional polyhedral complex whose edges have rational slopes and are weighted by positive integers (multiplicities). The balancing condition must hold at every vertex: for a vertex v with incident edges e₁,…,e_k having weights wᵢ and primitive direction vectors vᵢ pointing away from v, we require Σ wᵢ·vᵢ = 0. This is the tropical analogue of the zero-sum condition for tangent vectors at a node of an algebraic curve. The genus of a tropical curve is the first Betti number g = b₁(Γ) (the number of independent cycles in the graph). Tropical intersection theory (Allermann-Rau, Katz) gives well-defined tropical intersection products on tropical subvarieties. The tropical Bézout theorem: two tropical curves in ℝ² of degrees d₁ and d₂ intersect in exactly d₁·d₂ points (counted with multiplicities). Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem (2005): the number of degree-d tropical curves in ℝ² through (3d+g−1) points in general position equals the number of algebraic curves of genus g and degree d in a toric surface passing through the same number of points — i.e., tropical curve counts equal Gromov-Witten invariants. The Newton polygon of a tropical plane curve determines its genus via Pick's formula: g = #(interior lattice points of Newt).
Tropical curves are balanced weighted graphs whose combinatorial counts match algebraic Gromov-Witten invariants — connecting discrete geometry to enumerative algebraic geometry.

🧮 Amoebas and the Maslov Dequantization

Amoebas
Logarithmic Limit Sets, Ronkin Functions, and the Tropicalization Functor
The amoeba of an algebraic variety V ⊂ (ℂ^*)ⁿ is the image 𝒜_V = Log(V) ⊂ ℝⁿ where Log(z₁,…,zₙ) = (log|z₁|,…,log|zₙ|). The amoeba is a closed set whose complement consists of finitely many convex regions, each corresponding to an integer lattice point of the Newton polytope Newt(V) (the Gelfand-Kapranov-Zelevinsky correspondence). The Ronkin function N_V: ℝⁿ → ℝ defined by N_V(x) = (1/(2πi)ⁿ) ∫_{Log⁻¹(x)} log|f(z)|·Π (dzᵢ/zᵢ) is convex and piecewise-linear at infinity; its Legendre transform gives the tropical limit. The spine of the amoeba is the set where the Ronkin function is not strictly convex — it is a tropical variety (the tropical skeleton). Maslov dequantization (Litvinov-Maslov): consider the family of algebras (ℝ, +_h, ×) where a +_h b = h·log(e^{a/h} + e^{b/h}) and × is ordinary addition; as h → 0⁺, this deformation converges to the tropical semiring (min-plus). Applying this deformation to the equations defining a classical variety gives the tropicalization. The Bergman fan of a matroid M is the tropical variety Trop(V(I_M)) associated to the linear ideal over the Puiseux series. The tropicalization of a subvariety V ⊂ (K^*)ⁿ (K a valued field) is the closure of val(V) in ℝⁿ, which is the support of a rational polyhedral complex of pure dimension dim(V).
Amoebas are logarithmic images of complex varieties; their spines are tropical varieties, revealing that tropical geometry emerges as the semiclassical limit of complex geometry via Maslov dequantization.

🔥 Applications and Tropical Mirror Symmetry

Applications
Enumerative Geometry, Hodge Theory for Matroids, and Tropical Moduli
Enumerative geometry via tropical curves: Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem allows computing Gromov-Witten invariants of toric surfaces by counting tropical curves, which reduces a transcendental problem to a finite combinatorial enumeration (e.g., the number of rational degree-d curves through 3d−1 points in ℙ²). Tropical compactifications (Tevelev): given a subvariety V ⊂ (K^*)ⁿ, its tropicalization Trop(V) defines a fan whose associated toric variety X(Δ) gives a compactification of V with controlled boundary — the closure of V in X(Δ) is proper and flat. Adiprasito-Huh-Katz (2016): using tropical Hodge theory they proved the Hodge-Riemann relations for matroids, establishing the Rota-Heron-Welsh conjecture (the log-concavity of the characteristic polynomial). Tropical moduli spaces: the moduli space of tropical curves M_g^{trop} is a tropical variety (a generalized cone complex) whose cells correspond to stable graph types; it computes the boundary of the Deligne-Mumford compactification M̅_g. Tropical Grassmannians (the Dressian Dr(k,n)) parameterize tropical linear spaces and are related to the classical Grassmannian via the tropicalization map. Grigoriev-Podolskii proved a tropical analogue of the Sturmfels theorem on sparse elimination. Applications extend to algebraic statistics (tropical PCA), phylogenetics (the space of phylogenetic trees is a tropical variety — the Bergman fan of the graphic matroid), and optimization (tropical convexity provides new algorithms for discrete convex analysis).
Tropical geometry has unlocked enumerative computations, proved deep combinatorial conjectures via Hodge theory, and built bridges to statistics, phylogenetics, and optimization.

Tropical Intersection Theory and the Balanced Condition

Tropical intersection theory, developed by Allermann and Rau and later refined by Katz, provides a rigorous framework for intersecting tropical cycles inside a tropical variety. A tropical cycle is a weighted rational polyhedral complex satisfying the balancing condition at every codimension‑one face: the sum of the primitive integer normal vectors of the adjacent maximal cells, each multiplied by its weight, equals zero. This condition is the tropical analogue of the algebraic fact that the divisor of a rational function has degree zero. The intersection product of two tropical cycles of complementary dimension is defined by first refining the polyhedral structures so that the cycles intersect transversely, then assigning to each intersection point a multiplicity equal to the absolute value of the determinant of the lattice generated by the direction vectors of the intersecting cells. The resulting zero‑cycle is independent of the refinement, yielding a well‑defined intersection number. The tropical Bézout theorem follows immediately: two tropical plane curves of degrees d₁ and d₂ intersect in exactly d₁·d₂ points counted with these multiplicities. This theorem mirrors the classical Bézout theorem but is proved by a purely combinatorial argument using the Newton polygons of the defining tropical polynomials. The balancing condition also guarantees that tropical cycles form a ring under the intersection product, and the projection formula holds for morphisms of tropical varieties. These results allow one to compute Gromov‑Witten invariants of toric surfaces by counting tropical curves with prescribed incidence conditions, because Mikhalkin’s correspondence theorem identifies the tropical counts with the corresponding algebraic invariants.

Tropical Moduli Spaces and the Boundary of M_g

The moduli space of tropical curves M_gtrop is a generalized cone complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of stable metric graphs of genus g. A point in a cone represents a graph with fixed topology and positive edge lengths; the faces of the cone are obtained by collapsing edges of length zero, which corresponds to the formation of nodes in the algebraic setting. The space M_gtrop is connected, rational polyhedral, and of pure dimension 3g−3 for g≥2. Its natural compactification adds boundary strata indexed by stable graphs, exactly mirroring the Deligne‑Mumford compactification M̅_g of the moduli space of algebraic curves. The tropical Torelli map sends a metric graph to its Jacobian, a principally polarized tropical abelian variety, and this map is a morphism of cone complexes. The image of M_gtrop under the Torelli map is the tropical Schottky locus, a proper subcone complex of the moduli space of tropical principally polarized abelian varieties A_gtrop. The combinatorial structure of M_gtrop has been used to compute the rational cohomology of M_g and to prove that the tautological ring of M_g is generated by kappa classes. Moreover, the tropical boundary strata provide a concrete description of the limit mixed Hodge structures on the cohomology of degenerating families of curves, linking tropical geometry to the weight filtration in Hodge theory.

Worked Example: Computing a Tropical Intersection Multiplicity
Let C₁ be the tropical line defined by min(x, y, 0) and C₂ the tropical conic defined by min(2x, 2y, x+y, 0). Determine the intersection points of C₁ and C₂ in ℝ² and compute the multiplicity at each point.
The tropical line C₁ consists of three rays: x = y ≤ 0, x = 0 ≤ y, and y = 0 ≤ x, meeting at the vertex (0,0). The tropical conic C₂ has Newton polygon with vertices (2,0), (0,2), (1,1), (0,0). Its corner locus is a balanced graph with four unbounded rays in directions (−1,0), (0,−1), (1,−1), (−1,1) and a bounded edge connecting the vertices (−1,0) and (0,−1) after appropriate translation. By drawing both curves one finds three intersection points: (0,0), (−1,0), and (0,−1). At (0,0) the line has direction vectors (1,0) and (0,1); the conic has direction vectors (−1,0) and (0,−1). The lattice generated by (1,0) and (0,1) has determinant 1, so multiplicity = 1. At (−1,0) the line direction is (1,0) (the ray x=0) and the conic direction is (−1,0) (the ray (−1,0)). The two vectors are opposite, generating a lattice of index 1, multiplicity = 1. Similarly at (0,−1) multiplicity = 1. The sum of multiplicities is 3, which equals the product of degrees 1·2 = 2? Wait, the tropical Bézout theorem states the total intersection number equals d₁·d₂ = 2. The discrepancy arises because the conic as written has degree 2 but its Newton polygon contains the interior lattice point (1,1), giving genus 1; the tropical Bézout theorem for curves of genera g₁,g₂ requires counting with multiplicities that sum to d₁·d₂. Re‑examining the conic: the correct tropical conic of degree 2 has Newton polygon with vertices (2,0),(0,2),(0,0) and no interior points, giving a rational curve. Its corner locus has three unbounded rays and one bounded edge. Intersecting with the line yields exactly two points each of multiplicity 1, total 2, matching Bézout. The earlier conic included an extra term x+y which raises the degree. Thus the correct intersection count is 2.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for tropical intersection numbers, first write down the Newton polygons of the defining tropical polynomials. The mixed volume of the Newton polygons equals the total intersection number (Bernstein‑Kushnirenko theorem). Use this as a quick check: if your point‑by‑point multiplicities do not sum to the mixed volume, you have missed an intersection point or miscomputed a multiplicity.

Tropical Hodge Theory for Matroids and the Adiprasito‑Huh‑Katz Theorem

In 2018 Adiprasito, Huh, and Katz proved the Hodge‑Riemann relations for the Chow ring of a matroid, thereby establishing the long‑standing Rota‑Heron‑Welsh conjecture on the log‑concavity of the characteristic polynomial. The proof constructs a tropical Hodge theory on the Bergman fan of a matroid, which is the tropicalization of the linear subspace defined by the matroid over a valued field. The Chow ring A^*(M) of a matroid M is generated by variables x_F for each non‑empty proper flat F, subject to linear relations ∑F∋i x_F = ∑F∋j x_F for all i,j and quadratic relations x_F x_G = 0 when F and G are incomparable. The degree map sends the top‑degree monomial corresponding to a maximal chain of flats to 1. The key step is to define a strictly convex piecewise‑linear function on the Bergman fan, which serves as an ample class ℓ. One then verifies the hard Lefschetz property: multiplication by ℓd−k gives an isomorphism A^k(M) → Ad−k(M), and the Hodge‑Riemann bilinear form (−1)^k ℓd−2k is positive definite on the primitive subspace. The tropical proof uses the fact that the Bergman fan is a rational polyhedral fan satisfying the balancing condition, and the convex function is obtained by pulling back a strictly convex function on the tropical projective space. This machinery has since been extended to valuated matroids and to the tropical Hodge theory of arbitrary tropical varieties, providing a combinatorial framework for Hodge theory that does not require complex geometry.

Tropical Mirror Symmetry: SYZ Fibrations and Tropical Calabi‑Yau

Mirror symmetry predicts an equivalence between the symplectic geometry of a Calabi‑Yau manifold X and the complex geometry of its mirror X̂. The Strominger‑Yau‑Zaslow (SYZ) conjecture proposes that this equivalence is realized by dual torus fibrations: X and X̂ admit special Lagrangian torus fibrations over the same base B, with generic fibers dual tori. In the tropical limit, the base B becomes an integral affine manifold with singularities, and the fibrations are replaced by their tropicalizations. The tropical manifold Trop(X) is a polyhedral complex equipped with an integral affine structure away from a codimension‑two discriminant locus. The mirror tropical manifold Trop(X̂) is obtained by taking the fiberwise dual of the torus fibration, which at the tropical level corresponds to the Legendre transform of the affine structure. Gross and Siebert developed a program to reconstruct the mirror pair from a tropical manifold with a polarization, using scattering diagrams to correct the affine structure across walls. The tropical vertex, introduced by Kontsevich and Soibelman, encodes the wall‑crossing automorphisms that ensure the consistency of the scattering diagram. For toric Calabi‑Yau threefolds, the tropical mirror is described by a consistent scattering diagram on ℝ² whose walls are tropical curves counted by open Gromov‑Witten invariants. The resulting tropical geometry computes the generating functions of both closed and open Gromov‑Witten invariants, providing a combinatorial algorithm for mirror symmetry that has been verified in numerous examples, including the resolved conifold and local ℙ².

Tropicalization of Cluster Algebras and the Fock‑Goncharov Conjecture

Cluster algebras, introduced by Fomin and Zelevinsky, are commutative algebras generated by clusters of variables related by mutations. Fock and Goncharov associated to each cluster variety a tropical space, the tropicalization of the cluster variety, which carries a natural piecewise‑linear structure. The tropical points of a cluster variety correspond to valuations on the field of rational functions that are compatible with the cluster structure. The Fock‑Goncharov conjecture asserts that the tropical points of a cluster variety are in bijection with the set of generic bases of the associated upper cluster algebra, and that the tropicalization map induces an isomorphism between the tropical variety and the space of valuations. This conjecture has been proved for cluster varieties arising from surfaces (by Goncharov and Shen) and for acyclic cluster algebras (by Gross, Hacking, Keel, and Kontsevich). The proof uses the tropicalization of the cluster scattering diagram, which is a collection of walls in the tropical space where the cluster coordinates undergo piecewise‑linear transformations. The consistency of the scattering diagram is equivalent to the tropical version of the cluster mutation relations. The tropical cluster variety also carries a natural symplectic form, the tropicalization of the canonical Poisson structure on the cluster variety, making it a tropical symplectic manifold. This structure has applications to the study of Donaldson‑Thomas invariants, where the tropical scattering diagram computes the wall‑crossing of BPS invariants, and to the theory of higher Teichmüller spaces, where the tropical points parametrize positive representations of surface groups.

Tropical Compactifications and Toric Degenerations

Tropical compactifications provide a systematic way to extend a subvariety of an algebraic torus to a complete variety by using the combinatorics of its tropicalization. Given a subvariety X ⊂ (K*)ⁿ over a valued field K, its tropicalization Trop(X) is a polyhedral complex in ℝⁿ. A rational polyhedral fan Σ refining the recession fan of Trop(X) determines a toric variety X_Σ. The closure of X in X_Σ is a tropical compactification if the intersection of X with each torus orbit is either empty or of the expected dimension. Tevelev proved that such a fan always exists after a suitable subdivision, and that the resulting compactification is independent of the chosen refinement up to isomorphism. The boundary divisors correspond to the rays of Σ, and their intersections reflect the face structure of the tropical variety. This construction is functorial with respect to tropical modifications, which correspond to star subdivisions of the fan. In the context of degenerations, a tropical compactification yields a flat family over a valuation ring whose generic fiber is X and whose special fiber is a reduced union of toric varieties glued along toric strata. The dual intersection complex of the special fiber is naturally identified with Trop(X). This picture underlies the Gross–Siebert program, where logarithmic structures on the central fiber encode the scattering diagram that reconstructs the mirror. For curves, the tropical compactification of a smooth curve in (K*)² recovers the stable model after a suitable base change, and the tropicalization of the curve coincides with the metric graph of the special fiber. The theory extends to higher‑dimensional varieties, where the tropical compactification provides a combinatorial model for the minimal model program in the tropical setting.

Tropical Abel‑Jacobi Maps and Jacobians

The tropical analogue of the classical Abel–Jacobi map sends a divisor on a metric graph Γ to a point in the tropical Jacobian Jac(Γ) = H₁(Γ, ℝ)/H₁(Γ, ℤ). For a tropical curve C (a balanced weighted polyhedral complex of dimension one), the Jacobian is a real torus of dimension equal to the first Betti number of C. The map is defined by integrating a basis of harmonic 1‑forms along paths from a fixed basepoint to the points of the divisor. Baker and Norine established a Riemann–Roch theorem for finite graphs, which lifts to metric graphs via the theory of reduced divisors. The tropical Jacobian carries a natural principal polarization given by the intersection pairing on H₁(Γ, ℤ). The tropical Abel–Jacobi map is a piecewise‑linear isomorphism from the set of effective divisors of degree g modulo linear equivalence onto the theta divisor in Jac(Γ). This divisor is a polyhedral complex of codimension one whose faces correspond to divisors with non‑trivial automorphisms. The theory extends to tropical curves with marked points, where the Jacobian is replaced by a generalized Jacobian parametrizing line bundles with prescribed behavior at the marks. Mikhalkin and Zharkov showed that the tropical theta divisor is the tropicalization of the classical theta divisor on the Jacobian of an algebraic curve, and that the tropical Abel–Jacobi map is the limit of the classical map under the Maslov dequantization. Recent work by Amini, Baker, and others has developed a tropical version of the Schottky problem: characterizing which principally polarized tropical abelian varieties arise as Jacobians of tropical curves. The answer involves the tropical Novikov–Veselov hierarchy and the tropical Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation, providing a piecewise‑linear analogue of the classical KP hierarchy.

Worked Example: Computing a Tropical Jacobian
Let Γ be the metric graph consisting of two vertices joined by three edges of lengths 1, 2, and 3. Compute the tropical Jacobian Jac(Γ) and describe the tropical theta divisor.
The first Betti number of Γ is 2 (three edges, two vertices, so rank = 3−2+1 = 2). Choose a basis of cycles: c₁ goes along edge 1 and back along edge 2; c₂ goes along edge 1 and back along edge 3. The period matrix Ω has entries Ωij = ∫c_i ω_j where ω_j are the harmonic 1‑forms dual to the cycles. For this graph the harmonic forms are constant on each edge with values determined by Kirchhoff's laws. Solving yields Ω = [[1+2, 1], [1, 1+3]] = [[3, 1], [1, 4]]. The tropical Jacobian is ℝ² / Ωℤ², a real 2‑torus with the flat metric induced by Ω. The tropical theta divisor is the set of points x ∈ ℝ² such that the minimum of { x·m + ½ mᵀΩ m } over m ∈ ℤ² is attained at least twice. This is a piecewise‑linear curve in the torus with two vertices corresponding to the two break divisors of degree 2 on Γ. The divisor is the tropicalization of the classical theta divisor on the Jacobian of a genus‑2 curve degenerating to Γ.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the tropical Jacobian of a given metric graph, first compute the first Betti number to know the dimension of the torus. Then write down a basis of cycles and the corresponding period matrix using edge lengths. The tropical theta divisor is defined by the tropical quadratic form associated to the period matrix; sketch its polyhedral structure by finding where the minimum in the tropical theta function is attained at least twice. Remember that the tropical Abel–Jacobi map is a piecewise‑linear isomorphism onto the theta divisor, so the number of break divisors equals the number of vertices of the theta divisor.

Tropical Theta Functions and the Tropical Schottky Problem

Tropical theta functions are piecewise‑linear analogues of classical Riemann theta functions. For a principally polarized tropical abelian variety (A, Θ) given by a positive definite symmetric matrix Q of size g, the tropical theta function is θ_Q(z) = minm ∈ ℤ^g ( ½ mᵀ Q m + z·m ). Its corner locus, where the minimum is attained at least twice, is the tropical theta divisor Θ. The tropical Schottky problem asks which principally polarized tropical abelian varieties are Jacobians of tropical curves. A complete answer is known for g ≤ 3: every indecomposable principally polarized tropical abelian variety of dimension ≤ 3 is a Jacobian. For g = 4, the tropical Schottky locus is a proper subset characterized by the tropical Novikov–Veselov equation, a piecewise‑linear version of the KP hierarchy. The equation can be written as a tropical Hirota bilinear identity: for all vectors u, v, w ∈ ℤ^g, the minimum of { θ(z+u+v) + θ(z+w), θ(z+u+w) + θ(z+v), θ(z+v+w) + θ(z+u) } is attained at least twice. This condition is equivalent to the existence of a tropical curve whose period matrix equals Q. The tropical Schottky locus is a polyhedral fan in the moduli space of tropical abelian varieties A_g^trop. Its cones correspond to combinatorial types of tropical curves. Recent work by Jensen, Len, and others has shown that the tropical Schottky locus is the image of the tropical Torelli map from the moduli space of tropical curves M_g^trop to A_g^trop. The fibers of this map are the tropical analogues of the classical Torelli fibers, and their structure is governed by the tropical Prym variety construction. The tropical KP hierarchy also provides a method to compute tropical theta functions recursively, which is useful for explicit enumerative calculations.

Tropical Enumerative Geometry: Gromov‑Witten Invariants via Tropical Counts

Tropical enumerative geometry replaces counts of holomorphic curves in a symplectic manifold by counts of tropical curves in the associated tropical variety. Mikhalkin's correspondence theorem states that for a toric surface, the number of genus‑g tropical curves of degree Δ passing through 3g−1+|Δ∩ℤ²| generic points equals the corresponding Gromov‑Witten invariant. The tropical curves are weighted balanced graphs in ℝ² whose Newton polygon is Δ. Each vertex satisfies the balancing condition, and the multiplicity of a vertex is the lattice area of the parallelogram spanned by the primitive integer directions of the adjacent edges. The total multiplicity of a tropical curve is the product of its vertex multiplicities. The theorem extends to higher‑dimensional toric varieties via the work of Nishinou and Siebert, who used logarithmic geometry to prove a correspondence for arbitrary toric varieties. For Calabi‑Yau threefolds, the tropical vertex formalism of Kontsevich and Soibelman expresses the generating function of open and closed Gromov‑Witten invariants as a product of quantum dilogarithms associated to walls in a scattering diagram on ℝ³. The walls are tropical curves counted by open invariants, and the consistency of the scattering diagram is equivalent to the wall‑crossing formula for Donaldson‑Thomas invariants. Gross, Pandharipande, and Siebert proved that the tropical counts compute the descendant Gromov‑Witten invariants of toric Calabi‑Yau threefolds. The tropical approach also yields algorithms for computing invariants of non‑toric varieties by degenerating them to toric ones and analyzing the limit tropical curves. This has been applied to compute Gromov‑Witten invariants of del Pezzo surfaces, K3 surfaces, and the quintic threefold.

Tropical Geometry of Linear Systems and Brill‑Noether Theory

The tropical analogue of a linear system on an algebraic curve is a divisor class on a metric graph together with the set of effective divisors linearly equivalent to it. Baker and Norine's Riemann–Roch theorem for graphs gives the dimension r(D) of the complete linear system |D| as r(D) = deg(D) − g + 1 + r(K−D), where K is the canonical divisor. The tropical Brill‑Noether locus W^r_d(Γ) parametrizes divisor classes of degree d and rank at least r. Its expected dimension is ρ = g − (r+1)(g−d+r). For a generic metric graph, the locus is either empty or pure of dimension ρ, and its tropical homology groups satisfy a tropical version of the classical Brill‑Noether theorem. Jensen and Payne proved that the tropical Brill‑Noether locus is a polyhedral complex whose cells correspond to combinatorial types of divisors. The tropical Clifford index of a metric graph is the minimum of deg(D) − 2r(D) over all divisors with 0 ≤ r(D) ≤ g/2. This invariant coincides with the classical Clifford index of a smooth algebraic curve degenerating to the graph. Tropical linear systems also admit a theory of limit linear series on metrized complexes of curves, developed by Amini and Baker, which provides a bridge between tropical and algebraic Brill‑Noether theory. The tropical approach has been used to prove new results in classical algebraic geometry, such as the maximal rank conjecture for quadrics on algebraic curves, by degenerating to a chain of loops and analyzing the tropical linear series.

Common Traps

  • Confusing the tropical compactification of a subvariety with the closure in an arbitrary toric variety; the fan must refine the tropicalization to guarantee the expected dimension of orbit intersections.
  • Assuming the tropical Jacobian of a metric graph is always a product of circles with the standard metric; the period matrix depends on edge lengths and the choice of harmonic basis.
  • Treating the tropical theta divisor as a smooth hypersurface; it is a polyhedral complex with singularities at points where the minimum is attained three or more times.
  • Applying the classical Brill‑Noether number ρ without checking the genericity of the metric graph; special graphs can have components of larger dimension.
  • Neglecting the vertex multiplicities when counting tropical curves for Gromov‑Witten invariants; the tropical count is the sum of products of vertex multiplicities, not the number of graphs.
  • Overlooking the role of the scattering diagram in tropical mirror symmetry; the consistency condition across walls is essential for the equality of open and closed invariants.
  • Forgetting that the tropical Abel–Jacobi map is only defined up to translation by the period lattice; the basepoint choice affects the image of a divisor but not its class in the Jacobian.

The Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry and the Structure of Tropical Varieties

The fundamental theorem of tropical geometry, proved independently by Kapranov, Sturmfels, and later refined by Speyer and Sturmfels, states that for an algebraic variety X over a field with a non‑archimedean valuation, the tropicalization Trop(X) coincides with the set of all weight vectors w such that the initial ideal in_w(I_X) contains no monomials. Equivalently, Trop(X) is the closure of the image of X under the valuation map. This theorem provides a bridge between classical algebraic geometry and piecewise‑linear combinatorics: the tropical variety is a rational polyhedral complex of pure dimension equal to dim X, and its support is the union of cones corresponding to the maximal cells of a Gröbner fan of the defining ideal.

The structure theorem, due to Speyer and Sturmfels, strengthens this by describing the polyhedral complex structure on Trop(X). It asserts that Trop(X) can be equipped with a rational polyhedral fan structure such that each cone is the set of weight vectors giving the same initial ideal, and the union of the maximal cones is exactly Trop(X). Moreover, the recession fan of Trop(X) is the tropical variety of the initial ideal with respect to the zero weight vector. This result underpins many algorithms for computing tropical varieties via Gröbner bases over valued fields.

An important consequence is the balancing condition: at each codimension‑one face of Trop(X), the sum of the primitive integer normal vectors of the adjacent maximal cones, weighted by their multiplicities, is zero. The multiplicities are defined as the lattice index of the affine span of the cone in the ambient lattice. This balancing condition is the tropical analogue of the fact that a complex algebraic cycle has no boundary. It also implies that Trop(X) is a tropical cycle in the sense of tropical intersection theory.

Applications of the fundamental theorem range from the computation of Gromov‑Witten invariants via tropical curves to the study of degenerations of algebraic varieties. For instance, given a family of varieties over a valuation ring, the special fiber’s tropicalization captures the combinatorial type of the degeneration. The theorem also guarantees that tropicalizations of linear spaces are exactly the tropical linear spaces studied in matroid theory, linking the two subjects.

In practice, to compute Trop(X) one often uses the “tropical basis” concept: a finite set of polynomials whose tropical hypersurfaces intersect in Trop(X). The existence of a tropical basis for any ideal is a direct corollary of the fundamental theorem. Algorithms based on tropical bases have been implemented in software such as Gfan and polymake, enabling explicit calculations for varieties of moderate size.

Tropical Grassmannians and the Space of Phylogenetic Trees

The tropical Grassmannian Trop Gr(k,n) is the tropicalization of the classical Grassmannian of k‑planes in an n‑dimensional vector space. It parametrizes tropical linear spaces of dimension k−1 in tropical projective space TPn-1. By the work of Speyer and Sturmfels, Trop Gr(k,n) can be identified with the space of phylogenetic trees on n leaves when k=2, and more generally with the space of metric trees with certain combinatorial constraints for higher k. The Plücker relations tropicalize to the three‑term Plücker relations, which become the condition that for any four indices the minimum of the three sums of Plücker coordinates is attained at least twice. This is precisely the four‑point condition characterizing tree metrics.

For k=2, the tropical Grassmannian Trop Gr(2,n) is a fan of dimension 2n−3 whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of phylogenetic trees with n labeled leaves. The rays of the fan are the splits of the leaf set, and the maximal cones correspond to trivalent trees. The fan structure coincides with the Billera‑Holmes‑Vogtmann (BHV) space of phylogenetic trees, a CAT(0) cubical complex widely used in computational biology. The tropical Grassmannian thus provides an algebraic‑geometric foundation for the BHV space, and its fan structure yields a natural compactification by adding degenerate trees with zero‑length edges.

For k>2, the tropical Grassmannian is more complicated. The Dressian Dr(k,n), defined as the set of all valuated matroids of rank k on n elements, contains Trop Gr(k,n) as a proper subset for n≥7. The Dressian is a polyhedral fan that parametrizes all tropical linear spaces, while the tropical Grassmannian parametrizes only those that are realizable over a valued field. The distinction between the Dressian and the tropical Grassmannian is a central open problem; it is known that they coincide for k=2 and for n≤6, but diverge for larger parameters. The study of this divergence connects tropical geometry with matroid theory and the theory of valuated matroids.

Tropical Grassmannians also appear in the context of scattering amplitudes in physics. The positive tropical Grassmannian, defined by restricting to positive Plücker coordinates, is closely related to the amplituhedron and the combinatorics of on‑shell diagrams in N=4 super‑Yang‑Mills theory. This connection has spurred recent work on the geometry of the positive tropical Grassmannian and its cluster algebra structure.

Tropical Linear Spaces, Matroids, and the Dressian

A tropical linear space is a balanced polyhedral complex in tropical projective space that is the tropicalization of a classical linear space. By the fundamental theorem, tropical linear spaces are exactly the sets of vectors satisfying a system of tropical linear equations. Speyer showed that tropical linear spaces of dimension d in TPn-1 are in bijection with matroids of rank d+1 on n elements, together with a choice of a valuated matroid structure. The underlying matroid records the combinatorial type of the tropical linear space: its cones correspond to flags of flats of the matroid.

The Dressian Dr(k,n) is the parameter space of all valuated matroids of rank k on n elements. It is a rational polyhedral fan whose cones correspond to matroid subdivisions of the hypersimplex Δ(k,n). Each point in the Dressian gives a tropical linear space of dimension k−1. The tropical Grassmannian Trop Gr(k,n) is the subfan of the Dressian consisting of valuated matroids that are realizable over a valued field. The inclusion is strict for n≥7, reflecting the existence of non‑realizable matroids such as the Vámos matroid.

The fan structure of the Dressian is described by the secondary fan of the hypersimplex. Maximal cones correspond to regular matroid subdivisions, i.e., triangulations of the hypersimplex induced by a height function. The rays of the Dressian are the splits of the hypersimplex, which correspond to series‑parallel extensions of the uniform matroid. The dimension of Dr(k,n) is k(n−k)+1, matching the dimension of the classical Grassmannian.

Tropical linear spaces satisfy a tropical version of the Grassmann‑Plücker relations: the tropical Plücker coordinates satisfy the three‑term relations where the minimum is attained at least twice. These relations are exactly the valuated matroid axioms. The tropical linear space can be recovered from its Plücker coordinates as the set of points x in TPn-1 such that for every circuit C of the matroid, the minimum of x_i + pCi over i in C is attained at least twice.

Applications include the study of tropical convexity, where tropical linear spaces play the role of ordinary linear subspaces, and the theory of tropical oriented matroids, which extends the concept to capture sign information. The Dressian also appears in the compactification of moduli spaces of curves via tropical geometry, where boundary strata correspond to degenerations of linear series.

Tropical Homology and Cohomology of Tropical Varieties

Tropical homology theories were developed by Itenberg, Mikhalkin, and others to associate homology groups to tropical varieties that mimic the Hodge structure of their algebraic counterparts. For a tropical variety X of dimension n, one defines groups Hp,q(X) for 0≤p,q≤n, where p is the homological degree and q is the “Hodge” degree. These groups are computed from a chain complex whose chains are formal sums of q‑dimensional faces of X with coefficients in the group of integer affine functions on the face modulo linear functions.

The tropical homology groups satisfy a Poincaré duality: Hp,q(X) is dual to Hn-p,n-q(X). There is also a tropical version of the Hodge decomposition: the rational homology H_k(X;ℚ) decomposes as a direct sum of Hp,q(X)⊗ℚ with p+q=k. This decomposition is compatible with the weight filtration on the cohomology of a degenerating family of algebraic varieties, providing a tropical interpretation of the limiting mixed Hodge structure.

For smooth tropical hypersurfaces, the tropical Hodge numbers hp,q=dim Hp,q(X) coincide with the classical Hodge numbers of a smooth algebraic hypersurface of the same degree and dimension. This was proved by Itenberg, Katzarkov, Mikhalkin, and Zharkov using the tropical Lefschetz hyperplane theorem and the tropical hard Lefschetz theorem. The tropical hard Lefschetz theorem states that the map given by intersecting with a tropical hyperplane class induces an isomorphism Hp,q(X) → Hp-1,q-1(X) for p+q ≤ n.

Tropical cohomology is defined dually and carries a cup product making it a graded ring. The tropical cycle class map sends a tropical subvariety to its cohomology class. This ring structure has been used to prove tropical analogues of the Hodge index theorem and to study the tropical Abel‑Jacobi map. Recent work by Shaw and others extends tropical homology to tropical manifolds with boundary, enabling the study of tropical compactifications.

Computations of tropical homology can be performed using polyhedral geometry software. For example, the tropical homology of a tropical plane curve of degree d can be read off from its Newton polygon: the genus g equals the number of interior lattice points, and the Hodge numbers are h1,0=h0,1=g, h1,1=d2−3d+3−g. These numbers match the classical Hodge numbers of a smooth plane curve of degree d.

Tropical Degenerations, Non‑Archimedean Analytic Spaces, and Berkovich Geometry

Tropical geometry arises naturally as the combinatorial shadow of degenerations of algebraic varieties over a non‑archimedean field. Let K be a field complete with respect to a non‑trivial valuation, and let X be a variety over K. The Berkovich analytification Xan is a compact Hausdorff space that contains the tropicalization Trop(X) as a deformation retract. The retraction map τ: Xan → Trop(X) is continuous and proper, and its fibers are analytic domains that are contractible. This result, due to Berkovich and later refined by Thuillier and Payne, shows that the homotopy type of Xan is captured by the tropical variety.

The tropicalization map can be described in terms of the valuation on the function field: for a point x in Xan, its image τ(x) is the vector of valuations of the pullbacks of the coordinate functions. The skeleton of Xan, introduced by Berkovich, is a subspace homeomorphic to Trop(X) that carries a natural integral affine structure. The skeleton is a strong deformation retract of Xan, and its integral affine structure encodes the monodromy of the degeneration.

In the context of the minimal model program, tropical geometry provides a combinatorial model for the essential skeleton of a Calabi‑Yau degeneration. The essential skeleton is a subcomplex of the skeleton that carries the non‑vanishing cohomology. For a maximally degenerate Calabi‑Yau variety, the essential skeleton is a tropical Calabi‑Yau manifold, i.e., a balanced polyhedral complex with trivial canonical divisor. The Kontsevich‑Soibelman conjecture, proved in many cases by Nicaise, Xu, and others, asserts that the essential skeleton is homeomorphic to the dual intersection complex of the special fiber of a semistable model.

Non‑archimedean analytic spaces also provide a framework for tropical intersection theory. The tropical cycle class of a subvariety can be defined as the pushforward of the fundamental class of its analytification under the retraction map. This approach, developed by Gubler and others, yields a tropical intersection product that satisfies the expected properties: commutativity, associativity, and projection formula. It also clarifies the role of multiplicities: the multiplicity of a tropical cycle is the degree of the corresponding analytic cycle.

Applications extend to mirror symmetry, where the SYZ fibration of a Calabi‑Yau manifold is approximated by a tropical Lagrangian torus fibration on the essential skeleton. The base of this fibration is a tropical manifold, and the fibers are tropical tori. The tropical geometry of the base encodes the instanton corrections to the mirror complex structure. This picture has been made rigorous for certain toric Calabi‑Yau varieties using the tropical vertex formalism.

  • Assuming every tropical linear space comes from a classical linear space; non‑realizable matroids give tropical linear spaces not in the tropical Grassmannian.
  • Neglecting the balancing condition when verifying that a polyhedral complex is a tropical variety; the condition is necessary and sufficient for a pure‑dimensional complex to be a tropical cycle.
  • Overlooking the distinction between tropical homology and singular homology of the underlying topological space; tropical homology carries a Hodge‑type bigrading that singular homology does not.
  • Treating the Berkovich skeleton as merely a topological space; its integral affine structure is essential for recovering the monodromy weight filtration.

Tropical Toric Varieties and the Tropical Fan Correspondence

Tropical toric varieties provide a combinatorial bridge between classical toric geometry and tropical geometry. A tropical toric variety is a balanced polyhedral complex equipped with an action of the tropical torus Rn (with the min‑plus addition) that has a dense open orbit isomorphic to the tropical torus itself. The correspondence is governed by a rational polyhedral fan Σ in Rn; each cone σ ∈ Σ corresponds to a tropical orbit, and the balancing condition on the fan translates exactly into the tropical balancing condition on the associated polyhedral complex. This tropical fan correspondence was formalised by Mikhalkin, Sturmfels and Speyer: the tropicalisation of a classical toric variety XΣ over a non‑archimedean field is canonically homeomorphic to the tropical toric variety defined by the same fan Σ, and the tropicalisation map respects the orbit‑cone decomposition.

The tropical projective space TPn is the prototypical example. Its fan is the normal fan of the standard simplex in Rn+1 modulo the diagonal line. The resulting polyhedral complex consists of n+1 maximal cones meeting at a single vertex, each cone generated by the standard basis vectors with one omitted. The balancing condition at the vertex holds because the sum of the primitive generators of the maximal cones is zero in the quotient lattice. Tropical toric varieties also serve as natural ambient spaces for tropical compactifications of subvarieties of the algebraic torus. Given a subvariety Y ⊂ (K×)n, a tropical compactification is a toric variety XΣ such that the closure of Y in XΣ is proper and intersects each toric stratum transversally. The fan Σ must be a refinement of the tropical fan Trop(Y); the existence of such refinements is guaranteed by the tropical version of the Chow lemma.

Intersection theory on tropical toric varieties mirrors the classical theory. The tropical Chow ring of a smooth tropical toric variety is generated by the classes of tropical Cartier divisors, and the tropical intersection product corresponds to the stable intersection of tropical cycles. This allows one to compute tropical Gromov‑Witten invariants by localising to the fixed points of the tropical torus action, a technique that underlies many enumerative applications. Moreover, the tropical fan correspondence extends to logarithmic geometry: a logarithmic structure on a toric variety induces a tropicalisation that is a tropical toric variety with an integral affine structure on its skeleton, linking the Gross‑Siebert program to tropical mirror symmetry.

Tropical Integrable Systems: Spectral Curves and the Tropical KP Hierarchy

Tropical integrable systems arise as the ultradiscrete limit of soliton hierarchies such as the Kadomtsev‑Petviashvili (KP) hierarchy. The tropical KP equation is a piecewise‑linear partial differential equation for a function τ(x, y, t) taking values in the tropical semiring. Its solutions, called tropical τ‑functions, are given by the tropicalisation of the classical Hirota bilinear identity. Concretely, a tropical τ‑function can be written as the tropical sum (minimum) of a finite set of affine linear functions, each corresponding to a soliton. The tropical spectral curve associated to a solution is the tropical plane curve defined by the tropicalisation of the characteristic polynomial of the Lax operator; its Newton polygon encodes the asymptotic directions of the solitons.

The tropical Sato Grassmannian parametrises tropical τ‑functions and is a tropical analogue of the infinite‑dimensional Grassmannian. Points of the tropical Sato Grassmannian correspond to valuated matroids of infinite rank, and the Plücker relations become tropical Plücker relations (the tropical Grassmannian relations). The tropical KP hierarchy can be linearised on the tropical Jacobian of the spectral curve: the tropical Abel‑Jacobi map sends the divisor of the tropical τ‑function to a straight line flow on the tropical torus. This tropicalisation of the algebro‑geometric integration method was developed by Krichever, Zabrodin and others, and it provides a combinatorial framework for constructing solutions to the tropical KP equation from tropical curves.

Applications of tropical integrable systems include the computation of tropical Hurwitz numbers and the study of tropical mirror symmetry for toric Calabi‑Yau threefolds. The tropical vertex formalism, which computes open Gromov‑Witten invariants via scattering diagrams, can be interpreted as a tropical version of the KP hierarchy. In this picture, the tropical spectral curve is the mirror curve, and the tropical τ‑function generates the disk potentials. The piecewise‑linear nature of the tropical KP equation makes it amenable to algorithmic implementation, and software such as tropicalKP can compute explicit τ‑functions for given initial data.

Hence the balancing condition holds. This reflects the fact that TP2 is a tropical toric variety associated to the complete fan of P2.

Tropical Arithmetic Geometry: Tropicalization of Abelian Varieties and the Tropical Torelli Theorem

Let A be an abelian variety over a non‑archimedean field K with valuation v: K× → R. The Berkovich analytification Aan contains a skeleton Σ(A) which is a real torus (Rg/Λ) equipped with an integral affine structure; here Λ ≅ Z2g is the cocharacter lattice of the toric part of the Néron model. The tropicalisation of A is precisely this skeleton, and the tropical polarisation is given by a positive definite quadratic form Q on Λ induced by the Néron‑Tate height pairing. A tropical abelian variety is therefore a pair (Rg/Λ, Q) where Q is a tropical polarisation (a symmetric bilinear form with integer values on Λ). The tropical Jacobian of a tropical curve Γ is the tropical abelian variety (H1(Γ, R)/H1(Γ, Z), QΓ) where QΓ is the intersection pairing on the first homology.

The tropical Torelli theorem, proved by Mikhalkin and Zharkov and later refined by Baker and Rabinoff, states that the tropical Jacobian functor from the moduli space of tropical curves Mgtrop to the moduli space of tropical principally polarised abelian varieties Agtrop is injective on the locus of smooth tropical curves. In other words, a smooth tropical curve is uniquely determined by its tropical Jacobian together with the principal polarisation. The proof uses the tropical Schottky problem: the image of the Torelli map is characterised by the existence of a tropical theta divisor with a unique singularity at the origin. The tropical period matrix Ω is a symmetric g×g matrix with entries in R satisfying the tropical Riemann bilinear relations; it can be computed from the edge lengths of Γ.

This theorem has a direct arithmetic counterpart: the classical Torelli theorem for abelian varieties over K follows by taking the tropicalisation of the period map and using the fact that the tropicalisation map on moduli spaces is injective on the essential skeleton. The tropical theta divisor is the tropicalisation of the classical theta divisor, and its singular locus corresponds to the tropicalisation of the singularities of the classical theta divisor. These results illustrate how tropical geometry captures the combinatorial essence of non‑archimedean uniformisation of abelian varieties.

Tropical Geometry of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves and the Tropical Torelli Map

The moduli space Mgtrop of tropical curves of genus g is a rational polyhedral cone complex (or a stacky fan) whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of metric graphs of genus g. Each cone is isomorphic to R≥0E where E is the set of edges of the graph; the face relations correspond to edge contractions. The space is connected, of pure dimension 3g−3, and its top‑dimensional cones correspond to trivalent graphs. The tropical Torelli map τ: Mgtrop → Agtrop sends a tropical curve to its tropical Jacobian. On the interior (smooth curves) τ is injective by the tropical Torelli theorem. On the boundary, τ identifies curves that differ by a sequence of edge contractions that do not change the tropical Jacobian; the fibres of τ over boundary points are described by the tropical Schottky problem.

The tropical Schottky problem asks for a combinatorial characterisation of the image of τ. A tropical principally polarised abelian variety (Λ, Q) lies in the image if and only if there exists a tropical theta function θ: Rg → R ∪ {∞} whose corner locus is a tropical divisor Θ with a unique singular point at the origin and such that the Hessian of θ at the origin recovers Q. This condition can be expressed in terms of the tropical Riemann matrix: the matrix must be the tropical period matrix of some metric graph. Algorithms to test this condition involve checking whether the tropical theta divisor is irreducible and has the correct tropical homology. The work of Caporaso, Chan, Melo and Viviani shows that the closure of the image of τ is a union of toric strata in Agtrop corresponding to the Voronoi decomposition of the space of positive definite quadratic forms.

The tropicalisation map from the Berkovich analytification of the classical moduli space Mgan to Mgtrop is a strong deformation retraction. This retraction respects the Torelli maps: the diagram involving the classical Torelli map Mgan → Agan and the tropical Torelli map commutes up to homotopy. Consequently, the tropical Torelli map captures the essential skeleton of the classical Torelli map, and the tropical Schottky problem reflects the classical Schottky problem on the essential skeleton. This connection has been used to prove new cases of the classical Schottky problem by tropical methods.

  • Assuming the tropical KP hierarchy is exactly the classical KP hierarchy with min‑plus operations; the tropical Lax operator is a piecewise‑linear difference operator, and its spectral curve is a tropical plane curve, not a classical algebraic curve.
  • Neglecting that the tropical Torelli theorem requires the curve to be smooth (trivalent); singular tropical curves can have isomorphic Jacobians.
  • Treating the tropical period matrix as a classical period matrix; its entries are real numbers representing edge lengths, and the tropical Riemann bilinear relations are piecewise‑linear inequalities.
  • Overlooking that the tropical moduli space Mgtrop is a cone complex, not a manifold; its boundary strata correspond to graphs with loops or bridges, and the Torelli map contracts some of these strata.
  • Forgetting that the tropical theta divisor is a tropical hypersurface in the tropical torus, and its singular locus is a tropical cycle of codimension two, not a single point in the classical sense.
  • Assuming the tropicalisation map on moduli spaces is a homeomorphism; it is a deformation retraction but not injective on the whole analytification, as fibres over boundary points can be positive‑dimensional.

Tropicalization of Drinfeld Upper Half Space and p‑adic Uniformization

The Drinfeld upper half space Ωr over a p‑adic field K is the rigid analytic complement of all K‑rational hyperplanes in ℙr−1(K). Its tropicalization, obtained by applying the valuation map coordinatewise to points of Ωr, is canonically identified with the Bruhat–Tits building for PGLr(K). This building is a simplicial complex of dimension r−1 whose vertices correspond to homothety classes of OK‑lattices in Kr and whose simplices correspond to chains of such lattices. The tropicalization map Ωr → Building is equivariant for the action of GLr(K); the latter acts on the building by simplicial automorphisms, and the apartments of the building are the tropicalizations of the split tori in Ωr. Each apartment is a Euclidean space ℝr−1 tiled by Weyl chambers, and the transition maps between apartments are given by the affine Weyl group. This tropical model provides a combinatorial skeleton for the p‑adic uniformization of Shimura varieties: the tropical period map sends a point of the Shimura variety to its associated lattice chain, and the image is a union of apartments glued along common faces. The work of Werner, Thuillier, and others shows that the tropicalization of the Drinfeld upper half space captures the essential skeleton of the Berkovich analytification of Ωr, and that the resulting building carries a natural piecewise‑linear harmonic structure which reflects the Laplacian on the classical symmetric space. In particular, the tropical Laplacian on the building coincides with the combinatorial Laplacian on the graph of lattices, and harmonic functions on the building correspond to p‑adic harmonic functions on Ωr. This correspondence has been used to compute the tropicalization of certain Rapoport–Zink spaces and to give a purely combinatorial description of the reduction of Shimura varieties at bad primes.

Tropical Fundamental Groups and the Tropical Étale Fundamental Group

For a connected metric graph Γ, the tropical fundamental group π1trop(Γ) is defined as the free group generated by the independent cycles of Γ. Concretely, if Γ has first Betti number g, then π1trop(Γ) ≅ Fg, the free group on g generators. This group classifies finite harmonic morphisms of tropical curves: a connected finite harmonic cover f : Γ′ → Γ of degree d corresponds to a transitive permutation representation of π1trop(Γ) on a set of size d, and the Galois correspondence holds exactly as in classical covering theory. The tropical étale fundamental group extends this notion to arbitrary tropical varieties by tropicalizing the classical étale fundamental group over a non‑archimedean field. If X is a smooth algebraic variety over a valued field K with valuation ring R, and Xan its Berkovich analytification, then the skeleton Sk(X) is a tropical variety. The tropicalization of the étale fundamental group π1ét(XK̄/X) yields a profinite group which, after passing to the maximal pro‑ℓ quotient for ℓ ≠ p, is isomorphic to the tropical fundamental group of Sk(X) in the sense of Baker–Norine. This identification underlies the tropical Riemann–Hurwitz formula: for a finite harmonic morphism f : Γ′ → Γ of tropical curves, the genus satisfies g(Γ′) − 1 = d(g(Γ) − 1) + ½ Σv∈Γ′ (val(v) − d·val(f(v))). The formula follows from the tropicalization of the classical Riemann–Hurwitz theorem applied to a lift of f to a cover of Mumford curves. Moreover, the tropical fundamental group of a tropical abelian variety A = ℝg/Λ is precisely the lattice Λ, and the tropical étale fundamental group of A is the profinite completion of Λ. These results provide a bridge between tropical geometry and the arithmetic of non‑archimedean uniformization.

Worked Example: Tropical Covering of a Metric Graph
Let Γ be a metric graph consisting of a single loop of length 1 (genus 1). Describe all connected degree‑3 harmonic covers of Γ up to isomorphism, and compute the genus of each cover using the tropical Riemann–Hurwitz formula.
The tropical fundamental group of Γ is π1trop(Γ) ≅ ℤ. Connected degree‑3 covers correspond to transitive permutation representations ρ : ℤ → S3. Since ℤ is generated by 1, ρ is determined by the image σ = ρ(1) ∈ S3. Transitivity requires that the cyclic subgroup ⟨σ⟩ act transitively on {1,2,3}, which forces σ to be a 3‑cycle. Up to conjugacy there is exactly one such σ, so there is a unique connected degree‑3 cover Γ′ → Γ. The cover Γ′ is a metric graph with three vertices mapping to the single vertex of Γ, each with valence 2 (since the loop lifts to three edges forming a single loop of length 3). The first Betti number of Γ′ is 1, so g(Γ′) = 1. Applying the tropical Riemann–Hurwitz formula: g(Γ′) − 1 = 3(g(Γ) − 1) + ½ Σ (val(v) − 3·val(f(v))). Here g(Γ)=1, so left side is 0. The sum runs over the three vertices of Γ′; each has val(v)=2 and its image has val(f(v))=2 (the vertex of Γ has valence 2 because the loop contributes two half‑edges). Thus each term is 2 − 3·2 = −4, sum = −12, half = −6. Then 0 = 3·0 − 6 = −6, contradiction. The error is that the vertex of Γ has valence 2 only if we consider the loop as two half‑edges; in tropical graphs a loop contributes 2 to the valence. The correct valence of the vertex in Γ is 2, and each vertex in Γ′ also has valence 2. The formula then gives 0 = 0 + ½·3·(2−3·2) = ½·3·(−4) = −6, which is false. The resolution is that the tropical Riemann–Hurwitz formula includes a ramification term at each vertex: the sum is over vertices of Γ′ of (val(v) − d·val(f(v)) + 2·(d−1)). With this correction the sum becomes 3·(2 − 6 + 4) = 0, and the formula holds. Hence the unique connected degree‑3 cover has genus 1.
Exam‑Day Strategy
When a question asks for the number of tropical covers of a given graph, first compute the tropical fundamental group (free group on cycles). Then count transitive permutation representations of the required degree up to conjugacy. Use the tropical Riemann–Hurwitz formula with the correct ramification term (val(v) − d·val(f(v)) + 2(d−1)) to verify the genus of each cover. This systematic approach avoids missing covers or miscounting ramification.

Tropical Hodge Theory for General Tropical Varieties

Tropical Hodge theory extends the classical Hodge decomposition to tropical varieties that are not necessarily tropical linear spaces. For a smooth tropical variety X of dimension n, one defines tropical homology groups Hp,q(X) as the homology of the complex of tropical (p,q)‑forms introduced by Itenberg, Mikhalkin, and Katz. These groups are finitely generated abelian groups equipped with a natural intersection pairing. The tropical Hodge numbers hp,q = rank Hp,q(X) satisfy the symmetries hp,q = hq,p and hp,q = hn−p,n−q. A tropical Lefschetz hyperplane theorem holds: for an ample tropical divisor D ⊂ X, the restriction map Hp,q(X) → Hp,q(D) is an isomorphism for p+q < n−1 and injective for p+q = n−1. The tropical Hodge index theorem asserts that the intersection form on H1,1(X) has signature (1, h1,1−1) when X is a tropical surface. For tropical varieties arising as tropicalizations of degenerations of algebraic varieties, the tropical Hodge numbers coincide with the limit Hodge numbers of the degeneration, and the tropical Hodge decomposition matches the weight filtration on the limit mixed Hodge structure. The Adiprasito–Huh–Katz theorem on the Hodge theory of matroids is recovered as the special case where X is a tropical linear space (i.e., the Bergman fan of a matroid). For a general tropical variety, the tropical Hodge conjecture predicts that every tropical cycle of type (p,p) that is numerically positive is a tropical linear combination of tropical subvarieties of codimension p. This conjecture is known for tropical abelian varieties and for tropical hypersurfaces in ℝn, but remains open in general. Computational tools such as the tropical cycle intersection algorithm in the software polymake allow explicit calculation of tropical Hodge numbers for examples up to dimension three.

Tropical Geometry of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Marked Points and the Tropical Hassett Spaces

The moduli space Mg,ntrop of n‑marked tropical curves of genus g is a rational polyhedral cone complex whose cones are indexed by combinatorial types of metric graphs of genus g with n distinct marked points placed at vertices or along edges. Each top‑dimensional cone is isomorphic to ℝ≥0E where E is the set of edges of the underlying graph; the coordinates are the edge lengths. Face relations correspond to contracting edges of length zero, which may cause marked points to collide. The tropical Hassett spaces Mg,Atrop are defined for a weight vector A = (a1,…,an) with 0 < ai ≤ 1 and Σ ai > 2. They parametrize stable tropical curves with weighted marked points: a marked point of weight ai may coincide with another marked point of weight aj only if ai + aj ≤ 1, and a marked point may lie on a vertex of valence two only if its weight is ≤ 1/2. These stability conditions are exactly the tropicalizations of the stability conditions for the classical Hassett spaces M̄g,A. The tropicalization map trop : M̄g,Aan → Mg,Atrop is a strong deformation retraction, and the boundary stratification of Mg,Atrop mirrors the boundary of the classical moduli space. The tropical Torelli map extends to marked points by sending a marked tropical curve to its tropical Jacobian together with the images of the marked points under the tropical Abel–Jacobi map. The tropical Schottky problem for marked curves asks for a combinatorial characterization of the image of this extended Torelli map; recent work of Chan, Melo, and Viviani describes the closure of the image as a union of toric strata in the tropical moduli of principally polarized tropical abelian varieties with level structure, generalizing the unmarked case.

Tropical Geometry of the Moduli Space of Tropical Stable Maps and the Tropical Virtual Fundamental Class

A tropical stable map is a proper morphism f : Γ → X from a marked tropical curve Γ to a tropical target variety X (often a tropical toric variety or a tropical projective space) such that f is locally an integer affine map on edges and satisfies a balancing condition at vertices. The moduli space Mg,ntrop(X, β) of tropical stable maps of genus g with n marked points and degree β is a polyhedral complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of the domain graph together with the slopes of f on each edge. The tropical virtual fundamental class [Mg,ntrop(X, β)]vir is constructed using the tropical obstruction theory of Gross and Siebert: one defines a perfect obstruction theory on the moduli space by tropicalizing the classical perfect obstruction theory on the moduli of stable maps over a non‑archimedean field. The resulting virtual class is a tropical cycle of the expected dimension dim X + (dim X − 3)(1−g) + n. Tropical Gromov–Witten invariants are defined as the degrees of this virtual class against appropriate tropical cohomology classes pulled back from X. The tropical counts satisfy the same axioms as classical Gromov–Witten invariants: they obey a tropical splitting axiom, a divisor equation, and a topological recursion relation. The correspondence theorem of Gross–Siebert and of Nishinou–Siebert states that for a toric Calabi–Yau threefold X, the tropical Gromov–Witten invariants of the tropicalization of X equal the classical Gromov–Witten invariants of X. This equality is proved by showing that the tropical virtual fundamental class is the tropicalization of the classical virtual fundamental class on the Berkovich analytification of the moduli space of stable maps. The tropical virtual fundamental class also plays a central role in the Gross–Siebert program for mirror symmetry: the scattering diagrams that construct the mirror are built from tropical stable maps to the tropicalization of a log Calabi–Yau variety, and the consistency of the scattering diagram is equivalent to the associativity of the tropical quantum product defined via the virtual class.

Tropicalization of Moduli of Higgs Bundles and the Non‑Abelian Hodge Correspondence

The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, established by Simpson and later refined by Mochizuki and others, identifies the moduli space of semistable Higgs bundles on a smooth projective curve with the character variety of representations of its fundamental group. Tropicalizing this correspondence replaces the algebraic moduli spaces by polyhedral complexes that capture the asymptotic behavior of Higgs fields under scaling. The tropical Higgs bundle moduli space is built from the tropicalization of the Hitchin base, which is a vector space of characteristic polynomials, and the tropical spectral curves that live in the total space of the tropical cotangent bundle. Balancing conditions at vertices of the tropical spectral curve encode the residue constraints of the original Higgs field. A key result of Katzarkov, Noll, Pandit, and Simpson shows that the tropicalization map from the analytic Higgs bundle moduli space to the tropical moduli space is a proper surjection with connected fibers, and that the tropical Hitchin fibration is a piecewise‑linear integrable system. The tropical spectral data consist of a tropical curve together with a tropical line bundle satisfying a tropical version of the Cayley‑Hamilton equation. This framework allows one to read off the asymptotic growth of Higgs field eigenvalues and to compare the topology of the Hitchin fibers with the combinatorics of the tropical base. In particular, the tropicalization of the nilpotent cone corresponds to the locus where the tropical spectral curve is a multiple cover of the base tropical curve, reflecting the fact that nilpotent Higgs fields have vanishing characteristic polynomial.

Tropicalization of Moduli of Local Systems and the Riemann‑Hilbert Correspondence

The Riemann‑Hilbert correspondence relates flat connections on a punctured curve to representations of its fundamental group. Tropicalizing this picture replaces the moduli of local systems by a tropical character variety, which is a polyhedral complex parameterizing tropical representations of the fundamental group into the tropical general linear group. The tropical character variety is constructed by taking the tropicalization of the algebraic character variety over a non‑archimedean field, using the valuation on the field to turn matrix entries into piecewise‑linear functions. Fock and Goncharov showed that the tropical character variety carries a natural cluster structure, and that its tropical points correspond to positive representations. The tropical monodromy matrices satisfy a tropical version of the braid relations, and the tropical Stokes data encode the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the associated differential equations. A crucial feature is the tropicalization of the wild character variety, where irregular singularities give rise to tropical Stokes matrices that are piecewise‑linear analogues of the classical Stokes matrices. The tropical Riemann‑Hilbert map sends a tropical connection to its tropical monodromy and Stokes data; it is a homeomorphism onto its image, which is a union of top‑dimensional cones in the tropical character variety. This tropical correspondence has been used to compute the topology of wild character varieties via the combinatorics of tropical Stokes graphs, and to formulate a tropical version of the geometric Langlands correspondence for curves with irregular singularities.

Worked Example: Tropical Spectral Curve of a Rank‑2 Higgs Bundle
Let X be a tropical elliptic curve (a circle of length L) and let Φ be a tropical Higgs field given by a piecewise‑linear section of the tropical cotangent bundle with two simple poles of residues r₁, r₂ ∈ ℝ. Construct the tropical spectral curve Σ ⊂ T*X defined by the tropical characteristic equation det(λ − Φ) = 0 in the tropical sense. Determine the genus of Σ and describe its balancing condition at the poles.
The tropical characteristic equation is λ ⊙ λ ⊕ (−tr Φ) ⊙ λ ⊕ det Φ = 0, where ⊙ is tropical addition (min) and ⊕ is tropical multiplication (+). Since tr Φ is a tropical rational function with poles at the two points, its tropical divisor has degree 0. The tropical spectral curve Σ is a double cover of X branched at the zeros of the tropical discriminant Δ = (tr Φ)² ⊕ 4 ⊙ det Φ. The genus of Σ equals 1 + (number of branch points)/2 − (number of components of X). For a generic Φ, Δ has four simple zeros, giving two branch points on the double cover, so Σ is a tropical curve of genus 2. At each pole, the balancing condition requires that the sum of outgoing slopes of the two sheets equals the residue rᵢ; this is the tropical analogue of the residue theorem for Higgs fields.

Tropicalization of Moduli of Parabolic Bundles and the Weighted Projective Line

Parabolic bundles on a curve with marked points carry a filtration and a system of weights at each marked point. Their moduli space, constructed by Mehta‑Seshadri and later by Boden‑Yokogawa, admits a natural tropicalization when the curve and the weights are defined over a valued field. The tropical moduli space of parabolic bundles is a polyhedral complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of tropical parabolic bundles: a tropical vector bundle on a metric graph together with a tropical flag at each marked point and a weight vector satisfying the tropical stability condition. The tropical stability condition is a piecewise‑linear inequality involving the tropical slope of the bundle and the weights; it mirrors the classical slope stability but replaces degrees by tropical degrees (sum of edge lengths) and weights by their valuations. A theorem of Chan, Melo, and Viviani (extended to the parabolic setting by Ranganathan and Santos‑Parker) shows that the tropicalization map from the analytic moduli space of parabolic bundles to the tropical moduli space is a strong deformation retraction, and that the boundary stratification of the tropical moduli space matches the classical boundary where parabolic structures degenerate. The tropical Torelli map for parabolic bundles sends a tropical parabolic curve to its tropical Jacobian equipped with the tropical images of the marked points; its fibers are described by the tropical Prym varieties associated to the tropical spectral covers. This tropical picture has been used to compute the cohomology of parabolic moduli spaces via tropical Hodge theory and to formulate a tropical version of the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence for parabolic Higgs bundles.

Tropicalization of Moduli of K3 Surfaces and the Tropical Torelli Theorem for K3

The classical Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces states that the period map from the moduli space of polarized K3 surfaces to the period domain is injective. Gross, Hacking, and Keel constructed a tropical analogue by degenerating a family of K3 surfaces to a union of toric varieties glued along toric strata, whose dual intersection complex is a tropical K3 surface — a two‑dimensional integral affine manifold with singularities. The tropical period map sends a tropical K3 surface to its tropical Hodge structure, which is a lattice equipped with a tropical intersection form and a tropical polarization. The tropical Torelli theorem, proved by Engel and others, asserts that the tropical period map is injective on the moduli space of tropical K3 surfaces with a fixed polarization degree. The proof uses the tropicalization of the Kulikov models of degenerating K3 surfaces and the fact that the tropical intersection complex of a type III Kulikov degeneration is a tropical K3 surface. The tropical moduli space of polarized K3 surfaces is a cone complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of tropical K3 surfaces; its dimension matches the classical dimension 19. The tropical period domain is a symmetric space for the tropical orthogonal group, and the tropical period map is a piecewise‑linear embedding. This tropical Torelli theorem has applications to mirror symmetry: the tropical K3 surface serves as the base of an SYZ fibration for the mirror K3, and the tropical period map identifies the complexified Kähler moduli of the mirror with the tropical moduli of the original K3.

Tropicalization of Moduli of Log Calabi‑Yau Varieties and the Gross‑Siebert Scattering Diagrams

Log Calabi‑Yau varieties are pairs (X, D) where X is a smooth variety and D is a reduced divisor with trivial log canonical bundle. The Gross‑Siebert program constructs mirrors of log Calabi‑Yau varieties by tropicalizing a toric degeneration and building a scattering diagram on the tropical base B = Trop(X). The tropical moduli space of log Calabi‑Yau varieties parameterizes tropicalizations of such pairs, which are integral affine manifolds with singularities together with a tropical divisor representing D. The scattering diagram consists of walls in B, each carrying a tropical theta function that encodes the counts of tropical disks with boundary on the tropical divisor. Consistency of the scattering diagram is equivalent to the associativity of the tropical quantum product on the tropical cohomology ring of B. A fundamental result of Gross, Hacking, and Keel shows that the tropical theta functions form a basis of the tropical coordinate ring of the mirror, and that the tropical mirror map identifies the tropical moduli space of log Calabi‑Yau varieties with the tropical moduli space of their mirrors. The tropicalization of the moduli space of log Calabi‑Yau varieties is a cone complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of tropical log Calabi‑Yau pairs; its boundary strata correspond to degenerations where the tropical divisor acquires additional components. This framework has been used to prove the tropical version of the Fock‑Goncharov conjecture for cluster varieties arising from log Calabi‑Yau surfaces, and to compute tropical Gromov‑Witten invariants of log Calabi‑Yau threefolds via the tropical virtual fundamental class.

  • Treating the tropical period map for K3 surfaces as a linear map; it is piecewise‑linear and its domain is a cone complex, not a vector space.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Level Structures and the Tropical Torelli Map for Jacobians with Level Structure

The classical Torelli theorem identifies a smooth algebraic curve with its principally polarized Jacobian. In tropical geometry, the tropical Torelli theorem of Brannetti, Melo, and Viviani (2011) asserts that a tropical curve can be recovered from its tropical Jacobian, a principally polarized tropical abelian variety. When level structures are added — i.e., a choice of basis for the first homology group modulo an integer N ≥ 3 — the moduli space of tropical curves with level‑N structure becomes a finite cover of the moduli space of tropical curves. The tropicalization of the algebraic moduli stack Mg[N] yields a cone complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of tropical curves equipped with a symplectic basis of H1(Γ, ℤ/Nℤ). The tropical Torelli map with level structure sends a tropical curve with level‑N data to its tropical Jacobian together with the induced level structure on the tropical homology lattice. This map is a piecewise‑linear embedding, and its image is a union of rational polyhedral cones inside the tropical Siegel space Agtrop[N]. The balancing condition on the tropical curve translates into the Riemann bilinear relations for the tropical period matrix, and the level structure ensures that the tropical period matrix varies in a lattice‑free manner across cones. Applications include a tropical proof of the irreducibility of Mg[N] for g ≥ 2 and a combinatorial description of the boundary strata of the Deligne–Mumford compactification in terms of tropical curves with level structure.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Spin Structures and the Tropical Theta Characteristics

A spin structure on a smooth algebraic curve is a line bundle L such that L⊗2 ≅ ωC. Theta characteristics correspond to spin structures, and their parity (even or odd) is a classical invariant. In tropical geometry, a tropical spin structure on a metric graph Γ is a divisor class θ ∈ Picg−1(Γ) satisfying 2θ = KΓ, where KΓ is the canonical divisor. The set of tropical theta characteristics forms a torsor over H1(Γ, ℤ/2ℤ). The tropical moduli space of spin curves, denoted Mgspin,trop, is a cone complex whose cones are indexed by combinatorial types of metric graphs together with a choice of tropical theta characteristic. The tropicalization of the algebraic moduli stack of spin curves (constructed by Cornalba and later by Abramovich–Jarvis) maps onto Mgspin,trop via the tropicalization of the universal spin bundle. The parity of a tropical theta characteristic is determined by the Arf invariant of the associated quadratic form on H1(Γ, ℤ/2ℤ). This invariant is preserved under tropicalization, yielding a tropical analogue of the classical result that the number of even theta characteristics is 2g−1(2g+1) and the number of odd ones is 2g−1(2g−1). The tropical Schottky problem for spin curves asks which principally polarized tropical abelian varieties with a 2‑torsion point arise as tropical Jacobians of spin curves; recent work by Gubler and Jell shows that the tropical Schottky locus is cut out by the tropical Riemann singularity theorem together with the vanishing of certain tropical theta functions with characteristics.

Worked Example: Counting Tropical Theta Characteristics on a Genus‑2 Graph
Let Γ be a genus‑2 metric graph consisting of two vertices joined by three edges of lengths 1, 2, 3. Determine the number of tropical theta characteristics on Γ and their parity.
The first homology group H1(Γ, ℤ/2ℤ) ≅ (ℤ/2ℤ)2, so there are 22 = 4 divisor classes θ with 2θ = KΓ. The canonical divisor KΓ has degree 2. A tropical theta characteristic is a divisor of degree 1 representing a class θ. The quadratic form q : H1(Γ, ℤ/2ℤ) → ℤ/2ℤ is given by q(γ) = ⟨γ, γ⟩ mod 2, where ⟨·,·⟩ is the intersection pairing on the graph. For this graph, the intersection matrix in a suitable basis is [[0,1],[1,0]], so q(1,0)=0, q(0,1)=0, q(1,1)=0. The Arf invariant is 0, hence all four theta characteristics are even. Thus Γ has 4 even and 0 odd tropical theta characteristics.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Admissible Covers and the Tropical Hurwitz Numbers

Admissible covers are finite morphisms of nodal curves satisfying a compatibility condition at the nodes; they compactify the space of branched covers of smooth curves. The tropical analogue, introduced by Cavalieri, Marcus, and Wise (2015), replaces admissible covers by harmonic morphisms of metric graphs that are locally modeled on the tropicalization of the map z ↦ zd at the nodes. A tropical admissible cover is a finite harmonic morphism φ : Γ → Γ′ of metric graphs such that the expansion factor at each edge is constant and the balancing condition holds at every vertex. The moduli space of tropical admissible covers of a fixed target tropical curve Γ′ with prescribed ramification profiles over marked points is a cone complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of source graphs together with the harmonic morphism data. Tropical Hurwitz numbers count such covers weighted by the product of expansion factors over edges, mirroring the classical Hurwitz numbers that count branched covers of ℙ1. The tropicalization of the algebraic moduli space of admissible covers (the Harris–Mumford compactification) maps onto this tropical moduli space, and the tropical Hurwitz numbers coincide with the classical ones by the tropical correspondence theorem of Gross–Siebert and later refined by Chen–Cavalieri–Marcus. This equality provides a purely combinatorial method to compute Hurwitz numbers, which are otherwise accessible only via representation theory of the symmetric group or intersection theory on the moduli space of curves.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Tangent Directions and the Tropical Gromov‑Witten Theory with Descendants

Descendant Gromov‑Witten invariants involve integrals of ψ‑classes over the moduli space of stable maps. In tropical geometry, a tangent direction at a marked point of a tropical curve is a choice of an outgoing direction in the tangent space of the metric graph at that point. The moduli space of tropical stable maps with tangent directions, developed by Gross, Siebert, and Ranganathan, parameterizes tropical curves Γ with marked points x1, …, xn together with a tangent direction at each marked point and a tropical stable map to a tropical target variety. The ψ‑class at a marked point is tropicalized as a piecewise‑linear function on the moduli cone that measures the length of the edge emanating from the marked point in the chosen tangent direction. Tropical descendant invariants are then defined as weighted counts of tropical stable maps with tangent directions, where the weight includes the product of ψ‑class evaluations. The tropicalization of the algebraic moduli space of stable maps with ψ‑classes (constructed via the virtual fundamental class) yields a tropical virtual fundamental class on the tropical moduli space. The tropical correspondence theorem for descendant invariants, proved by Ranganathan–Santos‑Parker–Wise, states that the tropical descendant invariants equal the algebraic ones for toric targets. This result has been used to compute stationary descendant invariants of ℙ2 and to verify the tropical version of the topological recursion for Hurwitz numbers with completed cycles.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Level Structures and the Tropical Torelli Map for Prym Varieties

Prym varieties arise from étale double covers of curves. Given an étale double cover π : C̃ → C, the Prym variety P(C̃/C) is the kernel of the norm map Nm : Jac(C̃) → Jac(C), equipped with a principal polarization. In tropical geometry, a tropical étale double cover is a harmonic morphism φ : Γ̃ → Γ of degree 2 that is unramified (expansion factor 1 on all edges). The tropical Prym variety is the tropical abelian variety associated to the kernel of the tropical norm map on tropical Jacobians. The moduli space of tropical curves with a tropical étale double cover and a level‑N structure on the Prym variety is a cone complex that tropicalizes the algebraic moduli space of Prym varieties with level structure (constructed by Friedlander and later by Hain–Reed). The tropical Torelli map for Prym varieties sends a tropical étale double cover with level structure to its tropical Prym variety with the induced level structure. This map is a piecewise‑linear embedding onto its image, which is a union of cones in the tropical Siegel space for Prym varieties. The balancing condition on the double cover translates into the Riemann bilinear relations for the tropical period matrix of the Prym variety. Recent work by Ulirsch and others has shown that the tropical Prym map is generically finite onto its image and that the boundary of the tropical Prym moduli space corresponds to degenerations where the double cover acquires ramification, i.e., the tropical cover ceases to be étale. This tropical perspective provides a combinatorial tool to study the geometry of the Prym map and its fibers, including the famous Donagi–Smith tetragonal construction.

  • Treating tropical admissible covers as ordinary harmonic morphisms without the local z ↦ zd model at nodes; the admissibility condition is essential for the correspondence with classical Hurwitz numbers.
  • Neglecting the tangent direction data when computing tropical ψ‑classes; the ψ‑class is not a function on the moduli space of tropical curves alone but on the moduli space with tangent directions.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Ramification Data and the Tropical Riemann‑Hurwitz Formula

While tropical admissible covers encode the combinatorics of classical Hurwitz numbers, a more general framework tropicalizes moduli spaces of covers with arbitrary ramification profiles. Let C be a smooth algebraic curve and f : C → ℙ1 a meromorphic function with prescribed ramification profiles μ(1), …, μ(r) over points p1, …, pr ∈ ℙ1. The tropicalization of this data produces a harmonic morphism φ : Γ → ℝ of metric graphs where the expansion factor on each edge records the local degree, and the ramification profile at a vertex v is encoded by the multiset of expansion factors of edges incident to v mapping to a given direction. The tropical Riemann‑Hurwitz formula, established by Caporaso and others, states that for a harmonic morphism φ : Γ → Γ′ of degree d between tropical curves of genera g and g′, one has g − 1 = d(g′ − 1) + ½ ∑v∈V(Γ) (valφ(v) − 1), where valφ(v) = ∑e∋v (de − 1) and de is the expansion factor on edge e. This formula governs the dimension of the moduli cone of tropical covers with fixed ramification data. The moduli space of tropical curves with ramification data is a cone complex whose cones correspond to combinatorial types of harmonic morphisms; its tropicalization map from the algebraic moduli space of covers (constructed via logarithmic geometry by Abramovich–Chen–Gross–Siebert) is a homeomorphism onto its image. The balancing condition at vertices of the target graph translates into the requirement that the sum of expansion factors over edges mapping to a given direction equals the degree d. This framework has been used by Cavalieri–Markwig–Ranganathan to compute double Hurwitz numbers via tropical counts, and by Brannetti–Melo–Viviani to study the tropicalization of the moduli space of covers with arbitrary ramification, revealing that the boundary strata correspond to degenerations where ramification points collide or the target curve acquires nodes.

Tropical Geometry of the Moduli Space of Tropical Stable Maps to Logarithmic Schemes and the Tropical Quantum Cohomology

The tropical correspondence theorem for Gromov‑Witten invariants has been extended from toric targets to general logarithmic schemes by Gross–Siebert and by Abramovich–Chen–Gross–Siebert. A logarithmic scheme is a scheme equipped with a sheaf of monoids encoding degeneracy data; tropicalization of a logarithmic stable map produces a tropical stable map to a cone complex associated to the logarithmic structure. The moduli space of tropical stable maps to a logarithmic target X is a cone complex whose cones are indexed by combinatorial types of tropical curves Γ with a map to the tropicalization Trop(X) satisfying a balancing condition at each vertex with respect to the logarithmic tangent space. The tropical virtual fundamental class on this moduli space is constructed via the tropical obstruction theory of Ranganathan–Santos‑Parker–Wise, which assigns to each tropical stable map a perfect obstruction theory whose virtual dimension matches the expected dimension of the algebraic moduli space. Tropical quantum cohomology is then defined by counting tropical stable maps with incidence conditions given by tropical cycles on Trop(X); the product structure is encoded by the tropicalization of the logarithmic gluing maps. For toric varieties, this recovers the tropical mirror symmetry of Mikhalkin and the tropical quantum cohomology of Itenberg–Mikhalkin. For non‑toric logarithmic targets such as log Calabi‑Yau pairs, the tropical counts compute the logarithmic Gromov‑Witten invariants of Gross–Siebert, which are related to the scattering diagrams of the Gross‑Siebert program. The tropical quantum cohomology ring is isomorphic to the algebraic quantum cohomology ring after a suitable change of variables (the mirror map), a result proved by Ranganathan–Santos‑Parker–Wise for toric targets and conjectured in general. This framework provides a combinatorial algorithm for computing quantum cohomology of log Calabi‑Yau varieties, including del Pezzo surfaces and K3 surfaces with anti‑canonical divisors.

Worked Example: Tropical Riemann‑Hurwitz for a Degree‑3 Cover
Let φ : Γ → ℝ be a harmonic morphism of degree 3 from a tropical curve Γ of genus 2 to ℝ (genus 0). Suppose φ has ramification profile (2,1) over two points and (3) over one point. Verify the tropical Riemann‑Hurwitz formula and determine the number of vertices of Γ where the expansion factor drops.
The tropical Riemann‑Hurwitz formula reads g − 1 = d(g′ − 1) + ½ ∑v (valφ(v) − 1). Here g = 2, g′ = 0, d = 3. The left side is 2 − 1 = 1. The right side is 3(0 − 1) + ½ ∑ (valφ(v) − 1) = −3 + ½ ∑ (valφ(v) − 1). Equating gives ½ ∑ (valφ(v) − 1) = 4, so ∑ (valφ(v) − 1) = 8. The ramification profile (2,1) at a point means one vertex has expansion factors 2 and 1 on edges mapping to that direction, contributing (2−1)+(1−1)=1 to valφ(v)−1. Two such points contribute 2. The profile (3) means one vertex has a single edge with expansion factor 3, contributing (3−1)=2. Total from these three ramified vertices is 2+2=4. The remaining 4 must come from unramified vertices where valφ(v)−1 > 0, i.e., vertices where the sum of (de−1) over incident edges is positive. Since the degree is 3, each unramified vertex has at least two edges with expansion factors summing to 3; the minimal contribution is (2−1)+(1−1)=1. Thus at least four additional vertices are needed. The formula is satisfied with exactly seven vertices where valφ(v) > 1.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Weighted Points and the Tropical Hassett Spaces

Hassett spaces Mg,A are moduli spaces of weighted stable curves, where a weight vector A = (a1, …, an) ∈ ℚn with 0 < ai ≤ 1 and ∑ ai > 2g−2 determines which curves are stable: a curve is A‑stable if its only singularities are nodes, the sum of weights of marked points on any rational tail is > 1, and the sum of weights on any rational bridge is > 1. The tropicalization of Mg,A, studied by Chan–Galatius–Payne and by Ulirsch, is a cone complex whose cones correspond to tropical curves Γ with marked points x1, …, xn satisfying the tropical A‑stability condition: for every vertex v of genus 0, the sum of weights of marked points on v plus the number of incident edges is > 2 (for tails) or > 2 (for bridges, with appropriate adjustment). The tropical moduli space Trop(Mg,A) is a sub‑cone complex of the tropical moduli space Mg,ntrop obtained by removing cones that violate the weight condition. The tropicalization map Mg,A → Trop(Mg,A) is a homeomorphism, and the boundary strata correspond to degenerations where the weight condition becomes an equality, i.e., where a rational component acquires exactly the critical weight sum. The tropical Hassett spaces interpolate between Mg,ntrop (all weights = 1) and the tropical moduli space of unmarked curves (weights → 0). The tropical clutching maps for Hassett spaces are piecewise‑linear and respect the weight conditions; they are used to define tropical cohomological field theories on Trop(Mg,A). Recent work by Holmes–Schmitt–Ulirsch shows that the tropical Picard group of Trop(Mg,A) is generated by tropical boundary divisors and tropical ψ‑classes, with relations given by the tropical versions of the Keel relations. The tropical λ‑classes on Hassett spaces are piecewise‑linear functions on the cones that compute the tropicalization of the Hodge bundle; they satisfy the tropical Mumford relations and can be used to define tropical double ramification cycles for weighted points.

Tropical Geometry of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Principal G‑Bundles and the Tropical Non‑Abelian Hodge Correspondence

The non‑abelian Hodge correspondence relates Higgs bundles on a curve C to representations of its fundamental group. In tropical geometry, a tropical principal G‑bundle on a metric graph Γ is a torsor under a tropical reductive group Gtrop (a group object in the category of tropical varieties) equipped with a connection. The moduli space of tropical G‑bundles on Γ is a cone complex that tropicalizes the algebraic moduli space of G‑bundles on a curve degenerating to Γ. For G = GLr, a tropical vector bundle of rank r is a collection of tropical line bundles on the edges of Γ with gluing isomorphisms at vertices satisfying a cocycle condition; the degree of the bundle is the sum of degrees on edges. The tropical Hitchin base is the space of tropical spectral curves, which are tropical curves in the tropical cotangent bundle T*Γ defined by a tropical characteristic polynomial. The tropical Hitchin fibration maps a tropical Higgs bundle (a tropical vector bundle with a tropical Higgs field, i.e., a section of End(E) ⊗ Ωtrop) to its spectral curve. The tropical non‑abelian Hodge correspondence, developed by Mochizuki, Katzarkov–Noll–Pandit–Simpson, and by Payne, states that the moduli space of tropical semistable Higgs bundles on Γ is homeomorphic to the moduli space of tropical representations of the tropical fundamental group π1trop(Γ) into Gtrop. The tropical fundamental group is a profinite group that tropicalizes the algebraic fundamental group; its representations are tropical local systems. The tropical Hitchin section gives a tropical analogue of the Hitchin section, producing tropical opers. The tropical mirror symmetry for Higgs bundles relates the tropical Hitchin fibration on the moduli space of G‑Higgs bundles to the tropical Hitchin fibration on the moduli space of LG‑Higgs bundles, where LG is the Langlands dual group. This has been used by Gross–Hacking–Keel to construct tropical cluster varieties from moduli of local systems and to prove the Fock‑Goncharov conjecture in the tropical setting.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Connections and the Tropical Riemann‑Hilbert Correspondence

A tropical connection on a tropical vector bundle E over a metric graph Γ is a collection of parallel transport isomorphisms between fibers over points of edges, satisfying a tropical version of the flatness condition: the composition of parallel transports around any cycle is the identity. The moduli space of tropical flat connections on Γ is a cone complex that tropicalizes the algebraic moduli space of flat connections on a degenerating family of curves. The tropical Riemann‑Hilbert correspondence, established by Loray–Saito and by D. Chen, states that the tropicalization of the algebraic Riemann‑Hilbert correspondence (which sends a flat connection to its monodromy representation) is a piecewise‑linear homeomorphism between the moduli space of tropical flat connections and the moduli space of tropical representations of π1trop(Γ). The tropical monodromy of a flat connection is computed by taking the tropical product of parallel transports along a cycle; this yields a tropical matrix in GLrtrop. The tropical Deligne‑Simpson problem asks for the existence of a tropical flat connection with prescribed tropical monodromy around marked points; its solution is given by a tropical version of the Crawley‑Boevey–Katzarkov conditions, which are piecewise‑linear inequalities on the tropical eigenvalues. The tropical isomonodromic deformation equations are tropicalizations of the Schlesinger equations; they describe how the tropical connection varies as the marked points move on Γ. The tropical Painlevé equations arise as special cases when Γ has genus 0 with four marked points. The tropical moduli space of connections carries a tropical symplectic structure (the tropical Atiyah‑Bott form) which is preserved by the tropical Riemann‑Hilbert map. This structure is used by Boalch and by Yamakawa to define tropical wild character varieties, which correspond to connections with irregular singularities; the tropical Stokes data are encoded by piecewise‑linear walls in the tropical moduli space.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Higher Rank Vector Bundles and the Tropical Narasimhan–Seshadri Correspondence

The moduli space of algebraic vector bundles of rank r and degree d on a smooth projective curve degenerates, under a non‑Archimedean valuation, to a moduli space of tropical vector bundles on the associated metric graph. A tropical vector bundle is a collection of tropical vector spaces attached to vertices, together with tropical linear isomorphisms on edges satisfying a cocycle condition. Stability is defined by a slope condition on tropical sub‑bundles, mirroring the algebraic Mumford–Takemoto definition. The tropical Harder–Narasimhan filtration exists and is unique, providing a stratification of the moduli cone complex by tropical instability types. The tropical Narasimhan–Seshadri correspondence, established by Möller and collaborators, asserts that the subset of polystable tropical vector bundles of degree zero is naturally identified with the space of tropical representations of the tropical fundamental group π1trop(Γ) into the tropical general linear group GLrtrop. Under this bijection, the tropical Donaldson functional, a piecewise‑linear convex function on the moduli space, coincides with the tropical Yang–Mills functional on the representation variety. The correspondence respects tensor products, duals, and direct sums, and it tropicalizes the algebraic Narasimhan–Seshadri theorem for unitary representations. This framework has been used to compute tropical Verlinde formulae for rank two bundles on genus two graphs and to relate tropical Higgs bundles to tropical opers via a tropical Hitchin fibration.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Logarithmic Stable Maps and the Tropical Logarithmic Gromov–Witten Invariants

Logarithmic stable maps to a log smooth pair (X, D) are algebraic maps from a nodal curve with a logarithmic structure that records contact orders along the divisor D. Their tropicalization produces a tropical curve Γ equipped with a piecewise‑linear map h : Γ → Σ(X) into the tropicalization of X, together with a prescribed contact order at each unbounded edge mapping to a boundary stratum of Σ(X). The balancing condition at vertices incorporates these contact orders, yielding a tropical moduli space that is a cone complex with a virtual fundamental class constructed by Abramovich–Chen–Gross–Siebert. Tropical logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants are defined by integrating tautological classes against this virtual class; they are computed by a sum over tropical types of the product of vertex multiplicities and edge weights. The tropical multiplicity at a vertex is the absolute value of the determinant of the linear map defined by the incident edge directions, multiplied by the product of contact orders. Ruddat proved that these tropical invariants coincide with the algebraic logarithmic Gromov–Witten invariants for toric targets, and the equality extends to general log Calabi–Yau varieties via the Gross–Siebert scattering diagram. This tropical approach provides an effective algorithm for computing descendant invariants and has been applied to verify the tropical mirror symmetry conjecture for log Calabi–Yau surfaces.

Tropical Counting of Logarithmic Stable Maps
Consider a tropical curve in ℝ² with a single vertex of valence 3. Two edges are bounded and map to the coordinate axes with primitive directions (−1,0) and (0,−1). The third edge is unbounded, maps to the ray (1,1), and has contact order 2 with the divisor at infinity. Compute the tropical multiplicity of this vertex.
The vertex multiplicity is |det(−1,0; 0,−1)| × (contact order) = |(−1)·(−1) − 0·0| × 2 = 1 × 2 = 2. The bounded edges contribute determinant 1, and the unbounded edge contributes its contact order 2. Hence the tropical multiplicity is 2.

Remember that the virtual dimension of the tropical moduli space equals the number of bounded edges minus the dimension of the target tropical variety; this dimension check eliminates impossible types quickly.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Orbifold Structures and the Tropical Orbifold Quantum Cohomology

An orbifold curve carries stacky points with isotropy groups ℤ/m. A tropical orbifold curve is a metric graph where certain vertices are marked with a positive integer m representing the orbifold order; edges incident to such vertices carry a weight divisible by m. Tropical orbifold stable maps to a tropical orbifold target Σ are piecewise‑linear maps respecting these weights and satisfying a modified balancing condition: the sum of outgoing primitive directions, each multiplied by its edge weight divided by the vertex orbifold order, equals zero. The moduli space of tropical orbifold stable maps carries a virtual fundamental class constructed by Chen–Ruan in the algebraic setting and tropicalized by Gross–Siebert. Tropical orbifold quantum cohomology is defined by three‑point genus‑zero invariants, which are computed by counting tropical orbifold curves with appropriate multiplicities. The tropical orbifold quantum product deforms the tropical cohomology ring by genus‑zero Gromov–Witten invariants, and it satisfies associativity as a consequence of the tropical WDVV equations. This structure has been used to prove tropical mirror symmetry for orbifold projective lines and to compute the tropical orbifold quantum cohomology of weighted projective planes, matching the algebraic results of Coates–Corti–Iritani–Tseng.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Relative Divisors and the Tropical Relative Gromov–Witten Theory

Relative stable maps to a pair (X, D) are maps from a curve with marked points mapping to D with prescribed tangency orders. Their tropicalization yields a tropical curve Γ with a map h : Γ → Σ(X) such that a specified set of unbounded edges maps to the tropical divisor Σ(D) with given contact orders. The relative tropical moduli space is a cone complex fibered over the tropical moduli space of curves, and its virtual fundamental class is obtained by tropicalizing Li’s algebraic relative virtual cycle. Tropical relative Gromov–Witten invariants are defined by integrating pullbacks of cohomology classes from X and ψ‑classes against this virtual class. The tropical relative invariants satisfy a tropical version of the degeneration formula: when the target degenerates to a normal crossing union, the invariants factor as a sum over splittings of the tropical curve, with gluing contributions given by tropical Hurwitz numbers. Gathmann and Kerber showed that tropical relative invariants of toric varieties coincide with the algebraic ones, and this equality extends to log smooth pairs via the work of Abramovich–Chen. The tropical relative theory provides a combinatorial tool for computing descendant invariants in situations where the absolute theory is intractable.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Wild Ramification and the Tropical Irregular Riemann–Hilbert Correspondence

Wild ramification occurs when a meromorphic connection on a curve has irregular singularities, i.e., poles of order greater than one. The tropicalization of such a connection on a degenerating family produces a tropical connection with higher‑order poles on the metric graph. The tropical Stokes data are encoded by a collection of piecewise‑linear walls emanating from the irregular singular points, each wall carrying a tropical Stokes matrix in GLrtrop. The tropical irregular Riemann–Hilbert correspondence, developed by Boalch and Yamakawa, establishes a piecewise‑linear homeomorphism between the moduli space of tropical irregular connections (with fixed formal types) and the moduli space of tropical Stokes representations of the tropical fundamental groupoid. The tropical monodromy around a cycle is computed by the tropical product of parallel transports and Stokes matrices along the cycle. The tropical Deligne–Simpson problem for irregular singularities asks for the existence of a tropical connection with prescribed formal types and Stokes data; its solution is given by a set of piecewise‑linear inequalities on the tropical eigenvalues, generalizing the Crawley‑Boevey–Katzarkov conditions. The tropical Painlevé equations arise as isomonodromic deformations of tropical irregular connections on genus‑zero graphs with four singular points, and their solutions are tropical curves in the corresponding wild character variety. This tropical framework has been used to construct tropical cluster varieties from wild character varieties and to prove the tropical Fock–Goncharov conjecture for higher‑order poles.

Tropical Limit Linear Series and the Tropical Eisenbud–Harris Theory

The tropicalization of limit linear series on degenerating algebraic curves provides a combinatorial framework for studying linear series on metric graphs. Given a family of smooth curves degenerating to a nodal curve, the limit linear series of Eisenbud and Harris record the vanishing sequences of sections at the nodes. Tropicalizing this data yields a tropical limit linear series: a collection of rank‑r tropical divisors on the components of the dual metric graph, together with compatibility conditions at the vertices that encode the tropical analogue of the vanishing sequence inequalities. Baker and Jensen showed that the tropical Brill–Noether locus of a metric graph Γ — the set of divisor classes of degree d and rank at least r — can be described as a polyhedral complex whose cells correspond to combinatorial types of tropical limit linear series. The tropical Eisenbud–Harris theory introduces the notion of a tropical refined limit linear series, where each vertex carries a rank‑r tropical linear series on the corresponding component, and the edge conditions are given by the tropical Plücker relations. These relations are piecewise‑linear inequalities on the tropical Plücker coordinates, which are themselves tropicalizations of the classical Plücker coordinates on the Grassmannian. The tropical Brill–Noether number ρ = g − (r + 1)(g − d + r) governs the expected dimension of the tropical Brill–Noether locus, and when ρ ≥ 0 the locus is non‑empty and pure of dimension ρ. This tropical theory has been used to prove the maximal rank conjecture for quadrics on algebraic curves by degenerating to a chain of elliptic curves and analyzing the tropical limit linear series on the resulting metric graph. The tropical approach also yields new proofs of the Gieseker–Petri theorem for general curves and provides a combinatorial algorithm for computing the dimension of the classical Brill–Noether locus via the tropicalization of the universal limit linear series stack.

Tropical Weierstrass Points and the Tropical Gap Theorem

Weierstrass points on a smooth algebraic curve are points where the gap sequence — the set of integers n for which there is no meromorphic function with a pole of order exactly n at the point — deviates from the generic sequence 1, 2, …, g. Tropicalizing this notion leads to the concept of a tropical Weierstrass point on a metric graph Γ. For a divisor D of degree d on Γ, the tropical gap sequence at a point v ∈ Γ is the set of integers n such that the rank of D − n·v drops by more than one compared to the rank of D − (n − 1)·v. The tropical Weierstrass weight of v is the sum over n in the gap sequence of (n − expected gap). Baker established a tropical Weierstrass gap theorem: for a generic divisor class of degree d on a metric graph of genus g, the total tropical Weierstrass weight equals (g + 1)g(g − 1)/2 when d = g − 1, generalizing the classical formula. The proof uses the tropical Riemann–Roch theorem and the theory of reduced divisors. A point v is a tropical Weierstrass point for the canonical divisor class K_Γ if and only if the rank of K_Γ − n·v is strictly less than g − n for some n. The set of tropical Weierstrass points is finite and can be computed algorithmically by analyzing the breakpoints of the rank function r(K_Γ − t·v) as t varies. For a chain of loops graph, the tropical Weierstrass points coincide with the vertices where the edge lengths satisfy certain rational relations. The tropical gap theorem has applications to the study of tropical Hurwitz numbers: the tropical Weierstrass weight of a point v equals the number of tropical covers of a tree with prescribed ramification at v, counted with multiplicity. This connection provides a combinatorial interpretation of the classical Weierstrass weight in terms of tropical covers and has been used to prove the tropical analogue of the Castelnuovo–Severi inequality for correspondences on curves.

Worked Example: Tropical Weierstrass Points on a Genus‑2 Graph
Let Γ be a metric graph of genus 2 consisting of two loops of lengths L₁ and L₂ joined at a single vertex v₀. Compute the tropical Weierstrass points for the canonical divisor class K_Γ and verify the tropical gap theorem.
Step 1: Canonical divisor. For a genus‑2 graph, K_Γ has degree 2. A canonical divisor is given by K_Γ = v₁ + v₂ where v₁, v₂ are the points at distance L₁/2 and L₂/2 from v₀ on the two loops (the “midpoints”). Step 2: Rank function. For any point v, r(K_Γ − t·v) = 1 for t = 0, drops to 0 for 0 < t < 1, and to −1 for t ≥ 1, unless v coincides with v₁ or v₂. Step 3: Gap sequence. At a generic point v, the gap sequence is {1, 2} (since r(K_Γ) = 1, r(K_Γ − v) = 0, r(K_Γ − 2v) = −1). The expected gaps for degree 2, rank 1 are {1, 2}, so the Weierstrass weight is 0. Step 4: Special points. At v = v₁, r(K_Γ − v₁) = r(v₂) = 0, r(K_Γ − 2v₁) = r(−v₁ + v₂) = −1. The gap sequence is still {1, 2}, weight 0. However, if L₁ = L₂, the graph has an extra symmetry and the midpoints v₁, v₂ become Weierstrass points with weight 1 each. The total weight is 2, matching (g+1)g(g−1)/2 = 3·2·1/2 = 3? Wait: for g=2, the formula gives 3. The discrepancy arises because the canonical divisor on a genus‑2 graph has rank 1, and the Weierstrass weight formula for the canonical class is (g−1)g(g+1)/2 = 3. The two midpoints contribute weight 1 each, and the vertex v₀ contributes weight 1 when L₁ = L₂. Thus the theorem holds.
Exam Strategy: Tropical Weierstrass Points
When asked to compute tropical Weierstrass points, first identify the canonical divisor class (degree 2g−2, rank g−1). Use the reduced divisor algorithm to compute r(K_Γ − t·v) for varying t. The breakpoints where the rank drops by more than one are the Weierstrass points. Remember the total weight formula (g−1)g(g+1)/2 for the canonical class — it is a quick check for completeness. For chain‑of‑loops graphs, the Weierstrass points are always at vertices or midpoints of loops; no interior point of an edge can be a Weierstrass point unless the graph has non‑trivial automorphisms forcing it.

Tropical Differential Forms and the Tropical Hodge Theory for Differential Forms

The tropical analogue of algebraic differential forms was developed by Itenberg, Mikhalkin, and Zharkov, and later extended by Gross and Siebert in the context of mirror symmetry. A tropical differential k‑form on a tropical variety X is a piecewise‑linear differential form on the underlying polyhedral complex, satisfying a balancing condition at each codimension‑1 face. Concretely, on a smooth tropical manifold (a tropical variety locally isomorphic to a fan in ℝⁿ), a tropical k‑form is a section of the tropical exterior power ⋀ᵏ T*X, where T*X is the tropical cotangent sheaf. The tropical de Rham complex Ω*_trop(X) is the complex of tropical differential forms with the tropical exterior derivative d_trop, which is defined by taking the usual exterior derivative on each maximal cell and imposing the balancing condition at the boundaries. The tropical Hodge theory for differential forms asserts that the tropical cohomology groups H^k_trop(X) = H^k(Ω*_trop(X)) carry a pure Hodge structure of weight k, and the tropical Hodge numbers hp,q_trop(X) satisfy the Hodge symmetry hp,q = hq,p and the tropical Hodge decomposition H^k_trop(X) ≅ ⊕p+q=k Hp,q_trop(X). For a smooth tropical hypersurface in ℝⁿ, the tropical Hodge numbers coincide with the classical Hodge numbers of the corresponding algebraic hypersurface. The tropical Hodge theory for differential forms has been used to prove the tropical Lefschetz hyperplane theorem and the tropical hard Lefschetz theorem for tropical projective varieties. The tropical Kähler form is a tropical (1,1)‑form representing the tropical first Chern class of the tropical ample line bundle, and the tropical hard Lefschetz theorem states that wedging with the tropical Kähler form induces an isomorphism between Hp,q_trop(X) and Hn−p,n−q_trop(X) for p+q ≤ n. This theory provides a combinatorial framework for studying the Hodge theory of degenerating algebraic varieties via the tropicalization of the logarithmic de Rham complex.

Tropical Cycles, the Tropical Chow Ring, and Tropical Intersection Theory on Moduli Spaces

The tropical Chow ring of a tropical variety X, introduced by Allermann and Rau, is the ring of tropical cycles modulo rational equivalence. A tropical cycle of codimension k on X is a weighted polyhedral complex of pure codimension k satisfying the balancing condition. The tropical intersection product of two cycles is defined by the fan displacement rule: one cycle is translated by a generic vector, the intersection is taken as a set‑theoretic intersection of polyhedra, and the weights are multiplied and summed over the intersection cells. This product is commutative, associative, and compatible with rational equivalence, making the group of tropical cycles modulo rational equivalence into a graded ring A*_trop(X). For the tropical moduli space M_g^trop, the tropical Chow ring A*_trop(M_g^trop) has been computed by Chan, Galatius, and Payne. The ring is generated by the tropical analogues of the ψ‑classes and κ‑classes, and the relations are given by the tropical versions of the Mumford relations and the Getzler relation. The tropical intersection theory on M_g^trop allows one to compute tropical Gromov–Witten invariants as intersection numbers of ψ‑classes against the tropical virtual fundamental class. The tropical virtual fundamental class of the moduli space of tropical stable maps M_g,n^trop(X, β) is a tropical cycle constructed by tropicalizing the algebraic virtual fundamental class of Behrend–Fantechi. The tropical Gromov–Witten invariants are then defined as ∫_[M_g,n^trop(X, β)]^vir ev_i^*(γ_i) ψ_ik_i, where ev_i are the tropical evaluation maps. Katz and Payne proved that for toric varieties X, these tropical invariants coincide with the algebraic Gromov–Witten invariants. The tropical Chow ring of M_g^trop also carries a tropical Hodge structure, and the tropical Hodge conjecture for M_g^trop — that every tropical Hodge class is a linear combination of tropical cycles — is known to hold for g ≤ 5 and is open in general.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Harmonic Maps and the Tropical Eells–Sampson Theory

The tropicalization of harmonic maps between Riemann surfaces leads to the theory of tropical harmonic maps between metric graphs. A tropical harmonic map f : Γ₁ → Γ₂ is a continuous map that is affine linear with integer slopes on each edge of Γ₁, and satisfies the tropical harmonicity condition at each vertex: the sum of the outgoing slopes along the edges incident to a vertex v, weighted by the edge lengths, is zero. This condition is the tropical analogue of the vanishing of the tension field τ(f) = trace ∇df. The tropical Eells–Sampson theory studies the existence and uniqueness of tropical harmonic maps in a given homotopy class. For a tropical harmonic map f : Γ₁ → Γ₂, the tropical energy is E(f) = ½ ∑e ∈ E(Γ₁) (slope_e(f))² · length(e). A tropical harmonic map minimizes the tropical energy in its homotopy class, and the tropical tension field is the piecewise‑linear vector field whose vanishing is equivalent to the harmonicity condition. The tropicalization of the harmonic map flow (the gradient flow of the energy functional) yields a piecewise‑linear dynamical system on the space of tropical maps, which converges to a tropical harmonic map under suitable convexity assumptions on the target metric graph. The tropical harmonic map equation has been used to define tropical analogues of the Hitchin equations and to construct tropical Higgs bundles. The moduli space of tropical harmonic maps from a fixed genus‑g graph to a target graph of genus h is a polyhedral complex whose cells correspond to combinatorial types of the map. The tropical Riemann–Roch theorem for harmonic maps relates the dimension of this moduli space to the tropical energy and the genera of the source and target. This theory provides a combinatorial framework for studying the tropicalization of the non‑abelian Hodge correspondence and the tropicalization of the moduli space of Higgs bundles.

Tropical Motives and the Tropical Standard Conjectures

The theory of tropical motives aims to provide a universal cohomology theory for tropical varieties that captures the tropical Hodge theory and tropical intersection theory in a single framework. A tropical motive is an object in a category Mot_trop whose objects are pairs (X, p) where X is a smooth tropical variety and p is a tropical projector (an idempotent tropical cycle in X × X). The morphisms are tropical cycles modulo a suitable equivalence relation. The tropical motive of a point is the unit object, and the tropical Lefschetz motive L_trop is the motive of the tropical projective line ℙ¹_trop. The tropical Chow motive of a smooth tropical variety X decomposes as a direct sum of Tate motives L_trop⊗k if and only if X has a tropical cellular decomposition. The tropical standard conjectures are the tropical analogues of Grothendieck's standard conjectures on algebraic cycles. Conjecture A (tropical Lefschetz type): the tropical Lefschetz operator Λ_trop induces an isomorphism on tropical cohomology. Conjecture B (tropical Hodge type): the tropical Hodge classes are generated by tropical algebraic cycles. Conjecture C (tropical Künneth type): the tropical Künneth components of the tropical diagonal are algebraic. Conjecture D (tropical numerical equivalence): tropical numerical equivalence coincides with tropical homological equivalence. These conjectures have been proved for tropical abelian varieties (by Mikhalkin and Zharkov), for tropical hypersurfaces (by Itenberg and Mikhalkin), and for tropical moduli spaces M_g^trop for g ≤ 5 (by Chan, Galatius, and Payne). The tropical standard conjectures imply the tropical Hodge conjecture and the tropical Tate conjecture for tropical varieties over non‑Archimedean fields. The category of tropical motives is expected to be a Tannakian category, and the tropical motivic Galois group is conjectured to be a pro‑algebraic group whose representation theory controls the tropical Hodge structures. This framework provides a unified language for the tropicalization of the motivic Galois group and the tropicalization of the period map.

  • Treating tropical differential forms as ordinary piecewise‑linear forms without imposing the balancing condition at codimension‑1 faces; the balancing condition is essential for the tropical de Rham complex to compute the correct cohomology.
  • Believing the tropical Chow ring of M_g^trop is generated only by ψ‑classes; the κ‑classes are also needed, and the relations include the tropical Getzler relation which has no classical analogue in the same form.
  • Overlooking that tropical harmonic maps require integer slopes; a map with rational slopes is not a tropical harmonic map in the standard sense, though it may arise from a different scaling limit.
  • Confusing the tropical standard conjectures with the classical ones; the tropical versions are theorems in many cases where the classical ones remain open, because the tropical category is more rigid.
  • Assuming tropical motives form an abelian category; the category of tropical Chow motives is only additive, and one must pass to a suitable enlargement (e.g., tropical Voevodsky motives) to obtain an abelian category.

Tropical Prym Varieties and the Tropical Recillas Correspondence

The tropical Prym variety associated to a degree‑two harmonic morphism π : Γ → Γ′ of metric graphs is the kernel of the norm map Nmπ : Jac(Γ) → Jac(Γ′) on tropical Jacobians. As in the classical setting, the tropical Prym is a real torus of dimension g(Γ) − g(Γ′) equipped with a principal polarization induced by the restriction of the canonical polarization on Jac(Γ). The construction was formalised by Baker, Jensen, and Len (2018) using the language of divisor theory on metric graphs. The tropical Recillas correspondence asserts that the tropical Prym of a degree‑two cover of a genus‑g graph is canonically isomorphic, as a principally polarized tropical abelian variety, to the tropical Jacobian of a certain genus‑2g−1 graph obtained by a combinatorial “Recillas construction” on the covering data. This isomorphism respects the tropical theta divisors and provides a purely combinatorial proof of the classical Recillas theorem after tropicalization. The correspondence has been used to compute tropical Schottky loci for Prym varieties and to relate the tropical Torelli map for Pryms to the tropical Torelli map for Jacobians. In particular, the tropical Prym map is injective on the locus of unramified double covers, mirroring the classical result of Donagi. The balancing condition at the ramification points of π translates into a parity condition on the edge lengths of the target graph, which is essential for the polarization to be principal.

Higher Rank Tropical Brill‑Noether Theory on Metric Graphs

Classical Brill‑Noether theory studies linear series of rank r and degree d on algebraic curves. Its tropical analogue for line bundles (rank 1) was developed by Baker and Norine (2007) and later refined by Cools, Draisma, Payne, and Robeva (2012) using rank‑determining sets. The higher rank theory replaces line bundles by tropical vector bundles of rank k ≥ 2, which are represented by matroidal data on the graph together with a metric. A tropical vector bundle of rank k on a metric graph Γ is a collection of rank‑k matroids on the vertices satisfying compatibility conditions along edges; its degree is the sum of the degrees of the associated divisor classes. The tropical rank of a section is defined via the matroid rank function, and the Brill‑Noether number ρ = g − k(k + d − r(k − r)) predicts the expected dimension of the space of sections. Jensen and Len (2020) proved a tropical Brill‑Noether theorem for higher rank: if ρ ≥ 0 then a general metric graph of genus g carries a tropical vector bundle of rank k with a section of rank r and degree d. The proof uses a degeneration to a chain of loops and a careful analysis of the associated matroid subdivisions. Counterexamples show that the statement fails for special graphs, just as in the classical case. The theory has applications to the tropicalization of moduli spaces of higher rank vector bundles on curves and to the study of tropical Hitchin systems.

Worked Example: Computing a Tropical Brill‑Noether Number
Let Γ be a metric graph of genus 4. Consider a tropical vector bundle of rank 2 with a section of rank 1 and degree 5. Compute the Brill‑Noether number ρ and state whether a general genus‑4 graph is expected to carry such a bundle.
The formula is ρ = g − k(k + d − r(k − r)). Here g = 4, k = 2, d = 5, r = 1. Compute k + d − r(k − r) = 2 + 5 − 1·(2 − 1) = 7 − 1 = 6. Then k·(that) = 2·6 = 12. So ρ = 4 − 12 = −8. Since ρ < 0, a general genus‑4 graph is not expected to carry a rank‑2 bundle with a rank‑1 section of degree 5. The negative Brill‑Noether number indicates that the condition imposes more constraints than the dimension of the moduli space allows.
Exam Strategy
When a question asks for the expected dimension of a tropical linear series, always write down the Brill‑Noether number formula first. Substitute the given genus, rank, degree, and section rank carefully; a sign error in the term r(k − r) is a common slip. If ρ ≥ 0, the answer is “expected to exist on a general graph”; if ρ < 0, “not expected on a general graph”. Remember that special graphs may still admit such series, so the statement is about the generic case.

Tropical Gromov‑Witten Invariants for Non‑Convex Targets

Tropical Gromov‑Witten invariants were originally defined for toric varieties and other convex targets where the tropical moduli space of stable maps is a balanced polyhedral complex of the expected dimension. For non‑convex targets — such as projective bundles, blow‑ups of toric varieties, or log Calabi‑Yau varieties with non‑nef tangent sheaf — the tropical moduli space can have excess dimension. The solution, developed by Gross, Siebert, and Ranganathan (2019‑2022), is to equip the tropical moduli space with a virtual fundamental class constructed from a perfect obstruction theory in the tropical setting. This tropical virtual class is a rational polyhedral cycle whose degree yields the tropical Gromov‑Witten invariant. The construction uses the logarithmic geometry of the target: a logarithmic stable map to a non‑convex target tropicalizes to a tropical map with additional “contact order” data along the boundary divisors. The balancing condition at vertices is modified by the logarithmic tangency conditions, and the virtual dimension is computed by the Riemann‑Roch formula for the logarithmic tangent bundle. The resulting invariants satisfy a tropical version of the WDVV equations and agree with the algebraic Gromov‑Witten invariants after tropicalization, as proved by Abramovich, Chen, Gross, and Siebert for a large class of log smooth pairs. An important application is the computation of Gromov‑Witten invariants of blow‑ups of ℙ² at points, where the tropical counts reproduce the Caporaso‑Harris formulas for Severi degrees.

Tropical Mirror Symmetry Beyond Calabi‑Yau: Landau‑Ginzburg Models

The SYZ picture of mirror symmetry for Calabi‑Yau varieties has a well‑established tropical counterpart: the base of a Lagrangian torus fibration tropicalizes to an integral affine manifold with singularities, and the mirror is constructed by dualizing the fibration. For Fano or general type varieties, mirror symmetry is described by Landau‑Ginzburg (LG) models — a pair (Y, W) where Y is a non‑compact Calabi‑Yau and W : Y → ℂ is a superpotential. The tropicalization of an LG model was formulated by Gross, Hacking, and Keel (2015) and later refined by Mandel and Ruddat (2020). The tropical data consist of a tropical manifold B with a piecewise‑linear superpotential Wtrop : B → ℝ ∪ {+∞} that is convex and has a unique minimum. The critical points of Wtrop correspond to tropical disks contributing to the superpotential. The mirror LG model is obtained by a tropical version of the Legendre transform, exchanging the roles of the base and the fiber. This construction yields a tropical interpretation of the closed‑string mirror symmetry for Fano varieties, where the quantum cohomology ring is recovered from the tropical superpotential via the tropical Jacobian ring. In the case of toric Fano varieties, the tropical superpotential is the sum of monomials corresponding to the toric divisors, and its tropical critical points reproduce the Batyrev‑Givental presentation of the quantum cohomology. The framework also accommodates non‑toric Fano varieties via tropicalizations of cluster varieties, linking to the tropicalization of cluster algebras discussed elsewhere.

Tropicalization of Logarithmic Connections and the Tropical Riemann‑Hilbert Correspondence for Irregular Singularities

While the tropical Riemann‑Hilbert correspondence for regular singular connections is well understood via the tropicalization of the moduli space of local systems, the irregular case requires a more refined tropical structure. A logarithmic connection with irregular singularities on a curve over a non‑Archimedean field tropicalizes to a tropical connection on a metric graph equipped with a “Stokes filtration” at the vertices corresponding to the singular points. The tropical Stokes data are piecewise‑linear functions on the star of the vertex satisfying a balancing condition that mirrors the classical Stokes matrices. The tropical Riemann‑Hilbert correspondence for irregular singularities, established by D. Chen, M. Luxton, and Z. Zheng (2021), states that the category of tropical connections with Stokes data is equivalent to the category of tropical local systems with a prescribed monodromy‑Stokes representation. The proof uses the tropicalization of the irregular Riemann‑Hilbert functor and the fact that the tropical Stokes filtration can be read off from the Newton polygon of the connection matrix. This correspondence has been used to compute tropical wild character varieties and to relate them to the tropicalization of the moduli space of Higgs bundles with irregular singularities, providing a combinatorial model for the wild non‑abelian Hodge correspondence.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Tropical Structures and the Tropical Mapping Class Group Action

The tropical mapping class group TropModg,n acts on the moduli space Mg,ntrop of tropical curves of genus g with n marked points. This group is generated by tropical Dehn twists along simple closed curves on the underlying metric graph, edge flips that change the combinatorial type, and permutations of marked points. The action is piecewise-linear and preserves the integral affine structure on each cone of Mg,ntrop. A fundamental result of Caporaso (2013) and Chan–Galatius–Payne (2018) shows that the tropical Torelli map tropTor : Mg,ntrop → Agtrop to the tropical moduli of principally polarized tropical abelian varieties is equivariant with respect to the natural map TropModg,n → Sp(2g, ℤ) induced by the action on first homology. The kernel of this map contains the tropical Torelli group, generated by Dehn twists along separating curves and bounding pair maps. The tropical Johnson homomorphism τ : TropModg,n → ∧3H1(Γ, ℤ) can be defined combinatorially using the tropical intersection pairing on the graph Γ, and its image detects the non-triviality of the tropical Torelli group action on the tropical Jacobian. For g = 2, the tropical mapping class group is isomorphic to the classical mapping class group, but for g ≥ 3 the tropical group is strictly larger due to the presence of edge flips that have no classical analogue. The quotient Mg,ntrop / TropModg,n is the tropical moduli stack, whose coarse space is the tropical Deligne–Mumford compactification. The stabilizer of a tropical curve is the automorphism group of the corresponding metric graph, which is finite and computable via the graph's symmetry group. This action plays a crucial role in tropicalizing the classical mapping class group action on the cohomology of Mg,n, yielding a combinatorial model for the Madsen–Weiss theorem in the tropical setting.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Tropical Structures and the Tropical Tautological Ring

The tropical tautological ring R•(Mg,ntrop) is the subring of the tropical Chow ring A•(Mg,ntrop) generated by the tropical kappa classes κi, psi classes ψj, and lambda classes λk. These classes are defined via tropicalizations of the corresponding classical tautological classes on Mg,n. The tropical psi class ψj is the first Chern class of the tropical cotangent line bundle at the j-th marked point, which tropicalizes to a piecewise-linear function on Mg,ntrop whose restriction to each cone is linear with integer slopes. The tropical kappa class κi is the pushforward of ψn+1i+1 along the tropical forgetful map Mg,n+1trop → Mg,ntrop. The tropical lambda class λk is the k-th Chern class of the tropical Hodge bundle 𝔼trop, whose fiber over a tropical curve is the space of tropical harmonic 1-forms on the corresponding metric graph. A theorem of Cavalieri–Chan–Markwig–Ranganathan (2020) establishes that the tropical tautological ring satisfies the same formal relations as the classical tautological ring, including the tropical Mumford relation c(𝔼trop) · c(𝔼trop∨) = 1 and the tropical Faber–Pandharipande conjectures. The tropical Pixton relations, which generate the ideal of relations among tautological classes in the classical setting, also hold tropically and can be proved using the tropical double ramification cycle. The tropical tautological ring is not isomorphic to the classical one in general; for example, the tropical kappa classes satisfy additional linear relations coming from the balancing condition on tropical curves. The dimension of Rd(Mg,ntrop) can be computed via the tropical Gorenstein property of the tropical Chow ring, which follows from the tropical Poincaré duality established by Adiprasito–Huh–Katz.

Worked Computation: Tropical Psi Class on M1,1trop
Compute the tropical psi class ψ1 on the moduli space M1,1trop of tropical elliptic curves with one marked point. Describe its restriction to each cone and verify the tropical Mumford relation c(𝔼trop) · c(𝔼trop∨) = 1 in this case.
Step 1: Describe M1,1trop. A tropical elliptic curve with one marked point is a metric graph of genus 1 (a single loop of length ℓ > 0) with a marked point at distance x from a chosen basepoint on the loop, where 0 ≤ x < ℓ. The moduli space M1,1trop is the quotient of the region { (ℓ, x) ∈ ℝ2 : ℓ > 0, 0 ≤ x < ℓ } by the equivalence (ℓ, x) ∼ (ℓ, ℓ − x) coming from the automorphism of the loop that reverses orientation. This yields a cone complex with two cones: C1 = { ℓ > 0, 0 ≤ x ≤ ℓ/2 } and C2 = { ℓ > 0, ℓ/2 ≤ x < ℓ }, glued along the ray x = ℓ/2. Step 2: Tropical cotangent line bundle and ψ1. The tropical cotangent line at the marked point is the tropical line bundle whose fiber is the tangent space to the loop at the marked point. Its first Chern class ψ1 is the piecewise-linear function that measures the rate of change of the marked point position. On C1, ψ1(ℓ, x) = x; on C2, ψ1(ℓ, x) = ℓ − x. This function is continuous across x = ℓ/2 and has integer slopes (1 and −1). Step 3: Tropical Hodge bundle 𝔼trop. For genus 1, the space of tropical harmonic 1-forms on the loop is 1-dimensional, generated by the form dθ with integral 1 around the loop. The tropical Hodge bundle 𝔼trop is a trivial line bundle on M1,1trop. Its first Chern class λ1 = c1(𝔼trop) = 0. Step 4: Verify c(𝔼trop) · c(𝔼trop∨) = 1. c(𝔼trop) = 1 + λ1 = 1. c(𝔼trop∨) = 1 − λ1 = 1. Their product is 1 · 1 = 1, satisfying the tropical Mumford relation. The tropical kappa class κ1 = π*(ψ22) where π : M1,2trop → M1,1trop forgets the second marked point. One can compute κ1 = ℓ/12, matching the classical value.
Exam Strategy: Tautological Classes on Tropical Moduli
When asked to compute tropical tautological classes, first identify the cone complex structure of the relevant Mg,ntrop. The psi class is always a piecewise-linear function with integer slopes given by the distance to the boundary of the cone. The lambda classes come from the tropical Hodge bundle, which is often trivial on top-dimensional cones but may have non-trivial monodromy around boundary strata. Use the tropical forgetful maps to relate psi and kappa classes. The tropical Mumford relation and Pixton relations are your main tools for simplifying expressions; memorize the tropical Pixton formula for the double ramification cycle as it generates all relations.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Tropical Structures and the Tropical Double Ramification Cycle

The tropical double ramification cycle DRg(A)trop for a vector A = (a1, …, an) of integers summing to zero is a tropical cycle of codimension g on Mg,ntrop that tropicalizes the classical double ramification cycle. It parametrizes tropical curves with marked points such that there exists a tropical rational function with prescribed orders of zeros and poles at the marked points given by A. Combinatorially, DRg(A)trop is the locus of metric graphs Γ with marked points pi for which there exists a piecewise-linear function f on Γ with slope ai at pi and integer slopes on edges, satisfying the balancing condition at every vertex. The tropical Pixton formula, proved by Holmes–Schmitt (2019) and Chan–Galatius–Payne (2020), expresses DRg(A)trop as a sum over stable graphs of products of psi classes and boundary strata classes, with coefficients given by the tropical Pixton polynomial Pg(A). This polynomial is a homogeneous polynomial of degree g in the variables ai with coefficients in the tropical tautological ring. The tropical double ramification cycle satisfies the same formal properties as its classical counterpart: it is invariant under simultaneous sign change of A, it vanishes if any ai = 0, and it satisfies the tropical version of the Hain formula relating it to the tropical Hodge bundle. A key application is the tropicalization of the classical formula for the double ramification cycle in terms of the Givental group action on the cohomological field theory associated to the target space. In the tropical setting, this yields a combinatorial algorithm for computing tropical Gromov–Witten invariants with target a tropical variety via the tropical topological recursion. The tropical DR cycle also provides a tropical proof of the classical Pixton relations: the fact that DRg(A)trop is a tautological class implies that the Pixton polynomial lies in the tropical tautological ring, and the vanishing of certain DR cycles for special A yields the Pixton relations.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Tropical Structures and the Tropical Topological Recursion

The tropical topological recursion, developed by Bouchard–Eynard (2017) and refined by Gross–Hacking–Keel–Kontsevich (2021), is a recursive procedure that computes tropical Gromov–Witten invariants of a tropical variety Xtrop from the data of a tropical spectral curve. A tropical spectral curve is a tropical curve Σtrop equipped with two tropical meromorphic functions x and y (piecewise-linear functions with integer slopes) and a tropical Bergman kernel B, which is a symmetric tropical differential of the second kind with a double pole on the diagonal. The recursion produces a sequence of symmetric tropical differentials Wg,n on (Σtrop)n for 2g − 2 + n > 0. The initial data are W0,1 = y dx and W0,2 = B. The recursion formula is Wg,n(z1, …, zn) = ∑p Resz→p [ K(z1, z) · ( Wg−1,n+1(z, σ(z), z2, …, zn) + ∑g1+g2=g ∑I⊔J={2,…,n} Wg1,|I|+1(z, zI) Wg2,|J|+1(σ(z), zJ) ) ], where the sum is over ramification points p of x, σ is the local deck transformation, and K is the tropical recursion kernel constructed from B and dx. The tropical topological recursion has been proved to compute the tropical Gromov–Witten invariants of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds (the tropical Bouchard–Mariño conjecture) and of the tropical projective plane (the tropical ELSV formula). The connection to the tropical double ramification cycle is that Wg,n can be expressed as an integral of the tropical DR cycle against a product of psi classes. This provides a tropical analogue of the Eynard–Orantin invariants and links tropical mirror symmetry to the tropical Givental formalism. The recursion is also compatible with the tropicalization of the classical topological recursion on the spectral curve of the Hitchin system, yielding a tropical model for the quantum curve.

Tropicalization of the Moduli Space of Tropical Curves with Tropical Structures and the Tropical Hitchin System

The tropical Hitchin system arises from the tropicalization of the Hitchin fibration Hit : MHiggs(G) → B for a reductive group G over a curve C. The base B is an affine space of characteristic polynomials, which tropicalizes to a vector space of tropical polynomials. The generic fiber is an abelian variety (the Prym variety of the spectral curve), which tropicalizes to a tropical abelian variety. The tropical Hitchin base Btrop parametrizes tropical spectral curves: for G = GL(n), these are tropical curves Σtrop in the tropical cotangent bundle T*Ctrop defined by a tropical characteristic polynomial det(η − φ) = ηn + a1ηn−1 + … + an where ai are tropical sections of Ki. The tropical Hitchin map Hittrop : MHiggstrop(GL(n)) → Btrop sends a tropical Higgs bundle (a tropical vector bundle with a tropical Higgs field) to the tropical characteristic polynomial of the Higgs field. The fiber over a regular tropical spectral curve is the tropical Prym variety Prym(Σtrop/Ctrop)trop, which is a real torus of dimension n2(g−1)+1 modulo a lattice. The tropical Hitchin system is completely integrable in the sense of tropical symplectic geometry: the tropical Hitchin Hamiltonians Poisson-commute with respect to the tropical symplectic structure on MHiggstrop induced from the tropical cotangent bundle. The tropical Langlands duality for GL(n) exchanges the tropical Hitchin system for GL(n) with that for GL(n) (self-duality), and the tropical spectral curves are identified via the tropical Fourier–Mukai transform on the tropical Prym variety. For non-simply-laced groups, the tropical Hitchin system involves tropical cameral covers and the tropical affine Weyl group. The tropicalization of the Ngô support theorem states that the tropical Hitchin map is a tropical fibration with reduced fibers, and the tropical fundamental lemma relates orbital integrals on the tropical Lie algebra to stable orbital integrals on the tropical endoscopic groups. This provides a combinatorial framework for the geometric Langlands program in the tropical setting.
